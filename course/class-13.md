# Session 13 — Family, Class, and the "Culture" Question

**Big question.** When inequality is explained by culture, what is being explained — and what is being
excused?

**Where we are.** Session 12 was about the image. Today it is about the most radioactive explanatory
move in American social science: the claim that *culture* explains Black family outcomes. This is the
session with the longest intellectual pedigree and the worst track record, and the students should
leave angry at the right target — bad reasoning, not the families.

Childhoods* (2003), introduction; Prudence Carter, *Keepin' It Real* (2005), ch. 1 and conclusion.

## Sociological frame

1. **The Moynihan report and its critics.** In 1965 a government report located Black poverty in family
   structure ("the tangle of pathology"). Gutman's archival work showed extended Black kin networks
   surviving slavery and its aftermath, undermining the claim's history. Wilson relocated the argument
   to deindustrialisation and job loss. This is the discipline's best-documented example of structure
   displaced by culture — and of scholarship correcting itself.
2. **Culture is real; culture-as-explanation is a move.** Anthropologically, culture is shared practice
   and meaning. The *move* to watch is when a description becomes an attribution of cause.
3. **Class heterogeneity.** Black America is stratified by income, education, wealth and region.
   Treating it as one culture repeats the error at a different scale (Pattillo, *Black Picket Fences*,
   1999).
4. **Parenting under different conditions** (Lareau, 2003): what looks like a cultural difference in how
   families raise children is organised by class position and its resources.

## Discussion questions

1. Moynihan's report was intended as a call for jobs and civil rights enforcement. How did it become ammunition for the opposite policy? What does that say about how social research travels?
2. Gutman used plantation records. What kind of evidence can overturn a claim about a group's history — and what kinds cannot?
3. If two families with identical values face different job markets and schools, what work is "culture" doing in the explanation?
4. Whitley's father cuts up her credit cards. Is that a family story or a class story?
5. Where in the revival does the show itself risk a culture-deficit reading? Be specific, and say what the text does to resist it.

## Instructor notes

**Handle the 1965 document carefully.** Distribute the passages without the author's name or date first.
Most rooms will produce a mix of recognition and discomfort; the point is to let students feel how
plausible the reasoning sounds before they learn what it was used for. Then teach the history honestly:
Moynihan's own policy recommendations were about jobs, and the report's reception is a case study in
how social science gets weaponised.

**If a student defends the culture explanation.** Do not shame it; discipline it. Ask what evidence would
falsify it, and what mechanism it proposes. A culture explanation that cannot be falsified is not an
explanation — and culture-versus-structure is a false binary in most of the literature anyway.

**If the discussion becomes about students' own families.** Redirect to the same rule as Session 4:
patterns, not testimonies. The specific move that works here: "That's a case. What's the comparison that
would make it evidence?"

**If a student of colour feels the burden of representing a family form.** Make the norm explicit and
structural: no one in this room is a sample. The literature is about distributions, and a distribution
is not a person.

**Say clearly, at the end:** the class has just done the discipline's central self-correction. Sociology
got this wrong in public and then argued its way out of it. When they see a culture-deficit argument in
the wild — a column, a policy brief, a show — they now know what questions to ask.

**Content note.** Discussion of poverty, family structure and racism in scholarship. Some students may
find the 1965 material distressing; announce it before it is distributed.

# Session 8 — Pageants, Colourism, and Hierarchy Inside Black Space

**Big question.** How does stratification work inside a group that is itself stratified against?

## Sociological frame

1. **Colourism** (Margaret Hunter, *Race, Gender, and the Politics of Skin Tone*, 2005): stratification
   *within* racialised groups along skin tone, hair texture and related features — with documented
   consequences for income, marriage, and perceived competence.
2. **The politics of respectability** (Evelyn Brooks Higginbotham, 1993): a politics in which uplift
   requires the group to be seen as respectable, and which therefore polices Black behaviour, dress,
   speech and bodies. A pageant is that politics with a stage and a crown.
3. **Display and the gendered division of reputation.** Men in these episodes acquire status by doing;
   women are placed where they can be *looked at*. The original makes this its subject by having a man
   enter the pageant — the joke is the structural reversal.
4. **Intraracial class.** Whitley's family owns the dormitory named after them; Deborah's family are
   Hillman royalty; other characters work. The campus is not a class-free zone, and the franchise has
   always known it.

## Discussion questions

1. A crown is a judgment. What does it judge — the person, the family, or the institution?
2. Whitley enters to win her mother's approval. Where does a standard come from when the people enforcing it are also its subjects?
3. The 1988 episode has a student protest the pageant *from inside* it. Is that co-optation or subversion?
4. Does respectability politics protect a group or discipline it? Which does this text show?
5. If colourism operates inside Black institutions, whose interests does it serve?

## Instructor notes

**This is the session most likely to get personal.** Colourism and hair are embodied subjects, and
students may have direct experience of them. Set the norm at the top: we analyse the mechanism, nobody
is asked to testify, and no one's appearance is ever the topic. If a student offers their own
experience, thank them and immediately convert it to structure: *what system produced that, and who
benefits from it?*

**If the room wants to declare the pageant simply sexist.** Hold both readings: the event is
undeniably gendered display, and it is also a Black institution's route to recognition and scholarship
money inside a society that provides few others. That tension is the session.

**If a white student says something clumsy.** Do not let the room punish them into silence; get the
claim restated precisely, answer the precise version, and move on. A seminar that cannot survive clumsy
speech cannot teach anything.

**Use the casting as evidence.** Students can examine the on-screen representation of skin tone and hair
in both eras. Keep it descriptive and comparative rather than evaluative; the point is patterned
difference, not individual judgment.

**Midterm release mechanics.** Hand out the prompt at the end of class, in writing, with the due date
on it. Take no questions about the midterm in the last five minutes — the room needs to leave with the
document, not a paraphrase.

**Content note.** Body image, colourism, and gendered evaluation are discussed. Flag it plainly at the
start so nobody is ambushed.

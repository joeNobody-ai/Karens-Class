# Session 12 — Media, Image, and the Politics of Representation

**Big question.** Who controls the image — and what does an image do to the people it depicts?

**Where we are.** The episodic half of the course is finished. For the last four sessions we stop
walking the season and start interrogating it. Today's subject is the medium itself: not what the shows
say about Black life, but what it means that these shows exist, in these forms, made by these people,
for these audiences.

## Sociological frame

1. **Encoding and decoding** (Stuart Hall, 1973): a text carries a preferred reading, but audiences
   negotiate or oppose it. This is the theoretical licence for everything this course has done — reading
   against the grain of a show we also enjoy.
2. **Representation as a struggle, not a mirror** (Gray, 1995): what appears on screen is the outcome of
   institutional fights over money, writers' rooms, network standards and audience research.
3. **Respectability and the image.** Ruthless question for today: when a series is accused of being
   "positive" or "negative", who is the accusation *for*, and what does it assume about white viewers?
4. **The economics of who gets to be seen.** A network spinoff in 1987 and a Netflix revival in 2026 are
   different industrial arrangements: different episode counts, different risk models, different
   audience measurement. Form follows finance.

## Discussion questions

1. Does an image of a Black college change anything off screen? What would the evidence for "yes" look like?
2. Hall says texts have a preferred reading. What is the preferred reading of the revival — and what happens if you refuse it?
3. Riggs documented how television has repeatedly absorbed and neutered Black cultural forms. Where do you see that pattern in either show?
4. A legacy sequel both invokes and revises its original. Which does the 2026 show do more of?
5. Who is the imagined audience? Answer for each show separately, and say how the text tells you.

## Instructor notes

**If the room defends the show against criticism.** That is a fine place to start; require the defence to
use evidence. "The show did X because it needed to reach a new generation" is a testable industrial claim
— check the episode count, the release pattern, the casting and the streaming platform. Nothing deflates
or confirms a suspicion faster than a documented production fact.

**If the room treats representation as simply counting faces.** Push to mechanism: writing staff,
budgets, marketing decisions, time slots, distribution, who reads for the role, who directs. Gray's
point is that images are outcomes. Counting is a starting move, not an argument.

**If a student objects that this is "reading too much into it".** Excellent opportunity: ask what "too
much" would mean, and how much reading is exactly right. Then point out that the *show itself* invites
the reading — the title echoes are deliberate jokes for people who know the original. The text is
already doing close reading; we are just not pretending otherwise.

**If your institution's library has Riggs.** Screen it properly, on a real screen, with the lights down.
The film is a piece of television history and deserves better than a laptop speaker.

**Content note.** *Color Adjustment* contains historical racist imagery, including minstrel-era
material, as its subject. Warn the room in advance: the film uses offensive images to analyse them.

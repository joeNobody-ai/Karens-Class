## Part A (5 points each)

### A1 — "Colourism exists because Black people internalised racism"

| Points | What the answer must contain |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Mechanism identified:** internalisation is a real mechanism — standards of appearance are learned and reproduced in interaction (Hunter; the respectability literature). |
| 2 | **Mechanism missed:** colourism is also produced *externally and structurally* — labour-market returns to skin tone, media casting conventions, marriage-market patterns, beauty-industry markets, and institutions that reward particular presentations. Internalisation alone cannot explain patterned outcomes across a population. |
| 1 | **Evidence that would move it forward:** something measurable — e.g. skin-tone wage differentials controlling for education; casting and advertising content analyses over time; experimental studies of evaluation. Answers must specify *what* to measure and *why it would discriminate* between the two explanations. |

**Marker's note:** the best answers will say that internalisation and structure are not rivals: a structure
that rewards an appearance teaches the standard that then gets internalised. That is a full-credit insight.

### A2 — Whitley's father cuts up her credit cards (S4E73, 1990-10-11)

| Points | What the answer must contain |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Documented:** a character's father arrives and cuts up her credit cards, forcing her onto a budget; a parallel plot has friends making a pre-med student rest. |
| 2 | **Two readings:** *structural* — family wealth, credit access, and who underwrites a student's consumption; class position shaping what a budget even means. *Culture* — parental authority, expectations about thrift, family norms about money and dependence. |
| 1 | **Discriminating evidence:** income/wealth data on the family's class position; evidence about credit access by race; or an argument that the text itself cannot distinguish the two, in which case the student must say what *would*. |

**Common error:** treating the culture reading as obviously correct because the scene is about a father's
authority. Authority is exercised *through* resources; say which resource.

### A3 — Racialized organizations applied to Hillman's budget crisis

| Points | What the answer must contain |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Definition:** organizations can distribute resources and legitimacy along racial lines through their rules, routines and resource environments, without individual animus (Ray, 2019). |
| 2 | **Application:** Hillman's budget exposure follows from funding structures, endowment scale, tuition dependence, and access to philanthropic and state support — institutions doing what their resource environment allows. |
| 1 | **No-animus test met:** the answer shows how the outcome would arise even if every person in the episode were perfectly egalitarian. |

### What a full-credit Part B looks like

- Notice that Exhibit A's club scene is a **status drama** (the fake ID, the bar, the performance, the
  professor's arrival — respectability being managed in public), while Exhibit B's apartment is a
  **negotiation over shared space** (Ron as mediator, the underclassmen's stepping challenge as a
  unity project). The natural concepts are dramaturgy/back-stage for A and negotiated order for B, with
  ritual and solidarity available for either.
- State plainly that summary-level evidence cannot tell you how the scenes were shot, scored or
  performed; two student answers may reach different readings for that reason, and the marking must allow
  it.
- The transfer paragraph should do real work. A strong example: connecting Exhibit B's apartment
  negotiation to the revival's callback card on Dwayne and Whitley's marriage strain
  (`…/v2/ep03.html#ep03-dwayne-whitley-marriage`) — the card documents the 2026 version of the same
  relationship, which lets the student compare *how the same couple's conflict is framed 34 years apart*.
  A weak example: connecting Exhibit A's club to the revival's aunties card because both involve going out.

## Part C — Essay (20 points)

| Points | Criterion | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Argument** | A position is taken and sustained; the essay is not a survey |
| 5 | **Engagement with the strongest objection** | For C1, the commodity objection; for C2, the evidence problem; for C3, the risk of substituting entertainment for data. Answers that dodge the objection cap at 10 |
| 4 | **Evidence** | At least four sources, two from the reading, one primary; retrieval dates; episode facts accurate |
| 3 | **Concept use** | Course concepts deployed analytically; no concept salad |
| 2 | **Evidence-tier discipline** | Documented/interpretation/hypothesis visible throughout |
| 1 | **Prose** | Clear, well-organised, no padding |

### Notes per option

**C1 — the constitutive claim.** A full-credit answer distinguishes two versions of the claim: a *weak*
version (Black experience is important and often omitted) and a *strong* version (the categories and
institutions of American society were built through racial ordering, so a case about a Black campus is a
case about the general structure). Then it argues. The commodity objection should be met with the point
that this is true of *all* surviving texts of the period: the archive of what got made is itself shaped by
commercial and state power, and the sociologist's job is to read the conditions of production into the
evidence rather than wish for unpolluted data.

**C2 — the long 1992.** The strong answer separates three kinds of evidence: what the episodes show
(available), what the industry permitted (partly documented via credits, episode counts, network or
platform, release pattern), and what the writers intended (not available without statements). It should
note that a 1992 network sitcom could put an uprising in a season premiere while operating under
standards-and-practices review, and that a 2026 streaming series faces different constraints — global
distribution, binge release, subscription economics — whose effects on content are plausible but not
directly documented. Saying which parts are inference *is* the essay.

**C3 — teaching this course.** The best answers will use the course's own structure as evidence: eleven
episodic sessions build a shared case; four thematic sessions do the theory work; the evidence tiers are a
methods lesson in disguise; and homework 5, 10 and 14 are data-literacy exercises. The most serious
objection — that students may enjoy the show so much that the analysis becomes fan writing with citations
— should be addressed structurally (the tiers, the falsification conditions, the oppositions built into
homework 2, 7, 11, 12 and 13).

## Marking discipline

1. **Mark the evidence handling explicitly.** Write one sentence per answer about tiers. This is the
   course's most transferable skill.
2. **Do not reward confidence.** Reward claims with falsification conditions attached.
3. **Allow interpretive pluralism.** Where the answer commits to a reading and supports it, full marks —
   even if your reading differs, provided the sources and the text are respected.
4. **Watch for the honesty move.** A student who writes "the summary cannot tell me this, so here is what
   I can and cannot establish" has demonstrated LO4 better than one who sounds certain.
5. **Grade the transfer, not the recall.** Part B exists to test whether the tools work on new material.

# Session 6 — Generation, Memory, and "the Aunties"

**Big question.** How is a generation made — and who does the work of holding one together?

**Where we are.** Session 5 put a crisis in the middle of campus. Today the older generation arrives:
the revival's sixth episode puts the students' night out in the same room as the women who used to be
students, and the original introduces the professor who became one of its most durable characters.

## Sociological frame

1. **Othermothering and kin work** (Patricia Hill Collins, 1990; drawing on Black feminist scholarship
   generally): care that is organised through community relationships rather than the nuclear family.
   The "aunties" are not a joke about age; they are an institution with a job — custody of standards.
2. **Collective memory** (Maurice Halbwachs, 1925/1950): groups remember through members, and memory is
   maintained by practice. The returning characters carry the original's events as living reference
   points; ask what happens to a story when its carriers are still in the room.
3. **Age and authority.** In the original, Col. Taylor *is* the authority — the hard professor whose
   standards are the plot. In the revival, authority arrives socially (the auntie at the club) rather
   than officially. What has moved, and what has not?
4. **Generational transmission of advantage and expectation.** What gets handed down: money, status,
   standards, wounds, and the pressure not to waste what someone else fought for.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do "the aunties" arrive as a comic disruption and leave as an authority?
2. What does a college campus do that a family cannot, and vice versa, in the work of raising a young adult?
3. Col. Taylor is feared for his standards. Is fear the same thing as respect in an educational institution?
4. If memory is carried by people rather than archives, what is lost when a show's original cast does not return?
5. Deborah is told who her ideal partner is by a generation with a different marriage market. Whose standard is being applied?

## Instructor notes

**If the room enjoys the aunties as pure comedy.** Take the enjoyment seriously and then ask what the
joke is *for*. Comedy is a licence to enter a room where authority normally would not be welcome. Ask
what the scene would look like with the same characters played straight — that is usually enough to
show students that the humour is doing structural work.

**If a student challenges othermothering as sentimental.** Good. Push it: what does the concept
explain that the nuclear-family frame misses, and what does it risk *hiding* — especially the way care
work falls disproportionately on women? Collins' own work makes this critique.

**If a student in the room is themselves an older student or a caregiver.** Acknowledge the knowledge
in the room without making anyone a case. The best version of this session ends with the class able to
state the difference between *valuing* communal care and *relying on it* because nothing else is
funded.

**Where this session sets up later arguments.** Authority-through-relationship (this session) is what
makes Session 12's representation questions interesting and Session 13's family debate tractable. Say
so, so the room sees the arc.

**Content note.** Social drinking, adult language, and relationship talk. No graphic content.

# Session 2 — Voice, Censorship, and the Student as Political Actor

**Big question.** When a student speaks and an institution answers, what is being negotiated?

**Where we are.** Session 1 gave you the tools: social text, double consciousness, the three tiers of
evidence. Today we use them on the first open conflict in the revival — a student called out publicly
for saying what she thinks — set against the original's most direct campus-politics episode, from
January 1989.

## Sociological frame

1. **Social movements need an opening, not just a grievance.** Movements emerge when a structure of
   opportunity allows it — a shift in who can hear a claim and at what cost (McAdam; Meyer). Compare
   the 1989 dean, who does not punish the sit-in but suspends Dwayne from the station, with whatever
   2026 institution answers Deborah: *who absorbs the cost is the sociological question, not who is right*.
2. **Framing.** A grievance becomes a cause only after it is named (Snow & Benford). "Censorship" and
   "safety" are competing frames for the same event; the show usually lets both stay in the room.
3. **Free expression is not evenly distributed.** Formal rights do not equal effective voice: whose
   speech is heard as speech, and whose is heard as attitude or threat (a research literature students
   will meet again in Session 14).
4. **Institutional power is ordinarily legitimate.** The administrator's room is not a villain's lair:
   it runs on procedure, budgets, donors and review. That ordinary machinery is what student protest
   actually pushes against — and the original insisted on that.

## Discussion questions

1. Deborah is disciplined for an opinion. What is the mechanism of the discipline — a person, a rule, or a budget?
2. In *Radio Free Hillman*, the dean refuses to punish the protesters and suspends Dwayne instead. Is that principled or strategic?
3. "Voice" and "noise" are different judgments about the same sound. What social positions make the difference?
4. What does a student movement need that a class discussion does not: allies, media, money, or time?
5. If we call the 2026 episode a story about free speech, what does that framing leave out?

## Instructor notes

**If the room splits into free-speech absolutists and harm-prevention advocates** — and it will, in the
first ten minutes — do not adjudicate. Convert it: "Both of you are describing a *cost allocation*
question. Who is paying, and who decided?" That is the session's frame, and it moves the argument
somewhere a syllabus can grade.

**If a student wants to talk about a real campus conflict in this university.** Let them, briefly, with
one rule: describe the *mechanism* (who decided, under what authority, at what cost), not the
grievance. Then go back to the text. This keeps the seminar analytical and keeps anyone from being
conscripted as a witness.

**If someone claims the original "went easy" on the administration.** This is a good argument and
worth the time. The 1989 episode shows a dean who protects the institution's position while declining
to punish dissent. Ask what that combination *is* — and whether twenty-first-century campus
administrations behave differently or just speak differently.

**Historical accuracy points you may need.** The original's anti-apartheid and divestment threads
(Session 7) and the 1992 Rodney King premiere (Session 14) are the franchise's other direct political
engagements, and both belong to documented episodes — cite them now so students see the pattern.

**Content note.** Nothing graphic. Discussion of policing may arise; if it does, flag that Session 14
handles it directly so that the room knows it has a scheduled home.

**Do not leave this session without saying:** campus protest in this franchise is not a "special
episode" device; it is a recurring institutional fact. The show treats the college as a place where
power is exercised, not as a refuge from it.

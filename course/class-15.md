# Session 15 — Capstone: The Black Experience Is American Society

**Big question.** If the case is American, what follows for how the discipline tells its story?

**Where we are.** Fourteen sessions on one campus across two eras. Today we close the loop: the course's
claim is not that Black experience is *a* topic in sociology but that it is where American sociology
does its hardest thinking. The capstone memo is the evidence that students can make that case
themselves.

## Sociological frame

1. **Racial formation, revisited.** Race is made and remade in politics, law, culture and everyday
   life. The course has been a 39-year case study of that process, conducted by a text that is itself
   part of the process.
2. **The sociological imagination, revisited.** In Session 1 this was a tool for reading an episode.
   Here it is the course's conclusion: personal troubles and public issues are not two lists of things,
   they are one structure seen from two distances.
3. **Canon and omission.** Ask what a standard sociology syllabus would have had to include in 1987 for
   this show's campus to be unremarkable. The answer is a critique of the discipline's own history.
4. **Public sociology.** Scholarship that only reaches other scholars is a choice, not a virtue. The
   capstone memo is a public-facing piece with an academic spine.

## Discussion questions

1. Which single concept from the course would you keep if you could keep only one? Defend it against two classmates.
2. The revival is a legacy sequel to a show that ran when its audience's parents were students. What does that temporal structure do to the ideas the show inherits?
3. The final season of the original was a handover. What did it hand over, and to whom?
4. If a text can be scholarship's object *and* its accomplice, how should a sociologist teach it?
5. What is the strongest argument that this course should not exist — and what is your answer to it?

## Instructor notes

**The last question in the list is the one to protect.** Give it real time. The strongest version of the
objection is that a television show cannot bear this weight, that the course risks being entertainment
criticism with citations, and that the time is better spent on data and method. Then let the room answer
with the term's evidence. If the room can answer it, the course worked; if it cannot, that is your next
revision.

**On the capstone presentations.** Hold the 90-second limit. The value is in compression: a claim that
cannot survive 90 seconds is not yet a claim. Take notes — these are the students' own accounts of what
they learned, and they are better course evaluations than any form.

**On the final.** Whatever format you choose (see `../exams/final.md`), keep one applied question that
puts a new, unseen pair of scenes in front of them with the tools they have. The point of the final is
transfer, not recall.

**On ending well.** Say plainly what the course argued, including where you are unsure. A senior seminar
deserves a teacher's actual view, not a summary. If you have been changed by a session — Session 5 gets
people — say so; it models the intellectual honesty the whole term has been demanding of them.

**If the room wants to keep going.** Offer the obvious continuation: the revival has been renewed for a
second season, which means a live case. A reading group, an independent study, or a conference paper by
two students are all better uses of this momentum than another week of seminar.

**If a student's claim collapses in front of the room.** Do not rescue it. Ask the room what the claim
would need in order to survive, and let the student rebuild it aloud. The class then sees a claim being
revised rather than defended — which is the whole term's lesson, and it is worth saying that your own
argument is subject to the same treatment if they push it.

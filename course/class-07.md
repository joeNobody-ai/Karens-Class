# Session 7 — Diaspora: Who Belongs to the "Black Experience"?

**Big question.** What is at stake when a category that looks unified is contested from inside?

**Where we are.** Six sessions in, "Black" has been the common term. Today the text cracks it open: a
2026 student finds himself in an argument about African and diaspora identity, and the original answers
from 1990 with a dilemma about anti-apartheid principle and one student's scholarship.

## Sociological frame

1. **Diaspora is not a synonym for shared descent.** It names a *relationship* to a place of origin that
   is mediated by history, distance and power. Two people racialised the same way in the United States
   can hold incompatible accounts of what "Africa" means to them — that is the argument, and it is not
   a misunderstanding to be resolved.
2. **Racial formation** (Michael Omi and Howard Winant, 1986/2014): race is a social process in which
   racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed and destroyed. The revival's argument is
   *formation happening on camera*.
3. **The global colour line** (W. E. B. Du Bois, 1903 and again in 1915/1920s writing): the colour line
   is international, and anti-colonial and anti-racist struggles are connected. The original's
   anti-apartheid plot takes that seriously as campus politics.
4. **Divestment as a social movement tactic** (the 1980s US anti-apartheid campaign): a demand aimed at
   institutions — universities, pension funds, cities — to withdraw money. It is unusually good for
   teaching trade-offs, because the cost lands on named people.

## Discussion questions

1. "Black" is doing two jobs in this episode: a description of a shared condition and a claim about a
   shared culture. Which job does the plot need?
2. The 1990 episode makes a Black student's scholarship the price of an anti-apartheid demand. Is that a dilemma or a trap — and who set it?
3. What does an institution's investment portfolio say that its mission statement does not?
4. If diaspora is a relationship rather than a fact, what makes some accounts of it powerful and others marginal?
5. Which is more sociologically useful here: the students' disagreement, or the institution's response to it?

## Instructor notes

**If the room mishears this as an African-American-versus-African argument.** Correct the frame early:
the session is not about which group is right, it is about what *work* the category "Black" is being
asked to do in an American institution, and who is written out when it is defined tightly.

**If a student asserts a strong claim about a whole continent or a whole diaspora.** Ask for the
mechanism and the source. Then take the sharpest version of their claim and see whether the *episode*
supports it. Almost always the text is more careful than the room — that is the lesson.

**If the conversation becomes contemporary international politics.** Let it run, then anchor: this
course follows the franchise, and the franchise's version of this question comes from 1990's anti-
apartheid movement. What does it mean that a network sitcom in 1990 assumed its audience would follow a
divestment debate?

**Historical care.** Do not date the anti-apartheid movement as "over". Its US campus phase has a
precise context (the divestment campaigns of the 1980s, and the 1986 sanctions legislation), and the
original's episode sits inside that period. If you put dates on the board, keep them minimal and
accurate; the point is that the show's writers treated their audience as politically literate.

**Content note.** Discussion of apartheid and state violence against Black South Africans. No graphic
depiction on screen. Flag it and move on.

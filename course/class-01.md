# Session 1 — What *A Different World* Is: Hillman as a Sociological Object

**Big question.** What kind of social object is a television series, and what does it take to read one
sociologically?

**Where we start.** Nowhere. This is the first session: the campus, the two shows, the terms, and the
habit the whole course runs on — comparing a 2026 text with its 1987 counterpart and asking what
changed.

## Sociological frame

Four ideas do the work today. Introduce them as tools the students will use for fifteen weeks, not as
definitions to memorise.

1. **The sociological imagination** (C. Wright Mills, 1959): the capacity to see "personal troubles"
   as "public issues". The revival gives us a first-year with a famous family; sociology asks what
   structural arrangement turned a family into a reputation she has to carry.
2. **Double consciousness** (W. E. B. Du Bois, 1903): the sense of always looking at oneself through
   the eyes of others. Deborah's whole season is an argument with a standard she did not set.
3. **The sitcom as social text** (Herman Gray, *Watching Race*, 1995; Robin R. Means Coleman, 1998):
   television is not evidence *of* a community; it is a site where a community's image is contested.
4. **Structure and agency**: the course's recurring tension. Deborah is written as an agent inside a
   structure — the plot never lets you choose one reading over the other for long.

## Discussion questions

1. What is "a Black college" in each episode — setting, character, or argument?
2. Deborah is the daughter of the original's central couple. What does the revival gain and lose by making its lead an inheritance rather than an arrival?
3. The revival premiered on the same date as the original, 39 years later. Is that evidence of intent, or just scheduling? What would settle it?
4. If a sitcom is a social text, what does it represent — its audience, its writers, or its moment?
5. What would a sociologist need that these episodes cannot give?

## Instructor notes

**If the room says "it's just a sitcom."** Take it seriously and give it teeth: ask what television
has done to social knowledge historically — who learned what about which places from which shows.
Then the sharp version: if the only fictional HBCU most Americans can name came from a 1987 spinoff,
that is itself a finding about the distribution of public knowledge, not a complaint about taste.

**If the room goes to the Cosby problem.** It will, in the first hour, and it should. The original
cannot be taught as a simple object, because one of its originators is a convicted sexual offender and
the family sitcom that made it possible is implicated. Do not resolve this; teach it. Useful move:
the show's own history *is* a case study in the gap between an image and the institution that produced
it — which is exactly LO2. Ask: what does a text owe to its author, and what do readers owe to the
people the text claims to represent? Note that the revival was made by a largely new creative team,
with several original cast members returning. Then move on, and say you will return to it in Session
12, when the course takes up image politics directly.

**If someone says "my family was nothing like this."** That is data about portrayal, not a refutation.
Ask what the show would have to show for them to recognise it — and hold the difference between a
sample of one and a claim about a population.

**Content note.** Nothing graphic today. Announce the general policy: you will flag heavy sessions and
in Session 5 the material includes HIV and a death.

**Common misconception to head off.** "The 2026 show is a remake." It is not: it is a continuation
with new leads and returning characters. Netting out exactly what is reused and what is new is
homework 1 — and it is harder than it looks.

**Watch for.** The student who arrives with the whole franchise already known is an asset; give them
the job of correcting the room *with sources*. The reference site is the tool for that.

# Homework 5 — Verify before you argue

**Due:** start of Session 6 · **Length:** 1.5 pages · **Weight:** counts in the homework average

## Task

This is a data-literacy exercise. You will find one number yourself, from a primary source, and then use
it carefully.

1. **Find one current, citable statistic** about Black Americans' health in an area relevant to Sessions
   5 or 7 — HIV, sexual health, maternal health, or access to care. Use a primary source: a national
   public health agency, a national statistical agency, or a peer-reviewed study. Not a news article
   about a study, and not a summary site.
2. **Report it in this exact format:**
   - **Figure:**
   - **Source (agency, report or table name, year):**
   - **URL:**
   - **Date you retrieved it:**
   - **Denominator / comparison group:**
   - **One thing this figure cannot tell us:**
3. **Then use it.** In 250 words, make an argument about the revival's or the original's depiction of a
   health crisis, using your figure. The argument must be honest about what the number does and does not
   support.
4. **One sentence on the 1991 episode.** Why did a Black sitcom's disclosure episode matter more than
   the same information in a public health pamphlet?

## Criteria (out of 10)

| Points | For |
|---|---|
| 3 | The source is primary, current, and correctly described |
| 3 | Denominator and comparison group are stated (the most common failure in public argument) |
| 2 | The 250-word argument does not overclaim what the figure supports |
| 2 | The final sentence explains what television could do that a pamphlet could not |

**If you cannot find a figure you trust:** say so, name what you tried, and explain what the absence
itself might indicate about how this health issue is measured. Do not invent a number. Do not cite a
number you have not opened.

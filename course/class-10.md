# Session 10 — Budgets, Institutions, and Racialized Organizations

**Big question.** What is an institution for — and what happens when the money runs out?

**Where we are.** Nine sessions in, the campus has been a place of identity, ritual, romance, care and
conflict. Today it becomes a *budget*. The revival's ninth episode puts Hillman's survival on the table;
the original's second season had already established that money runs underneath everything, including
the scholarships that keep students enrolled.

## Sociological frame

1. **Racialized organizations** (Victor Ray, "A Theory of Racialized Organizations", 2019): organisations
   can distribute resources and legitimacy along racial lines *without anyone intending to*, because
   their rules, routines and hiring patterns carry history. Hillman is an organisation with a budget,
   a board, and a donor base — and its constraints are not neutral.
2. **Resource dependence** (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978): organisations do what their resource
   environment allows, and are shaped by whoever holds the resources. Put the revival's funding crisis
   next to the original's donor, scholarship and job plots and the structure is identical.
3. **HBCU funding as policy.** Historically Black institutions have been financed through a long,
   documented pattern of unequal state support and reliance on private philanthropy and federal
   programmes. The course should not pretend this is background; it is the plot.
4. **Student debt as stratification.** Debt decides who can stay, who takes the extra shift, who
   graduates and who leaves. Both shows put a working student at the centre of the money story.

## Discussion questions

1. Hillman's crisis is financial. Why is the episode's title about romance?
2. When a college closes, who loses what — students, employees, the surrounding neighbourhood, the alumni, the idea?
3. The 1989 episode treats paid work as a solution. Is that dignity, or a subsidy the institution should have provided?
4. Which decisions in a budget crisis are made by people, and which by rules that no one in the room wrote?
5. Student organising is aimed at administrators. If the resource constraint is external, is the target chosen correctly?

## Instructor notes

**If the room blames the administration.** Useful, but incomplete. Push to the resource environment:
who controls the money the college needs, and what do they get in return? This is the session where
students learn that "the institution" is a set of relationships with a balance sheet, not a villain.

**If someone says this is "just a business plot".** Take it as the opening: the sociological content is
precisely that a college is an organisation, with the same survival pressures as a business and
different legitimacy claims than a business. Ask what a college is *for* if not profit — then test
whether the budget lines match the answer.

**The data exercise in homework 10 is the term's hardest.** Students must find a current, citable
figure about HBCU funding or enrolment and explain what it does and does not measure. Expect confusion
between institution counts, enrolment shares and per-student appropriations; that confusion is the
lesson, and it will come up again in Session 14.

**If a student's own institution is in financial trouble.** Let the structural analysis carry it, and
do not allow the seminar to become a rumour exchange. Refer specifics to public documents, and note
that the course's method — documented claim, source, tier — is exactly what a campus in crisis needs
from its students.

**Content note.** None. This is the most "normal" reading load of the term; use the spare attention to
run the data exercise in class if you have time, with laptops out.

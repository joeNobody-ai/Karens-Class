# Session 4 — Intimacy, Gender, and the Script of Romance

**Big question.** Is romance personal or structural — and what changes when both are true?

**Where we are.** Session 3 showed the community performing itself. Today we go into the smallest unit
the show cares about: two people negotiating. The revival gives us "cuffing season" and a friend group
whose hook-ups collide with exams and money; the original gives us the five-season courtship that the
revival's entire family premise depends on.

## Sociological frame

1. **Doing gender** (Candace West and Don Zimmerman, 1987): gender is not what one *is* but what one
   *does*, continuously, and is held accountable for. The courting scenes in both shows are gender
   being manufactured in public, with an audience.
2. **Mate selection as structure.** Who is available, who is desirable, who marries whom is patterned
   by class, education, geography and race — not merely by feeling. The original's plot works because
   Whitley and Dwayne are *not* an obvious match: she is status-driven, he is a math nerd with no
   polish. Ask whether a 2026 audience would still bet on them.
3. **The marriage market and its vocabulary.** "Cuffing season" names a temporally bounded, mildly
   ironic commitment — a phrase the 1988 characters did not have. Vocabulary is evidence: it tells you
   what a relationship is expected to be *for*.
4. **Black feminist thought on love and work** (Collins, 1990): intimacy is not a private refuge from
   racial and economic pressure; it is where that pressure gets processed. Both shows put money in the
   middle of romance.

## Discussion questions

1. If gender is something you *do*, who is the audience in a college romance — and what happens to the performance when the audience leaves?
2. Whitley runs out of the dance. Is that refusal, or is it the script working correctly?
3. The revival's Episode 4 puts romance next to academic and financial pressure. Is that plot crowding, or the point?
4. "Cuffing season" is a joke about commitment with an expiry date. What social conditions make that joke legible?
5. What is the sociological difference between *I could not find love* and *the structure of my options was narrow*?

## Instructor notes

**If the room wants to debate whether the couples "should" be together.** Let it happen, then convert:
what in the *structure of the campus* made these two the available options? Enrolment, geography, the
major, the dorm, the money. Romance on a small campus is a constrained matching problem — that is the
analytic move, and it is a genuinely cheerful one to teach in week four.

**If a student says the 1988 courtship is "toxic".** Take the claim seriously and test it: identify the
behaviour, name the norm it violates, and ask whether the norm existed in the text's world. The best
outcome is that the room ends up distinguishing *evaluating a relationship* from *analysing a
script* — an ability students will need in every subsequent session.

**If the discussion turns confessional.** Redirect gently but firmly: the seminar analyses texts and
patterns, and no one should feel their own history is the material. The move that works: "That's a
pattern we can name without naming you — what would we need to know to say it's typical?"

**Data point for the board (verify before teaching).** Claims about Black marriage rates, single
parenthood and relationship formation are exactly where sloppy numbers breed deficit narratives. If you
put a figure up, put its source and its year next to it, and note that Session 13 devotes a full
session to the history of that arithmetic. Do not let the room leave believing a statistic it did not
read.

**Content note.** Sexual content is discussed clinically; there is no explicit material. Say so at the
top so students who brace for the wrong thing can relax.

# Session 14 — The State, the Street, and the Ledger

**Big question.** How did a family sitcom put an uprising in its season premiere — and where is that
tradition now?

**Where we are.** Session 13 cleared away the culture explanation. Today we put three structural
systems on the table: policing, wealth, and the state that mediates both. The original opens its final
season in September 1992 at the Rodney King trial and the Los Angeles uprising — and the revival's
ninth episode, in 2026, is about students organising against institutional failure.

## Sociological frame

1. **Policing as social control.** Contemporary sociology treats policing not as an individual
   encounter but as a system of governance with measurable distributional patterns. The 1992 premiere
   put that system at the centre of a comedy's season opening, in a network time slot, and trusted its
   audience with it.
2. **Racial wealth, not racial income.** Wealth is inherited, extracted, and secured by law — through
   redlining, contract sales, expropriation, and unequal credit. Rothstein's point is that the
   *architecture* was legal. Income gaps can narrow while wealth gaps widen; this is the single most
   useful correction students can take from the session.
3. **State violence and legitimation.** The state claims a monopoly on legitimate force. The 1992
   verdict is a case study in the gap between the claim to legitimacy and the perception of it — which
   is what an uprising makes legible.
4. **Movement response.** From the 1992 Los Angeles uprising to contemporary protest, the question for
   sociologists is what movements change, what they cost the people in them, and how they are narrated
   afterwards.

## Discussion questions

1. A network sitcom in 1992 put a police-brutality trial and an uprising in its season premiere. What does that fact tell you about the audience the show assumed it had?
2. Why can income gaps narrow while wealth gaps widen? Give the mechanism, not the slogan.
3. The revival's students organise against a budget crisis. What can a campus movement actually move: money, policy, or public opinion?
4. Who decides when a disturbance is a riot, an uprising, or a protest — and what does each word do?
5. If Rothstein is right that the segregation architecture was legal, what follows for remedies that focus on individual discrimination?

## Instructor notes

**Use facts, not vibes, for the 1992 material.** Have the dates, the verdicts and the timeline accurate.
Students may arrive with strong second-hand narratives of the uprising; the discipline's contribution is
to specify. Where accounts are contested, say so and show the disagreement rather than picking a side
silently.

**If the room wants to relitigate a recent case.** Permit one pass, then move to patterns and sources.
This course is not a forum for adjudicating individual incidents, and the interesting sociology is
systemic. If a student is personally connected to a case, the same rule as every prior session: no one
is a sample, and nothing here requires disclosure.

**If the data exercise exposes honest confusion.** That is the point of the session. Institution counts,
enrolment shares, per-student appropriations, and median household wealth are four different things, and
students who can distinguish them are already more literate than most public argument.

**If someone asks why the revival does not put an uprising on screen.** This is the sharpest question
available and it should be the last one you take. Ask what a streaming show in 2026 can put in a drama
that a network sitcom in 1992 put in a family comedy slot, and what the constraints suggest about
audiences, revenue, and risk. Then note the honest answer: we do not know the writers' reasoning. What
we can document is the difference in what got made.

**Content note.** Police violence, state violence, and the 1992 Los Angeles uprising. Announce this at
the start of the previous session; students may have direct connections to these subjects, including
loss. Do not show footage that is not required by the reading.

# Session 9 — Storm, Confinement, and the Conflict We Avoid

**Big question.** What does forced proximity do to a relationship — and what does a scene reveal about
how people disclose hard things?

**Where we are.** Sessions 7 and 8 put the group under collective pressure (identity, hierarchy). Today
a weather warning removes the group's escape routes, and the original's fifth season does the same thing
to two characters who cannot stand each other.

## Sociological frame

1. **Dramaturgy** (Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959): we perform for
   audiences, and we manage a backstage. A confinement plot removes the backstage — which is why
   sitcoms use it and why sociology should pay attention.
2. **Negotiated order** (Anselm Strauss, 1978): social order is continuously worked out in interaction
   rather than fixed in advance. Watch a relationship being renegotiated in real time, under pressure.
3. **Interaction ritual chains** (Randall Collins, 2004): interactions that generate emotional
   solidarity create the bonds that carry forward. A confrontation that goes well *builds* the
   relationship; this episode is about the difference between a fight that binds and one that breaks.
4. **Disclosure under constraint.** Sessions 5 and 9 both involve telling someone a difficult truth.
   Here the constraint is physical; in Session 5 it was stigma. Ask students to name the difference
   between the two pressures.

## Discussion questions

1. A storm is not sociological. A shelter policy is. What does each episode show about the second thing?
2. Deborah and Shaquille finally discuss their break-up. Why does the show make that happen in a room they cannot leave?
3. Front stage and back stage: which character in the revival has no back stage at all — and what does that cost them?
4. In the original, the people trapped together are the two who most irritate each other. What is the argument for that arrangement?
5. Which disclosure changes a relationship more: information offered freely, or information forced out?

## Instructor notes

**If the room treats the episode as pure comedy.** Sitcom confinement episodes are formally interesting
precisely because they are cheap to produce and psychologically efficient — one room, all the
relationships. Say that out loud: production constraints shape narrative forms, which is media
sociology (Session 12) leaking into Session 9, and that is a genuine insight rather than a trivia point.

**If the discussion turns to the students' own relationship histories.** Redirect to structure and
pattern. The move that works: "Bring me the general claim, not the case. What would have to be true of
everybody's relationship for your story to be typical?"

**Midterm debrief.** Do not grade in public. But do ask the room one question after collecting: *which
prompt made you work the hardest, and why?* Their answer tells you what to reteach before Session 10,
and it tells them that the exam was a course question, not a trap.

**If a student is frustrated that the revival's storm plot feels low-stakes after Session 5's crisis.**
This is a legitimate criticism of the season's pacing and a good thing to have noticed. Ask them to
state the criticism as a structural claim about what a 10-episode streaming season can do that a
22-episode network season could not — and then note that Session 11 will test it on the finale.

**Content note.** Relationship conflict and unkind words between friends; no violence, no graphic
material.

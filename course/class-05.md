# Session 5 — Crisis, Care, and the Sociology of Health

**Big question.** Why did a Black sitcom put an HIV diagnosis on prime time in 1991 — and what does a
crisis mean on television now?

**Where we are.** Sessions 1–4 built a picture of campus life: arrival, conflict, ritual, intimacy.
Today the season's fifth episode interrupts all of it with a serious crisis during finals, and we put
that episode next to the most important hour in this franchise's history.

**This is the heaviest session in the course.** Read the notes below before you teach it.

## Sociological frame

1. **Stigma** (Erving Goffman, 1963): a spoiled identity, managed in interaction. The 1991 episode is
   essentially a study in what happens when a disclosure is made and a room has to decide how to
   respond.
2. **Structural violence** (Paul Farmer, 2004; the term from Johan Galtung, 1969): harm that is built
   into arrangements rather than intended by individuals. The original's episode arrives in a period
   when Black Americans were being told, by neglect and by silence, that HIV was someone else's crisis.
3. **The sitcom as public health intervention.** In 1991, prime time was where a mass audience could be
   reached, and Black audiences were disproportionately affected by an epidemic that mainstream
   coverage largely framed as white and gay. Cathy Cohen's *The Boundaries of Blackness* (1999) is the
   canonical sociological account of how that boundary was drawn and contested.
4. **Care as labour, again.** Who does the caring when someone is ill, and what does that cost? Both
   episodes leave the practical work with friends and family, not institutions.

## Discussion questions

1. Why does disclosure restore a relationship in one text and endanger it in another?
2. In 1991, a sitcom took on an epidemic. What did that make possible, and what did it leave out?
3. If stigma is managed in interaction, who pays the cost of managing it — the person with the diagnosis, or the people around them?
4. The revival places its crisis inside finals week. What does the juxtaposition argue about what matters?
5. What can television do about a health crisis that a public health campaign cannot?

## Instructor notes

**Before you begin: set the room.** Say what is coming, say that stepping out is fine and carries no
penalty, and identify the counsellor or service you would send someone to. Offer a five-minute break
mid-session if the pairing lands hard. Do not ask anyone to share personal experience; if a student
volunteers something serious, acknowledge it, thank them for trusting the room, and move the analysis
to the structural level rather than following the personal story.

**If the room goes quiet after the HIV episode.** That silence is the pedagogical object. Do not fill
it. Ask: *what did the class in the episode do with the information, and what are we doing with it right
now?* The parallel usually unlocks the discussion honestly.

**If a student asserts something false about HIV transmission.** Correct it plainly, without
humiliation, and cite the current public health source rather than your authority. This is the one
session where you should have the CDC's current HIV pages open on your laptop.

**If the discussion slides from sociology into therapy.** Name the difference and be kind about it:
"We can analyse why care work falls on the people who have least of it — that's the sociological
question. Whether a particular person is okay is not something the seminar can carry." Have the
counselling referral ready to hand over after class.

**If someone asks why the revival's crisis is unnamed in the materials.** Do not improvise a
specification. Say exactly this: the published summaries do not name it, this course refuses to assert
content it has not verified, and what we discuss is what is on screen. That is the course's evidence
rule applied to itself, and it is worth saying out loud in Session 5.

**Data point for the board (verify before teaching, and again each term).** Both Sessions 5 and 14
involve numbers about Black health and Black wealth. Any figure you write on the board needs its
source, its year, and its denominator on the same line. Homework 5 exists to make students do that
rather than trust a handout.

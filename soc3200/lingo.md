# Lingo &amp; slang — vocabulary for the course

This course uses the shared vocabulary reference built for the *A Different World* project — the same
tiered resource the upper-division seminar (SOC 4150) uses. This course needs less of it, but the
discipline around it is identical.

## 1. The evidence rule, applied to vocabulary

The reference applies the course's three tiers to *words*:

- **documented** — a term verified against the project's own evidence base (a published summary, an
  episode title). Example: *cuffing season* — the episode is titled after it and the summary names it.
- **era lexicon** — a term that is real vocabulary of the period, sourced from dictionaries and
  scholarship (Green's, the OED, Smitherman, Major, Rickford &amp; Rickford, the LSA) but **not** claimed
  as dialogue from the show. Most slang is in this tier.
- **handle with care** — terms used *about* Black people in the era's media and criticism, analysed in
  the third person and never spoken, defined, or performed by anyone in the room.

The point is not to be cautious; it is to be accurate. A word either is documented in the show's record,
or it is period vocabulary, or it is a term that must be handled as history — and those three are not the
same claim.

## 2. Where it does work

Where a module's concepts touch language — culture, race, gender, media, family, movements — the module
directs students to the reference for the terms it uses, rather than asking them to internalise the
whole reference. Students need the terms only as far as a module uses them.

## 3. The protocol for the instructors

See the instructor guide, norm 0, in full. The short version: the instructor does not perform the
vernacular; no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term; definitions come from
the cited sources; care-tier terms are numbered on slides and discussed by number.

## 4. Why this belongs in a *sociology* course

Language is the most ordinary evidence there is of the social: who can say what to whom, in what register,
with what consequence. The show's two eras are a natural experiment in how a community's words change when
its situation changes — which is sociology, not lexicography.

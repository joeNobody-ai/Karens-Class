# Module 2 — Theories of Class: Marx and Weber

**Big question.** Is class about what you own, or about status and power?

**Where we start.** Last week we saw a ladder; this week we meet the two theorists who argued about what
the ladder is made of. We return to Ep 9 — *Romancing the Unknown* — but now reading its money and status
beats through Marx's relationship to production and Weber's three dimensions of ranking. The throwback
*Take This Job and Love It* gives us the same question in a different register: is a job about wages, or
about honour, or about power?

## Sociological frame

1. **Marx and class** (Marx & Engels, 1848): class is a relationship to the means of production — who
   owns and who must sell their labour — which sets owners and workers in structured opposition.
2. **Weber and status** (Weber, 1922): status is a distinct dimension of ranking based on honour,
   lifestyle, and social estimation, independent of market position.
3. **Class, status, party** (Weber, 1922): three analytically separate axes — economic position, social
   honour, and organised power — that combine unevenly in any concrete order.
4. **Exploitation** (Marx): the appropriation, by the class that controls the means of production, of the
   surplus produced by the labour of the class that does not.

## Discussion questions

1. In the Ep 9 scene, is the budget crisis primarily a *class* conflict (Marx) or a *status* conflict
   (Weber)? Anchor your answer in a specific beat.
2. What would Weber see in the scene that Marx's categories cannot name — and vice versa?
3. Is "exploitation" a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? What would move
   it up or down a tier?
4. In *Take This Job and Love It*, does a job read as pay, as honour, or as power? Does the scene let all
   three be true at once?
5. If you could ask one question of Marx and one of Weber about Hillman's crisis, what would each be?

## Instructor notes

**If students treat Marx and Weber as rivals to be scored.** Reframe: the question is not which theorist
wins but what each lens *reveals and hides*. Ask "what does this lens make visible that the other cannot?"

**If a student collapses "status" into "class" (says it's all just money).** Push back with a concrete
case from the scene where honour and money point in different directions, and ask which axis explains the
ranking.

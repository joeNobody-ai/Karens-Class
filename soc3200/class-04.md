# Module 4 — Status, Culture, and Distinction

**Big question.** How does taste do the work of class without ever naming money?

**Where we start.** So far the ladder has been made of money. This week we meet Bourdieu's claim that it
is also made of taste: that the way we judge what is good, beautiful, and "us" is class doing its work in
disguise. The homecoming episode *Center de Stage* is a stage for exactly this — who gets to headline,
who belongs, what counts as the right kind of performance. The throwback *Whitley's Last Supper* gives us
distinction at a table, where taste and manners mark position as clearly as a price tag would.

## Sociological frame

1. **Bourdieu** (Bourdieu, 1979): the theorist who showed that class operates through culture — through
   taste, judgement, and disposition — as much as through money.
2. **Cultural capital**: the knowledge, tastes, and dispositions that signal belonging and convert into
   advantage in the right settings.
3. **Distinction** (Bourdieu, 1979): the process by which taste marks social position — the aesthetic
   judgement that separates "us" from "them" without ever naming money.
4. **Habitus** (Bourdieu): the embodied set of dispositions, formed by upbringing, that generates the
   tastes and practices fitting one's position.

## Discussion questions

1. In the Ep 3 headliner fight, what is at stake that is *not* money? Name it precisely.
2. How does a judgement of taste ("that's not a real headliner") do the work of ranking without naming
   class?
3. Is habitus a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? What would you need to
   move it?
4. In *Whitley's Last Supper*, which manners read as high-status and which read as low — and to whom?
5. Can taste ever be separated from position, or is every aesthetic judgement a status claim? Argue a
   side.

## Instructor notes

**If students say the fight is "just about taste, not class."** That is the whole claim under test. Ask
them to find the money in the scene anyway, then ask why the scene works so hard to keep it offstage —
which is Bourdieu's point.

**If a student dismisses Bourdieu as "elitist."** Distinguish the *theory* (which describes a mechanism)
from any endorsement of it. Bourdieu is describing how distinction works, not celebrating it.

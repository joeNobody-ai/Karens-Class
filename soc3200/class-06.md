# Module 6 — Education and Reproduction

**Big question.** How does schooling sort people into futures — and pass the sorting on?

**Where we start.** The college that has been our setting all term is now the object of study. A
historically Black college is a ladder *and* a sorting machine: it lifts, and it also sorts who goes
where afterward, and it passes that sorting to the next generation. We return to Ep 9's institutional
crisis and to *Take This Job and Love It* to ask what a degree actually does — teach, or credential, or
both — and whose advantage the institution quietly reproduces.

## Sociological frame

1. **Reproduction**: the process by which institutions pass advantage and disadvantage from one
   generation to the next, making an inherited position look earned.
2. **Credentialing** (Collins, 1979): the use of degrees and certificates as a sorting and rationing
   device rather than — or in addition to — a measure of skill.
3. **Cultural capital** (Bourdieu, 1986): the knowledge and dispositions schools reward, which privileged
   families transmit at home.
4. **Bourdieu and Passeron** (*Reproduction*, 1977): the claim that schooling's hidden work is to
   legitimate inherited inequality as merit, so that the system's winners look self-made.

## Discussion questions

1. In the Ep 9 organising beat, is the institution opening or closing? Anchor your answer.
2. If a degree rations opportunity, what exactly is being rationed — and to whom?
3. Does *Take This Job and Love It* show a degree as skill, as credential, or as both? Where?
4. What does a school reward that a student could not have learned in class? Name one such thing from the
   scene.
5. "Reproduction makes the inherited look earned." Is that a documented fact, an interpretation, or a
   hypothesis — and what would falsify it?

## Instructor notes

**If students insist schooling is a pure meritocracy.** Do not lecture; hand them Collins's question and
one scene, and ask them to find the sorting that is *not* about skill. Let the evidence do the work.

**If the room confuses "reproduction" with "no one moves."** Clarify sharply: individual mobility and
structural reproduction can be true at once. The question is whether the *ladder* changes, not whether
individuals climb it.

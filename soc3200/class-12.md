# Module 12 — Housing, Neighborhood, and Place

**Big question.** How does the address you were born into become a life outcome?

**Where we start.** The ladder is not only carried in a body or a balance sheet; it is drawn on a map.
This week we read Hillman itself as a *place* — not a backdrop but a bundle of resources, networks, and
hazards attached to an address. We return to Ep 1 (*Irreconcilable Differences*), where a freshman
arrives at a campus that will shape her future, and to the throwback *Whitley's Last Supper*, where class
and place meet around a table set in a particular kind of home. The question is how the address you were
born into becomes a life outcome.

## Sociological frame

1. **Residential segregation** (Massey & Denton, 1993): the spatial separation of groups that
   concentrates advantage and disadvantage by address.
2. **Neighborhood effects** (Wilson, 1987): the ways the place you grow up shapes outcomes independent of
   your own family's traits.
3. **Place**: the geographically specific bundle of resources, networks, and hazards attached to an
   address.
4. **Opportunity hoarding** (Tilly, 1998): the use of a group's resources and networks to secure its own
   members' advantage at others' expense.

## Discussion questions

1. What does the Hillman address give Deborah that her own effort cannot? Name the resource and its
   mechanism.
2. How does residential segregation explain *who gets to attend* a place like Hillman in the first place?
3. In *Whitley's Last Supper*, what does the table's address say that the food cannot?
4. Is "opportunity hoarding" a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? Defend
   the tier.
5. Draw the causal chain from "born at address X" to "life outcome Y." Where is each link strongest and
   weakest?

## Instructor notes

**If students treat "place" as scenery.** Interrupt with the module's question verbatim: what *resource*
is attached to this address? Push until someone names a network, a school, a safety margin, or a price of
entry.

**If a student says "it's not the neighborhood, it's the family."** Do not choose sides; note that
neighborhood effects are defined as what the place does *beyond* family traits, and ask what the scene
would have to show to separate the two.

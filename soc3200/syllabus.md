# SOC 3200 — Social Stratification and Inequality

**Upper-level (3000) · 15 modules · 75 minutes · Prerequisite: an introductory sociology course or permission of the instructor**

## 1. Catalogue description

Why does inequality survive every effort to reduce it? This upper-level seminar takes up the question
that sits at the centre of the sociological imagination: how societies sort people into ranked positions,
and how that ranking comes to look natural, earned, and permanent. We read the founding theorists of
stratification — Marx, Weber, and Bourdieu — alongside contemporary work on wealth, race, gender, family,
education, health, housing, and place. We do all of it through one sustained case: the two eras of
*A Different World* (1987–1993 and the 2026 revival), a series set at a historically Black college whose
characters are constantly negotiating money, status, taste, and belonging. The series is not the object
of the course; it is the shared screen on which the course's concepts become visible. Students learn to
move between a scene and a theory, to keep documented fact apart from interpretation, and, by the end, to
build and defend a causal account of one real inequality with evidence.

## 3. How the course works

**One case, fifteen concepts.** The entire course runs on a single case — *A Different World*, in its two
eras — and fifteen modules, each built around one or two core concepts. A concept is not a vocabulary
item to memorise; it is a lens you learn to aim at a scene and at the world.

**Screen scenes, not seasons.** We do not watch or summarise whole seasons. Each module asks you to
screen a small number of specific scenes (the published episode summaries and the reference site tell you
which), and to treat those scenes the way a fieldworker treats an interview: as evidence to be described
carefully, never as a plot to be retold.

**The evidence rule.** Everything in this course keeps three kinds of claim apart — **documented** facts
(an episode title, an air date, a credit, a published summary), **interpretations** (readings of what a
scene or season argues, always labelled as readings), and **hypotheses** (claims on the table for
testing). We never invent dialogue, statistics, or plot beats, and we say plainly when a detail is
documented, when it is our reading, and when it is a guess.

**The reference site.** The course's facts about the series live at the project's reference site,
`https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/`, which carries its own evidence tiers and
sources. "Further reading" deep links in each module point to specific cards there.

## 6. Policies

**Content note.** The material includes discussions of poverty, racial and gender inequality, health
disparity, and other real harms. We treat these as sociological questions, never as entertainment, and we
name them plainly. Anyone may step out or flag a topic at any time without explanation.

**Discussion norms.** No one is ever the spokesperson for a group. Engage the strongest version of a view
you disagree with. Allow silence. Change your mind when the evidence requires it, and say what changed
it. Definitions come from the cited sources — never from another student pressed to "speak for" their
own background (see `lingo.md` and the instructor guide, norm 0).

**AI policy.** Generative tools may be used to help you find sources, check grammar, or brainstorm — but
not to produce the analysis you submit. Every claim in your work must be one you can defend, with a
documented source, in the room. Submitting machine-generated analysis as your own is an academic-integrity
violation.

**Accessibility.** Accommodations are honoured without question. Screenings are captioned; written
materials are available in advance. Tell the instructor what you need and it will be arranged.

**Late work.** Two homework submissions are dropped automatically, which covers ordinary life. Beyond
that, late work is accepted with a stated penalty and a conversation, not a silent zero.

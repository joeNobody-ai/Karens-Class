# Module 7 — Race and Stratification

**Big question.** How does race structure the ladder before anyone climbs it?

**Where we start.** We have treated class and status as if the ladder were neutral, and race as something
that arrives later. This week we reverse the order: race structures the ladder *before* anyone climbs it,
setting who stands where before any individual effort enters. The talent-show episode *Sometimes You Get
the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You* (first half) puts the argument about African and diaspora
identity on stage, and the throwback *A World Alike* returns to the same fault line decades earlier.

## Sociological frame

1. **Racial stratification**: the ranking of groups by racial category, with unequal access to resources,
   power, and life chances.
2. **Racial formation** (Omi & Winant, 1986): the sociohistorical process by which racial categories are
   created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed — not fixed, but made and remade.
3. **Segmented labor markets**: the division of the labour market into distinct sectors with different
   pay, security, and mobility, in which racialized groups are unevenly distributed.

## Discussion questions

1. In the Ep 7 first-half scene, what is being ranked — culture, national origin, or something else?
   Anchor it.
2. Is the African/diaspora argument a *class* conflict, a *status* conflict, or a *racial formation*
   process? Defend one.
3. What does "race is made, not given" change about how you read the scene?
4. How do segmented labor markets explain who ends up where, in ways individual effort cannot?
5. What would you need to observe, across a decade, to show that race structures the ladder *before* the
   climb?

## Instructor notes

**If a student says the scene is "about culture, not stratification."** Ask them to find the ranking in
the culture — who is allowed to claim which identity with what consequence. Culture and stratification
are not rivals here; culture is one of the places stratification lives.

**If the room treats race as a fixed category.** Introduce formation gently: the question is not what race
*is* but how a category is built, used, and changed — and the scene is evidence of that building in
progress.

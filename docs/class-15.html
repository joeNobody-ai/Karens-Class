Session 15 — Capstone: The Black Experience Is American Society

Big question. If the case is American, what follows for how the discipline tells its story?

Where we are. Fourteen sessions on one campus across two eras. Today we close the loop: the course's claim is not that Black experience is a topic in sociology but that it is where American sociology does its hardest thinking. The capstone memo is the evidence that students can make that case themselves.

Before class: viewing and reading

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 10, A Gift & The Curse, revisited 2026-09-24 The finale's argument about what survives — read now as a thesis about institutions
Original S6E141, A Rock, a River, a Lena 1993-07-02 The original's final season as a handover: older characters graduating into adulthood, new underclassmen arriving, the show visibly changing shape in its last year before it ended in 1993

Homework 14 is due at the start of this session. The capstone memo is due at the start of this session and is presented, briefly, in class.

Reading: C. Wright Mills, The Sociological Imagination (1959), closing pages; Michael Omi and Howard Winant, Racial Formation in the United States (3rd ed., 2014), conclusion; and one text of your own choosing that the course has not assigned — students bring a citation into the discussion.

Sociological frame

  1. Racial formation, revisited. Race is made and remade in politics, law, culture and everyday life. The course has been a 39-year case study of that process, conducted by a text that is itself part of the process.
  2. The sociological imagination, revisited. In Session 1 this was a tool for reading an episode. Here it is the course's conclusion: personal troubles and public issues are not two lists of things, they are one structure seen from two distances.
  3. Canon and omission. Ask what a standard sociology syllabus would have had to include in 1987 for this show's campus to be unremarkable. The answer is a critique of the discipline's own history.
  4. Public sociology. Scholarship that only reaches other scholars is a choice, not a virtue. The capstone memo is a public-facing piece with an academic spine.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–15 Capstone presentations, part 1. Five students, 90 seconds each: their claim, their evidence, one thing they could not settle. The room's job: find the unexamined assumption
15–30 Capstone presentations, part 2. Same format, five more
30–45 The synthesis. On one board, the course's terms; on another, the episodes. Draw the lines the class has been drawing all term, out loud, with students supplying the connections
45–58 The discipline question. Present the 1987 syllabus hypothesis and let the room argue. What would change if Black experience were treated as constitutive rather than supplementary?
58–68 Final exam briefing. Format, coverage, and the applied question — and the one thing you want them to remember: evidence tier, source, mechanism. Take questions
68–75 Close. Say the thing you actually think about why this show, this campus, and this discipline. Then hand out the final

Discussion questions

  1. Which single concept from the course would you keep if you could keep only one? Defend it against two classmates.
  2. The revival is a legacy sequel to a show that ran when its audience's parents were students. What does that temporal structure do to the ideas the show inherits?
  3. The final season of the original was a handover. What did it hand over, and to whom?
  4. If a text can be scholarship's object and its accomplice, how should a sociologist teach it?
  5. What is the strongest argument that this course should not exist — and what is your answer to it?

Instructor notes

The last question in the list is the one to protect. Give it real time. The strongest version of the objection is that a television show cannot bear this weight, that the course risks being entertainment criticism with citations, and that the time is better spent on data and method. Then let the room answer with the term's evidence. If the room can answer it, the course worked; if it cannot, that is your next revision.

On the capstone presentations. Hold the 90-second limit. The value is in compression: a claim that cannot survive 90 seconds is not yet a claim. Take notes — these are the students' own accounts of what they learned, and they are better course evaluations than any form.

On the final. Whatever format you choose (see ../exams/final.md), keep one applied question that puts a new, unseen pair of scenes in front of them with the tools they have. The point of the final is transfer, not recall.

On ending well. Say plainly what the course argued, including where you are unsure. A senior seminar deserves a teacher's actual view, not a summary. If you have been changed by a session — Session 5 gets people — say so; it models the intellectual honesty the whole term has been demanding of them.

If the room wants to keep going. Offer the obvious continuation: the revival has been renewed for a second season, which means a live case. A reading group, an independent study, or a conference paper by two students are all better uses of this momentum than another week of seminar.

If a student's claim collapses in front of the room. Do not rescue it. Ask the room what the claim would need in order to survive, and let the student rebuild it aloud. The class then sees a claim being revised rather than defended — which is the whole term's lesson, and it is worth saying that your own argument is subject to the same treatment if they push it.

Session 3 — Homecoming, Ritual, and Collective Identity

Big question. What does ritual do for a community — and what does it hide?

Where we are. Session 2 ended on conflict: a student versus an institution. Today the campus stops fighting and starts performing itself. Homecoming is where a college says out loud what it thinks it is. This is the first session where we treat a practice — stepping — as evidence rather than colour.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 3, Center de Stage 2026-09-24 Homecoming brings the alumni back; Deborah chases a headliner; Dwayne and Whitley's marriage shows serious strain
Original S2E29, A Stepping Stone 1988-12-01 Whitley choreographs Gilbert Hall's step routine, is thrown off the squad by its captain, and is rescued by a meeting with her 92-year-old childhood idol

Homework 2 is due at the start of this session.

Sociological frame

  1. Collective effervescence (Émile Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, 1912): in shared ritual, a group generates a force it then experiences as larger than its members. Homecoming is the semester's version: the alumni, the band, the step show, the same songs.
  2. Ritual makes boundaries visible. Solidarity has an outside. The step routine decides who belongs on the line, and the original's plot turns on exactly that: an insult about "the rhythmless Freddie" gets Whitley removed by the captain. Ritual is inclusion and exclusion in the same act.
  3. Civil religion (Robert Bellah, 1967, transposed): institutions have sacred calendars. Homecoming does for a college what a national holiday does for a state — it consecrates a story about itself.
  4. Ritual as labour, usually someone's. The band, the squad, the alumni office, the aunties. Ask whose unpaid or underpaid work makes the ceremony possible; that question runs into Sessions 6 and 10.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 2 in. Two students read their competing frames aloud and the room rules on which frame best fits the text
10–25 The pairing. Describe the two homecomings without commentary, then list every ritual element in each (procession, performance, alumni, food, dress, reunion)
25–40 Durkheim, plainly: what emotion does the ritual produce, and where does it go afterward? Ask students to identify a moment in either episode where the feeling curdles — the revival gives you one on purpose
40–55 Boundaries. In the original, Whitley is removed for an insult; in the revival, who is inside the circle and who is standing at the edge? Sort the cast
55–68 The shadow over the ritual. The revival runs its alumni reunion over a failing marriage. Why put the institution's most joyful hour next to its most private fracture? Argue from the text
68–75 Assign homework 3. Preview Session 4: intimacy, gender, and the script of romance

Discussion questions

  1. Homecoming produces belonging. What does it require people to forget for a weekend?
  2. The original's step plot punishes a comment, not a crime. What does that tell you about the social rules being enforced?
  3. Alumni returning to campus are both audience and creditors. Which role dominates in Ep 3?
  4. If ritual is someone's labour, whose is it in each version of this story?
  5. The episode puts a marriage in trouble inside a celebration. Is that drama, irony, or an argument?

Instructor notes

If the room calls homecoming "just tradition." Push on function: what would break if it stopped? That converts tradition into an institution, which is what a sociology course needs. Good follow-up: who decides the programme, and who is not consulted.

If a student with HBCU experience wants to correct the shows. Excellent — make it formal. Ask for one documented difference between the televised homecoming and the real practice, then ask what the difference does to the text's authority. This is LO2 and LO4 in one move, and it prevents the room from treating either show as a documentary.

If the conversation becomes nostalgic. Let it run 90 seconds, then convert: "What is nostalgia doing as a social mechanism here?" — the impulse to preserve a version of a place is itself a political act that shapes funding, alumni giving and admissions. Sessions 10 and 11 will need that.

Landmine to name, not dodge. The original uses the step/cheer culture as gendered display: who gets looked at, who gets judged for how they look, who does the judging. Say so now; Session 8 takes it up properly through the pageant.

Content note. Nothing graphic. Brief mention of a failing marriage; the participants are adults on screen. If your own institution's homecoming has a documented racial history students may raise, keep it on the board as an institutional fact rather than a personal grievance.

Craft note for discussion leaders. This is the best session of the term for teaching how to interrupt productively — the step-captain's decision is exactly the kind of moment where someone has to say "that was not okay" in public. Ask students to script the sentence.

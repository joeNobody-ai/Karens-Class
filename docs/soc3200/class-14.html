Module 14 — Global Inequality and Diaspora

Big question. How does the ladder look different once you draw it across borders?

Where we start. Every ladder so far has been drawn within one country. This week we draw it across borders and find a different shape: a world-system in which some places are core and others periphery, and in which migration itself is stratified — open to the rich, gated for the poor. We return to Ep 7's diaspora argument, where the talent show becomes a debate about African and diaspora identity, and to the throwback A World Alike, which took the same question abroad decades earlier.

Sociological frame

  1. Global stratification: the ranking of countries and regions into a world-wide order of wealth and power.
  2. The world system (Wallerstein, 1974): the capitalist world-economy divided into core, periphery, and semi-periphery, whose positions are relational — one region's wealth is another's extraction.
  3. Diaspora: a dispersed population linked across borders by origin, memory, and ongoing connection.
  4. Migration: movement across borders that is itself stratified — differently open to the rich and the poor, the skilled and the unskilled.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question; screen the diaspora-argument beat from Ep 7 and ask what changes when the ladder crosses a border
8–22 Global stratification and the world system. Core, periphery, semi-periphery; draw the system and place the argument inside it
22–36 Diaspora, defined. Dispersal, memory, connection; ask how the scene's identity argument is also a position argument
36–50 Stratified migration. Who crosses a border easily and who cannot; screen the A World Alike beat and read movement as privilege
50–63 The ladder across borders. Synthesise: what does the world-system explain that a national ladder cannot — and what does it obscure?
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 14; preview the capstone (Module 15) and the final

Discussion questions

  1. In the Ep 7 scene, is the diaspora argument about culture, about position, or both? Anchor it.
  2. What does "core and periphery" explain about the argument that "we're all the same people" cannot?
  3. Who gets to cross a border in these scenes, and what does that movement cost each of them?
  4. Is diaspora a bond or a ranking — or a bond that carries a ranking inside it? Defend your answer.
  5. What would a global measure of inequality show that a national one hides — and vice versa?

Instructor notes

If students collapse "global inequality" into "poor countries vs. rich countries." Push to the relational claim: the world-system says the positions are connected, not just different. Ask what the wealth of the core has to do with the periphery.

If the room treats diaspora as purely cultural. Route it back: diaspora is also a position — who moved, who was moved, and who can move back. Ask them to find the position inside the identity.

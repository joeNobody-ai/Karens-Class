Module 4 — Status, Culture, and Distinction

Big question. How does taste do the work of class without ever naming money?

Where we start. So far the ladder has been made of money. This week we meet Bourdieu's claim that it is also made of taste: that the way we judge what is good, beautiful, and "us" is class doing its work in disguise. The homecoming episode Center de Stage is a stage for exactly this — who gets to headline, who belongs, what counts as the right kind of performance. The throwback Whitley's Last Supper gives us distinction at a table, where taste and manners mark position as clearly as a price tag would.

Sociological frame

  1. Bourdieu (Bourdieu, 1979): the theorist who showed that class operates through culture — through taste, judgement, and disposition — as much as through money.
  2. Cultural capital: the knowledge, tastes, and dispositions that signal belonging and convert into advantage in the right settings.
  3. Distinction (Bourdieu, 1979): the process by which taste marks social position — the aesthetic judgement that separates "us" from "them" without ever naming money.
  4. Habitus (Bourdieu): the embodied set of dispositions, formed by upbringing, that generates the tastes and practices fitting one's position.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question: where in a homecoming line-up is class visible, if nobody talks about money? Screen the headliner beat from Ep 3
8–22 Bourdieu's move. Define cultural capital and distinction; ask what the fight over the headliner is really a fight over
22–36 Habitus. Define habitus; ask how the characters' judgements of "good" and "right" come to them before they think about it
36–50 Distinction at the table. Screen the Whitley's Last Supper dinner beat; catalogue the tastes and manners on display and read them as position
50–63 Taste as class without money. Why distinction works best when it never mentions money; test the claim against a scene where money is mentioned
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 4; preview Module 5, which takes distinction inside Black America

Discussion questions

  1. In the Ep 3 headliner fight, what is at stake that is not money? Name it precisely.
  2. How does a judgement of taste ("that's not a real headliner") do the work of ranking without naming class?
  3. Is habitus a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? What would you need to move it?
  4. In Whitley's Last Supper, which manners read as high-status and which read as low — and to whom?
  5. Can taste ever be separated from position, or is every aesthetic judgement a status claim? Argue a side.

Instructor notes

If students say the fight is "just about taste, not class." That is the whole claim under test. Ask them to find the money in the scene anyway, then ask why the scene works so hard to keep it offstage — which is Bourdieu's point.

If a student dismisses Bourdieu as "elitist." Distinguish the theory (which describes a mechanism) from any endorsement of it. Bourdieu is describing how distinction works, not celebrating it.

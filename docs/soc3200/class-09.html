Module 9 — The Family and Intergenerational Mobility

Big question. How much of your place in the ladder was set before you were born?

Where we start. Every theory so far has assumed individuals climbing a ladder. This week we ask how much of the climb was already decided at birth — by family, by inheritance, by the position you were handed. The episode Insomnia and the Daydreamer brings the original cast (the "aunties") back into the present, making the family's class history visible in its children, and the throwback Whitley's Last Supper shows the same family's money at its source. Inheritance here is not just a will; it is everything handed down.

Sociological frame

  1. Intergenerational mobility: the movement of individuals up or down the ladder relative to their parents' position.
  2. Inheritance: the transfer of wealth, position, and advantage across generations — through assets, networks, and dispositions, not only through wills.
  3. Family and class: the family as the primary site where class position is produced, transmitted, and naturalized.
  4. The aunties: the original-cast characters who return in the revival, carrying the family's class history into the present generation.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question: what was already decided before Deborah was born? Screen the aunties-arrival beat from Ep 6
8–22 Intergenerational mobility, defined. Distinguish absolute from relative mobility; ask what the scene lets you observe about either
22–36 Inheritance beyond the will. Catalogue everything handed down that is not money — networks, tastes, dispositions, a name
36–50 Family as the transmission site. Screen the Whitley's Last Supper beat; read the table as a machine for passing position down
50–63 How much was set at birth? Put the two eras side by side; test the strong claim that most of a character's place is inherited against what the scenes can actually show
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 9; collect the midterm (due this module)

Discussion questions

  1. In the Ep 6 scene, what does an "auntie" transmit to the next generation that is not money? Name it.
  2. Is Deborah's position inherited, earned, or both? What in the scene would settle it?
  3. How does Whitley's Last Supper show inheritance happening at a table, without anyone signing a will?
  4. What is the difference between absolute and relative mobility — and why does only one of them tell you whether the ladder itself is fair?
  5. "How much was set before you were born" — is that a question a scene can answer, or only a dataset? What would the data need?

Instructor notes

If students reduce inheritance to "money from parents." Broaden it explicitly: name the non-monetary transfers (networks, dispositions, a surname, a way of speaking) and ask which of them the scene actually shows.

If someone claims "everyone earns what they have." Do not argue; ask what a mobility measurement would look like and what it would find — then let the module's question ("set before you were born") do the reframing.

Key terms

intergenerational mobility · inheritance · family · absolute vs. relative mobility

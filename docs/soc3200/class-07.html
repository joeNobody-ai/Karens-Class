Module 7 — Race and Stratification

Big question. How does race structure the ladder before anyone climbs it?

Where we start. We have treated class and status as if the ladder were neutral, and race as something that arrives later. This week we reverse the order: race structures the ladder before anyone climbs it, setting who stands where before any individual effort enters. The talent-show episode Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You (first half) puts the argument about African and diaspora identity on stage, and the throwback A World Alike returns to the same fault line decades earlier.

Sociological frame

  1. Racial stratification: the ranking of groups by racial category, with unequal access to resources, power, and life chances.
  2. Racial formation (Omi & Winant, 1986): the sociohistorical process by which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed — not fixed, but made and remade.
  3. Segmented labor markets: the division of the labour market into distinct sectors with different pay, security, and mobility, in which racialized groups are unevenly distributed.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question: what is already decided before any character "climbs"? Screen the diaspora-argument beat from Ep 7's first half
8–22 Racial stratification, defined. Distinguish it from prejudice and from class; ask what the scene ranks and how
22–36 Racial formation. Omi and Winant's claim that race is made, not given; read the on-stage argument as a moment of formation, not just disagreement
36–50 Segmented labor markets. How race sorts people into different jobs, pay, and security; apply it to what the talent show and the throwback make visible about who gets which platform
50–63 The ladder before the climb. Synthesise: how much of the ranking is already set? Test the claim against A World Alike
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 7; release the midterm (due before Module 9)

Discussion questions

  1. In the Ep 7 first-half scene, what is being ranked — culture, national origin, or something else? Anchor it.
  2. Is the African/diaspora argument a class conflict, a status conflict, or a racial formation process? Defend one.
  3. What does "race is made, not given" change about how you read the scene?
  4. How do segmented labor markets explain who ends up where, in ways individual effort cannot?
  5. What would you need to observe, across a decade, to show that race structures the ladder before the climb?

Instructor notes

If a student says the scene is "about culture, not stratification." Ask them to find the ranking in the culture — who is allowed to claim which identity with what consequence. Culture and stratification are not rivals here; culture is one of the places stratification lives.

If the room treats race as a fixed category. Introduce formation gently: the question is not what race is but how a category is built, used, and changed — and the scene is evidence of that building in progress.

Key terms

racial stratification · racial formation · diaspora · segmented labor markets

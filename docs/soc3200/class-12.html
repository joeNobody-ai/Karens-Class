Module 12 — Housing, Neighborhood, and Place

Big question. How does the address you were born into become a life outcome?

Where we start. The ladder is not only carried in a body or a balance sheet; it is drawn on a map. This week we read Hillman itself as a place — not a backdrop but a bundle of resources, networks, and hazards attached to an address. We return to Ep 1 (Irreconcilable Differences), where a freshman arrives at a campus that will shape her future, and to the throwback Whitley's Last Supper, where class and place meet around a table set in a particular kind of home. The question is how the address you were born into becomes a life outcome.

Sociological frame

  1. Residential segregation (Massey & Denton, 1993): the spatial separation of groups that concentrates advantage and disadvantage by address.
  2. Neighborhood effects (Wilson, 1987): the ways the place you grow up shapes outcomes independent of your own family's traits.
  3. Place: the geographically specific bundle of resources, networks, and hazards attached to an address.
  4. Opportunity hoarding (Tilly, 1998): the use of a group's resources and networks to secure its own members' advantage at others' expense.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question; screen the arrival beat from Ep 1 and ask what the address carries that the character does not
8–22 Place, defined. Resources, networks, hazards — catalogue what a campus (or a home) bundles together
22–36 Residential segregation and neighborhood effects. How an address shapes outcomes beyond the family; apply to who can arrive at Hillman and who cannot
36–50 Opportunity hoarding. Screen the Whitley's Last Supper beat; ask how a home and a table secure advantage for insiders
50–63 From address to outcome. Trace the causal path — place to networks to outcomes — and mark where the scene lets you see it and where it does not
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 12; preview Module 13 (elites and the institution's rulers)

Discussion questions

  1. What does the Hillman address give Deborah that her own effort cannot? Name the resource and its mechanism.
  2. How does residential segregation explain who gets to attend a place like Hillman in the first place?
  3. In Whitley's Last Supper, what does the table's address say that the food cannot?
  4. Is "opportunity hoarding" a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? Defend the tier.
  5. Draw the causal chain from "born at address X" to "life outcome Y." Where is each link strongest and weakest?

Instructor notes

If students treat "place" as scenery. Interrupt with the module's question verbatim: what resource is attached to this address? Push until someone names a network, a school, a safety margin, or a price of entry.

If a student says "it's not the neighborhood, it's the family." Do not choose sides; note that neighborhood effects are defined as what the place does beyond family traits, and ask what the scene would have to show to separate the two.

Key terms

residential segregation · neighborhood effects · place · opportunity hoarding

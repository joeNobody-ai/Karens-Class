Instructor Guide — SOC 3200 Social Stratification and Inequality

Why this course works (and what it is not)

A stratification course usually has to spend half the term convincing students that class exists at all — that the ladder is real, that merit is a partial story. This course gets that argument for free, because its single case is a show about a historically Black college in which money, status, taste, and belonging are in the air in every scene. The pedagogy runs the other direction: the students already feel the ladder; our job is to give them the theory to see it precisely and the discipline to talk about it honestly.

What this course is not: it is not a media-studies course (we do not theorise television as a form), not a history of Black colleges (we use Hillman as a setting, not as an object), and not a lecture series on "what the show really meant" (there is no hidden meaning to decode). It is a sociology seminar with a screen instead of a textbook. The show is evidence, and evidence is treated with the same care you would give a field site.

Before the term starts

  1. Read the three course reference files — rights.md, lingo.md, rubrics.md — and the vocabulary reference they point to. You cannot teach the hard conversations without them.
  2. Screen the assigned scenes yourself, using the module files and the reference site. Mark, for your own notes, where a scene supports the concept and where it resists it. The resistance is the lesson.
  3. Pre-load the "check before you teach" items. Every empirical figure in the course (the racial wealth gap, the health gradient, mobility rates) is dated and sourced; verify the current number before the module where it appears. The course teaches how to read a figure, not a fixed number.
  4. Set the calendar. The midterm releases after Module 7 and is due before Module 9; the capstone proposals land around Module 10; the final sits in Module 15. Announce all three on day one.
  5. Decide the facilitation roster. Participation is 15% and includes each student leading one module. Assign slots early so a facilitator always knows their week in advance.

The evidence rule (three tiers)

The single most important discipline in the course, stated on day one and enforced every day after:

Tier Definition Example What we may say
Documented A title, air date, credit, or published summary — look-up-able Romancing the Unknown is the ninth 2026 episode; the published summary states Hillman faces a budget crisis and the students organise We cite it and name its source
Interpretation A reading of what a scene or season argues "The budget crisis makes institutional power visible" We say "my reading is…" or "the course argues…"
Hypothesis A claim on the table for testing "The students' organising will shape the college's future" We say "this is a hypothesis"

The rule has one job: keep the three apart so that a student can tell, at a glance, what is a fact they could check, what is a reading someone is offering, and what is a guess. When a student blurs the tiers — asserts a population claim from a sample of one, or presents a reading as documented fact — the response is the automatic rewrite request (see rubrics.md), returned once, ungraded, with the rewrite condition stated.

The hard conversations

This course sits on ground where the conversations are difficult by design: class inside Black America, race and the ladder, gender and pay, the family and inherited advantage. Run them, do not avoid them, and run them by the norms below.

  1. The vocabulary protocol. Two rules, stated on day one and never relaxed: the instructor does not perform the vernacular, and no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term. Definitions come from the cited sources (see lingo.md), the "handle with care" terms are numbered on slides and discussed by number, and nobody in the room is ever the consultant on their own language.
  1. No spokesperson rule. No one speaks for "their" group, and no one is invited to. The material stands on its own evidence; the students stand only on their reasoning.
  2. Definitions from sources. When a term needs defining — bougie, the second shift, precarity — the definition comes from the cited text, read aloud if useful, never from a student improvising.
  3. The strongest version. Disagreement is welcome; misrepresentation is not. A student rebutting a classmate must restate the view they are rebutting well enough that its holder recognises it.
  4. Allow silence. A hard question can sit unanswered. Silence is data; do not fill it, and do not rescue the room.

Running the room on screened scenes

  • Screen small, discuss long. A scene runs two or three minutes; the discussion runs twenty. If a scene is being described more than it is being read, the screening was too long.
  • Anchor every claim. "Where in the scene did you see that?" is the room's most-used question. A reading without an anchor is a hypothesis; say so and move on.
  • Use the reference site as the shared text. When a factual question comes up (who was in the scene, what the summary says), the answer is looked up on the reference site, not adjudicated from memory.
  • Protect the tiers aloud. Model the words yourself: "the published summary states," "my reading is," "this is a hypothesis." The room will copy you.

Adaptation notes

  • Shorter term. If you must cut modules, cut by dropping a revisit module (2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14) rather than a first-pass module, and keep Modules 1, 4, 7, 11, 13, and 15 — they carry the spine of the theory.
  • Larger class. The capstone defence (Module 15) becomes a poster or short panel; the facilitation requirement moves to pairs.
  • No access to the revival. The original-series episodes in each module can carry the concept alone; the 2026 scenes are the richest examples but not the only ones. Note which tiers change when you drop the revival, and re-flag the evidence rule.
  • Students who have not seen the original. Do not assume era knowledge. Each module states what a throwback episode is and why it was chosen; spend ninety seconds on context before screening.

Module 2 — Theories of Class: Marx and Weber

Big question. Is class about what you own, or about status and power?

Where we start. Last week we saw a ladder; this week we meet the two theorists who argued about what the ladder is made of. We return to Ep 9 — Romancing the Unknown — but now reading its money and status beats through Marx's relationship to production and Weber's three dimensions of ranking. The throwback Take This Job and Love It gives us the same question in a different register: is a job about wages, or about honour, or about power?

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 9, Romancing the Unknown (revisit: class and money) 2026 The budget crisis separates those who own the institution from those who work and study in it
Original (1989) S2E41, Take This Job and Love It 1989-03-16 A job-hunt story that lets us ask what a job is for — pay, standing, or power

Sociological frame

  1. Marx and class (Marx & Engels, 1848): class is a relationship to the means of production — who owns and who must sell their labour — which sets owners and workers in structured opposition.
  2. Weber and status (Weber, 1922): status is a distinct dimension of ranking based on honour, lifestyle, and social estimation, independent of market position.
  3. Class, status, party (Weber, 1922): three analytically separate axes — economic position, social honour, and organised power — that combine unevenly in any concrete order.
  4. Exploitation (Marx): the appropriation, by the class that controls the means of production, of the surplus produced by the labour of the class that does not.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Restate the big question; contrast a one-line Marx and a one-line Weber on the whiteboard
8–22 Marx, closely. The Manifesto's account of class and exploitation; apply it to the Ep 9 budget-crisis scene: who owns, who sells labour, where is the surplus
22–36 Weber, closely. Class, status, party as three axes; apply to the same scene and note what Marx's lens misses
36–50 The throwback and the three axes. Screen the Take This Job and Love It beat; map each visible ranking onto class, status, or party
50–63 The confrontation. Where do the two theorists disagree, and can a single scene satisfy both? Introduce the idea of testing theories against a case, not choosing one as "right"
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 2; flag that next week we move from theory to measurement (wealth and income)

Discussion questions

  1. In the Ep 9 scene, is the budget crisis primarily a class conflict (Marx) or a status conflict (Weber)? Anchor your answer in a specific beat.
  2. What would Weber see in the scene that Marx's categories cannot name — and vice versa?
  3. Is "exploitation" a documented fact of the scene, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? What would move it up or down a tier?
  4. In Take This Job and Love It, does a job read as pay, as honour, or as power? Does the scene let all three be true at once?
  5. If you could ask one question of Marx and one of Weber about Hillman's crisis, what would each be?

Instructor notes

If students treat Marx and Weber as rivals to be scored. Reframe: the question is not which theorist wins but what each lens reveals and hides. Ask "what does this lens make visible that the other cannot?"

If a student collapses "status" into "class" (says it's all just money). Push back with a concrete case from the scene where honour and money point in different directions, and ask which axis explains the ranking.

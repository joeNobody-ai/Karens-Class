Module 5 — Class Inside Black America

Big question. What does stratification look like inside a group that is itself stratified against?

Where we start. Everything so far has treated the ladder as one national ranking. This week we zoom into a harder question: Black America is not a single position on that ladder — it is itself stratified. We return to Ep 3's status competition and to Whitley's Last Supper, and we read them for a second, intraracial ladder: the tension between inherited standing and scholarship, between the "bougie" and the working route through a historically Black college. Frazier's account of the Black bourgeoisie is our anchor text for a stratification that runs inside the group.

Sociological frame

  1. The Black middle class: the stratum within Black America defined by occupation, education, and income, whose position is shaped by internal mobility and external racial constraint at once.
  2. Bougie versus scholarship: a vernacular axis that names a real intragroup status tension — between position read as inherited or performed, and a route carried by need-based aid and work.
  3. Intraracial class: stratification that runs inside a racial group, distinct from (but entangled with) the group's position relative to other groups.
  4. Frazier: E. Franklin Frazier's account (Black Bourgeoisie, 1957) of a class torn between aspiration and marginality, performing a status it cannot fully secure.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Open. Pose the question of a ladder inside a group; screen the Dwayne–Whitley tension beat from Ep 3
8–22 Two ladders at once. Diagram the national ladder and an intraracial ladder on the same board; ask how a single character sits on both
22–36 Frazier's Black bourgeoisie. Read the concept and apply it: who performs status, to whom, and with what cost
36–50 Bougie versus scholarship. Screen the Whitley's Last Supper beat; separate the class signal (standing) from its source (inheritance vs. aid)
50–63 The trap of the single ladder. What do we miss when we treat Black America as one position? Name the analytical cost, then the political one
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 5; preview Module 6, where the college itself becomes the sorting machine

Discussion questions

  1. Where in the Ep 3 tension beat do you see the national ladder, and where the intraracial one?
  2. Is "bougie versus scholarship" a documented fact of the show, an interpretation, or a hypothesis? What would settle it?
  3. What does Frazier's Black bourgeoisie explain about a character's performance that "class" alone cannot?
  4. Can a group be privileged within and subordinated without at the same time? Show it in one scene.
  5. What would a researcher have to measure to establish that an intraracial gap exists — and what would they risk missing?

Instructor notes

If the room flinches at discussing class inside Black America. Name the difficulty directly: the conversation is hard because it is real, not because it is forbidden. Hold to the norms — no one is a spokesperson, and definitions come from Frazier and the cited texts, not from any student's life.

If a student reduces the tension to "rich vs. poor." Push for precision: standing is not the same as money, and the bougie/scholarship axis is about source and performance, not just amount. Ask them to re-map the scene with that distinction.

SOC 4150 — A Different World: Black Experience and American Society

Seminar · 15 sessions · 75 minutes · upper division (Renumber to fit your catalogue. Written to be taught by a sociologist who has never used a TV show as a text before — and adapted freely by one who has.)

1. Catalogue description

In 1987 NBC put a fictional historically Black college on prime time and got six seasons and 144 episodes out of it. In 2026 Netflix put the same campus back on screen, 39 years to the day after the original premiere, with the original's most famous couple as the parents of the new lead. This seminar uses that 39-year conversation between two television series as a sustained case for sociological analysis. Each session pairs a 2026 episode with the 1987–1993 episode(s) it calls back to and asks a sociological question across the pair: how is a racialized self made? Who gets to speak on a campus, and what happens when they do? What does a pageant do inside a Black institution? Why did a network sitcom put an HIV diagnosis on prime time in 1991, and what does a health crisis mean on television now? The course treats Black experience not as a topic adjacent to "general" sociology but as the terrain on which general theory — stratification, institutions, culture, the self — is tested and revised. Students finish able to read a popular text sociologically, to evaluate empirical claims about Black life against data, and to write for a non-specialist audience.

2. Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students can:

# Outcome
LO1 Apply core sociological frameworks — structure and agency, stratification, institutions, culture, the self — to a single sustained case across 39 years
LO2 Analyse how a commercial entertainment text encodes, contests, and reproduces racial ideology
LO3 Use double consciousness and its later refinements (intersectionality, racial formation) to analyse identity as both social structure and meaning-making
LO4 Evaluate empirical claims about Black life in the United States — education, family, wealth, health, media, policing — and distinguish structural accounts from deficit narratives
LO5 Write sociological analysis for a non-specialist audience, with citations and an explicit account of evidence
LO6 Connect a Black-specific case to general social theory, demonstrating how Black experience is constitutive of American society

3. How the course works

The callback as method. Every session opens with a 2026 episode and its 1987–1993 counterpart. The sociology lives in the comparison: what the earlier text could say, what the later one assumes, what neither can settle. Students do the pairing themselves from Session 10 on.

Two texts, one campus. The 2026 revival is 10 episodes. The original is 144. So the original does the historical work the revival cannot: it is the primary source for what a Black campus story was allowed to be on network television in 1987, and the point of comparison for what a streaming audience is offered in 2026.

Where the episodes are. The 2026 season (10 episodes) streams on Netflix. Availability of the 1987–1993 series varies by region and service — verify before you set the syllabus, and tell students where to find it (library DVD sets, streaming, or the selected scenes you screen in class). Each session notes which original episode is essential so you can screen a clip if students cannot access the series.

The reference site. All sessions and homework point at a documented reference site that pairs the revival's callbacks with the original episodes and labels its evidence: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/ — students are expected to use it and to check it, because every entry there carries a tier and a source. Homework 1 formalises that habit.

6. Policies

Content note. This course discusses racism, racial violence, HIV and sexual health, intimate relationship conflict, and a health crisis presented in Episode 5 that the show's own summary does not name. You will be told in advance what each session involves, and no one is required to disclose personal experience to participate. If a session's material lands hard, the instructor will say so and make room; campus counselling is listed in the instructor guide.

Discussion norms. Everyone speaks from the text and the evidence; nobody is asked to speak for a group; disagreement is expected and is graded on argument, not on comfort.

Academic integrity and AI. Generative AI may be used for transcription, accessibility, and language polishing with disclosure. It may not be used to generate the analysis, the reading of a scene, or the argument: those are what you are being taught to do. Any submission may be discussed orally; you should be able to defend every sentence you hand in. Undisclosed AI-generated analysis is a violation. This is enforced by the structure of the assignments, not by surveillance — most homework asks for something AI cannot supply: your own claim, tied to a specific scene, checked against a specific source.

Accessibility. All screened material is captioned. Slides, handouts and the course site are screen-reader compatible; accommodations are honoured without documentation theatre — talk to me.

Late work. Homework is worth little individually and matters cumulatively: one week late, half credit; after two weeks, no credit but a conversation. Exams are due as scheduled except by prior arrangement.

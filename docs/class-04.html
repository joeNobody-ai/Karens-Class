Session 4 — Intimacy, Gender, and the Script of Romance

Big question. Is romance personal or structural — and what changes when both are true?

Where we are. Session 3 showed the community performing itself. Today we go into the smallest unit the show cares about: two people negotiating. The revival gives us "cuffing season" and a friend group whose hook-ups collide with exams and money; the original gives us the five-season courtship that the revival's entire family premise depends on.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 4, Those Who Can...Cuff 2026-09-24 "Cuffing season" and a group whose relationships tangle with academic pressure and money
Original S2E26, Dream Lover 1988-11-03 Dwayne notices Whitley at a party, she runs out of the dance, dreams about him, then denies everything
Original (clip) S1E4, Those Who Can't...Tutor 1987-10-22 The title the revival's Episode 4 is playing with — tutoring, curfew, and a cover story

Homework 3 is due at the start of this session.

Sociological frame

  1. Doing gender (Candace West and Don Zimmerman, 1987): gender is not what one is but what one does, continuously, and is held accountable for. The courting scenes in both shows are gender being manufactured in public, with an audience.
  2. Mate selection as structure. Who is available, who is desirable, who marries whom is patterned by class, education, geography and race — not merely by feeling. The original's plot works because Whitley and Dwayne are not an obvious match: she is status-driven, he is a math nerd with no polish. Ask whether a 2026 audience would still bet on them.
  3. The marriage market and its vocabulary. "Cuffing season" names a temporally bounded, mildly ironic commitment — a phrase the 1988 characters did not have. Vocabulary is evidence: it tells you what a relationship is expected to be for.
  4. Black feminist thought on love and work (Collins, 1990): intimacy is not a private refuge from racial and economic pressure; it is where that pressure gets processed. Both shows put money in the middle of romance.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 3 in. Two students present their ritual ethnography; the room checks the evidence tier of each claim they made
10–25 The pairing. Map each episode's romantic sequence as a chain of moves: approach, refusal, cover story, public claim, private admission
25–40 West and Zimmerman, applied: for each move, who is being held accountable for doing gender correctly — and by whom?
40–55 Structure enters the room. Where does money appear in each version? Track every scene that turns on cost, work or debt. Compare the 1988 "she takes a job" plot to the 2026 financial-squeeze plot
55–68 Vocabulary drill. List what each generation calls the same phenomenon (dating, hooking up, cuffing, going together, seeing someone). What does each word assume about duration, exclusivity and expectation?
68–75 Assign homework 4. Preview Session 5 — and give the content warning early: HIV, illness, and a death

Discussion questions

  1. If gender is something you do, who is the audience in a college romance — and what happens to the performance when the audience leaves?
  2. Whitley runs out of the dance. Is that refusal, or is it the script working correctly?
  3. The revival's Episode 4 puts romance next to academic and financial pressure. Is that plot crowding, or the point?
  4. "Cuffing season" is a joke about commitment with an expiry date. What social conditions make that joke legible?
  5. What is the sociological difference between I could not find love and the structure of my options was narrow?

Instructor notes

If the room wants to debate whether the couples "should" be together. Let it happen, then convert: what in the structure of the campus made these two the available options? Enrolment, geography, the major, the dorm, the money. Romance on a small campus is a constrained matching problem — that is the analytic move, and it is a genuinely cheerful one to teach in week four.

If a student says the 1988 courtship is "toxic". Take the claim seriously and test it: identify the behaviour, name the norm it violates, and ask whether the norm existed in the text's world. The best outcome is that the room ends up distinguishing evaluating a relationship from analysing a script — an ability students will need in every subsequent session.

If the discussion turns confessional. Redirect gently but firmly: the seminar analyses texts and patterns, and no one should feel their own history is the material. The move that works: "That's a pattern we can name without naming you — what would we need to know to say it's typical?"

Data point for the board (verify before teaching). Claims about Black marriage rates, single parenthood and relationship formation are exactly where sloppy numbers breed deficit narratives. If you put a figure up, put its source and its year next to it, and note that Session 13 devotes a full session to the history of that arithmetic. Do not let the room leave believing a statistic it did not read.

Content note. Sexual content is discussed clinically; there is no explicit material. Say so at the top so students who brace for the wrong thing can relax.

Session 2 — How Sociologists Know: Methods and Evidence

Big question. How would you check a claim about the show or the world?

Where we start. Last week we read two openings. This week we ask the question that makes reading count: how do you know? A sociologist who cannot tell a documented fact from an interpretation is not doing sociology — they are doing opinion. The reference site is the course's worked example of evidence, so we use it as the textbook.

Sociological frame

  1. Empirical claim: a statement that could in principle be shown true or false with evidence. "The revival premiered on 24 September 2026" is one; "the revival argues that inheritance is a burden" is not.
  2. Evidence: the material that bears on a claim — a date, a credit, a summary, a statistic. Evidence is not the same as proof; it is what a claim has to answer to.
  3. Fact versus interpretation: a fact can be looked up; an interpretation is a reading you defend and someone else can disagree with while you are both still right about the facts.
  4. The three tiers: the course's shorthand — documented (look-up-able), interpretation (a labelled reading), hypothesis (a guess on the table for testing).

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Warm-up. On the board: three claims about the show. The room votes — documented, interpretation, or hypothesis — and defends the votes
10–22 What counts as evidence. Distinguish a fact (look-up-able) from a reading (defensible) from a guess (testable). Work one callback card slowly
22–35 The reference site as a machine. Walk one card end to end: what it claims, what tier it assigns, what source it cites, and what would move it up or down a tier
35–50 Methods in one breath. Surveys, interviews, observation, documents, and experiments — the standard toolkit, each matched to a question the course will ask later
50–63 Drill. Each pair picks a card and completes the homework's four-part sort: claim, tier, the card's label, and what would change their mind
63–75 Close. Why the evidence rule is graded all term. Assign homework 2

Discussion questions

  1. Which of the three tiers is hardest to tell apart in practice, and why?
  2. Give an example of a claim that is documented but misleading, and one that is interpretation but true.
  3. What is the difference between "there is no evidence" and "we have not looked"?
  4. Why does a sociology course spend a whole week on evidence rather than jumping to the big topics?
  5. What would it take for you to change your mind about a reading of a scene you hold strongly?

Instructor notes

If the room splits on a card's tier. Do not settle it too fast. The disagreement is the lesson: ask each side what evidence would move them. A claim nobody can imagine evidence for is the interesting one.

If a student says "but all interpretations are just opinions." Push back precisely: an interpretation can be wrong and still be an interpretation — it is not the same as an opinion, because it has to answer to the text and can be defended or defeated with reasons. An opinion has no such obligation.

Common misconception to head off. "The show says X" is almost always an interpretation dressed as a fact. The fastest teaching move all term is: is that documented, or is that you?

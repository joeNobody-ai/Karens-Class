Rubrics

Short, reusable, used every week. If a rubric takes longer to read than the assignment took to grade, it is the wrong rubric.

1. Weekly concept application (out of 10)

Points Criterion The question you are asking
3–4 Concept use Is the concept used for something, or just named? Is it applied to a specific scene or case?
2–3 Analytical work Does the submission make a claim, or summarise? Does it answer the question asked?
2 Evidence discipline Are documented facts and interpretations kept apart? Is the student's own observation marked as theirs?
1 Precision of prose Clear, no padding, no throat-clearing.

Written feedback formula: one thing that worked, one thing to fix, one sentence on evidence handling. Three sentences, every time. Students read these; they do not read numbers.

The automatic rewrite request: a submission that asserts a population claim from a sample of one, or presents an interpretation as documented fact. Return it once, ungraded, with the rewrite condition stated. Almost everyone fixes it, and nobody has to lose marks to learn it.

2. Participation and discussion (15% of the course grade)

Points Criterion
4 Facilitation (module led) — opened the material, brought at least two people in, kept the room on the text, closed with a question
3 Analytical contribution — claims with evidence; built on others rather than replacing them
3 Norms — respected the no-spokesperson rule; engaged the strongest version of a view they disagreed with; allowed silence
2 Text discipline — kept the room anchored; checked claims rather than letting them pass
3 Intellectual honesty — changed position when the evidence required it, said so, and named what changed their mind

What does not earn credit: airtime, repetition, performing certainty, or "I just feel like".

3. Data exercise (homework 7)

Points Criterion Automatic failures
3 Source quality — primary, current, correctly described Citing a news article about a study; citing an advocacy summary as primary
3 Completeness — figure, source, year, denominator or comparison group Missing denominator or comparison group
2 Honesty about limits — "what this cannot tell us" is about measurement, not a hedge "It might not be accurate" (a shrug, not a limit)
2 Use — the figure is used in an argument without overclaiming Treating a correlational figure as causal

4. Scene analysis (final, Part B — out of 20)

Points Criterion
3 Documented fact accurate and clearly separated from interpretation
6 Three concepts used, each doing distinct work
3 Structure and agency — one of each identified in the scene
3 Honest limit — what the text cannot show, named
3 Evidence discipline — tiers visible throughout
2 Sources — two readings cited for something, not decoratively

5. Capstone: explain a concept to a non-major (homework 15, 10% of the course grade)

Points Criterion
5 Plain definition — a non-major could repeat it
5 Concrete example — carries the concept, not decorates it
4 Stakes — why it matters, argued with a real consequence
3 Honest limit — about the concept, not a hedge
3 No jargon as crutch — documented facts and interpretation kept apart

6. Self-assessment prompt (Module 15, ungraded — for your own use)

Hand out and collect before the final. It is the best course evaluation you will get, and it takes five minutes.

  1. Which module changed how you see something you previously took for granted?
  2. Name one concept you will still use in five years.
  3. Where did this course let you get away with sloppy evidence?
  4. What is the strongest argument that this course should not exist?
  5. One thing you would teach differently if you were teaching it.

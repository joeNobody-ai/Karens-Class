Session 6 — Deviance, Stigma, and Social Control

Big question. Who gets labelled deviant, and what does the label do?

Where we start. Finals week, and then something worse arrives that the show's own summary does not name. We pair it with the episode the original made famous: a 1991 prime-time sitcom put an HIV diagnosis at its centre. Deviance is not a property of an act — it is a label applied by people with the power to apply it.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 5, Final Words 2026-09-24 A crisis interrupts finals week (the published summary does not name it)
Original (1991) S4E92, If I Should Die Before I Wake 1991-04-11 The episode that put an HIV disclosure at the centre of a sitcom
Optional clip The 1991 disclosure scene, if you can source it — The stigma mechanism, dramatised

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Deviance: behaviour that violates a group's norms — but the key move is that deviance is labelled, not inherent. The same act is deviant or not depending on who does it, where, and who is watching.
  2. Labeling theory: the label, once applied, sticks and reshapes the person — a "deviant" identity becomes a master status that overrides everything else.
  3. Stigma (Goffman): a mark that spoils identity — an attribute that makes a person "less than" in the eyes of others. HIV carried a stigma that became its own second illness.
  4. Social control: the mechanisms — formal and informal — a society uses to keep behaviour in line, from a raised eyebrow to a sanction.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Set the frame carefully. Announce the content note: health crisis, stigma, and (in the original) an HIV disclosure. Screen the 1991 disclosure if available; otherwise describe it from the documented summary
10–24 Define deviance and labelling. Why deviance is a property of the reaction, not the act. The master status
24–38 Stigma and disclosure. Goffman's spoiled identity; why disclosure is costly; what the 1991 episode did by making it public on prime time
38–52 Social control. Formal versus informal; the medicalization of deviance — when a "moral" failing becomes a "medical" condition and what that changes
52–64 Pair drill. Each pair names one act in the week's material, who labels it, who is spared the label, and what the label costs the labelled person
64–75 Close and assign. Why the 2026 episode keeps its crisis unnamed (and what we can and cannot say about it). Assign homework 6

Discussion questions

  1. Is the deviant the person who breaks the rule, or the person who gets labelled? Argue with an example.
  2. What did putting an HIV disclosure on prime-time television in 1991 do — and what could it not do?
  3. Name a label that has stuck to you or someone you know. What did it change about how you were treated?
  4. What is the difference between a medical label and a moral label — and who decides which one applies?
  5. Why might a show choose not to name its own crisis? What does the silence do?

Instructor notes

Watch Ep 5 yourself before teaching this. Its published summary does not name the crisis it depicts. Run the module on the documented material (the 1991 episode's facts, the 2026 episode's structural position in the season) and be explicit that the 2026 crisis is unnamed in the sources. Do not fill the gap from memory — the evidence rule exists for exactly this.

If a student discloses something personal. Do not make them the example. Thank them, move the room back to the text, and follow your campus's care procedures. The material is heavy and the module says so up front.

If someone argues HIV was "a 1990s thing." The episode is a historical document of stigma, but stigma is not historical — ask what conditions carry the same weight now. The concept outlives the example.

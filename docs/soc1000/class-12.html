Session 12 — Media, Representation, and the Sitcom as Text

Big question. Who controls the image, and what does it do?

Where we start. The show has spent the term teaching us sociology. Now we turn the lens on the show itself: who makes it, who it depicts, and what an image does to the people it depicts. A sitcom is not a window onto a community; it is a site where a community's image is fought over.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 2, O Captain! My Captain! 2026-09-24 A student speaks in public and gets called out — speech and its costs
Original (1988) S1E16, The Show Must Go On 1988-02-18 The show itself as the subject: image, audience, production
Optional clip The two openings again (from Module 1) — Now read for who made them, not what they mean

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Encoding and decoding (Stuart Hall): a text is encoded with meaning by its makers and decoded by its audience — and the two readings never perfectly match.
  2. Representation: the act of depicting a group — and the power question of who depicts whom, for whom, and with what consequences.
  3. Framing: the selection and emphasis that shapes how an image or issue is understood — what is included, what is left out, whose angle is "normal."
  4. The Black sitcom: a specific battleground — a genre that has carried both visibility for Black life and the constraints of what a network or a streamer will sell.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Screen the public call-out. Ask: who is controlling the image in this scene, and who is being imaged?
10–24 Define representation and framing. Depiction is a power relation, not a mirror. What "normal" framing hides
24–38 Encoding and decoding. The show as a made object: writers, directors, a network, an audience. Where the readings diverge
38–52 The Black sitcom as battleground. Visibility and its limits; the 2026 revival's new creative team and returning cast; the show's own origins and the Cosby problem, met head-on
52–64 Pair drill. Each pair takes one image from the season and names who made it, who it is for, and what it emphasises and omits
64–75 Close and assign. Bridge to Module 13 (the student as political actor). Assign homework 12

Discussion questions

  1. Who is "the audience" for this show — and how do you know?
  2. What does it mean to be seen by a text but not controlled by it?
  3. Name something the framing leaves out, and what changes when you add it back.
  4. How should the show's origins (a spinoff whose creator was later convicted) shape how we read it?
  5. Who controls the image of a community — the community, the creators, or the market?

Instructor notes

If the room wants to dismiss the show as "Hollywood." That is the starting point, not the end: the show is a commercial product, and because of that it is a perfect object for studying how images are produced and sold. The commodity nature is data, not disqualification.

If the Cosby question returns. This is the module for it. Teach it: the gap between an image and the institution that produced it is itself a sociological finding. Do not resolve it into comfort.

If a student asks whether representation "matters." Hold both sides: representation is not a substitute for policy, and it is also not nothing — images shape who is imaginable as what. The evidence for each is different; keep them apart.

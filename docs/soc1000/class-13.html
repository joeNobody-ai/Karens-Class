Session 13 — Social Movements and Social Change

Big question. When does a student become a political actor?

Where we start. A student speaks in public and is called out; a generation earlier, students held a sit-in after their radio show was pulled; and a season premiere once opened on an uprising after a police brutality trial. Change does not just happen — it is organized, and campuses are where a lot of it learns to walk.

Sociological frame

  1. Collective behavior: relatively spontaneous, unstructured group action — the raw material of a movement before it is organized.
  2. Social movement: sustained, organized collective action aimed at change (or blocking change).
  3. Framing (Snow and Benford): how a movement defines the problem and its solution — the diagnostic frame ("this is unjust"), the prognostic frame ("here is what to do"), and the motivational frame ("we can do this").
  4. Resource mobilization: a movement needs more than grievance — it needs people, money, networks, and legitimacy, and its fate turns on how it gets them.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Screen the sit-in (1989) or the 2026 call-out. Ask: what turns a complaint into a movement?
10–24 Define collective behavior and social movements. The difference between a crowd and a campaign
24–38 Framing. Walk the diagnostic/prognostic/motivational triad on one of the week's fights. How the problem is named shapes the response
38–52 Resource mobilization. The sit-in needed a station, an audience, and a dean who would blink. The 2026 fight needs different resources. What changed
52–64 Pair drill. Each pair takes one movement moment and names its frame, its resources, and its cost to the participants
64–75 Close and assign. Bridge to Module 14 (synthesis). Assign homework 13

Discussion questions

  1. What is the difference between being angry and being in a movement?
  2. Who bears the cost of a student protest — and who did in 1989 versus 2026?
  3. How does the way a problem is framed change what counts as a solution?
  4. What resource does a movement need that it usually does not have?
  5. When does a student stop being a student and become a political actor — and who gets to decide?

Instructor notes

If a student says "protests don't work." Make them specify: work at what, over what timescale? Movements change law, culture, and the people in them on different clocks. The claim needs a frame before it can be assessed.

If the room wants to debate the 2026 season's politics in the abstract. Pull it back to mechanism: what the text actually shows (who speaks, who pays, who organizes) rather than what anyone wishes it showed.

If someone raises the Rodney King precedent (the 1992 season premiere). It is documented on the reference site; name it as the franchise's own history of putting a movement on prime time, and note it is Module 14's material if they want the long version.

Session 10 — Budgets, Institutions, and Racialized Organizations

Big question. What is an institution for — and what happens when the money runs out?

Where we are. Nine sessions in, the campus has been a place of identity, ritual, romance, care and conflict. Today it becomes a budget. The revival's ninth episode puts Hillman's survival on the table; the original's second season had already established that money runs underneath everything, including the scholarships that keep students enrolled.

Before class: viewing

Homework 9 is due at the start of this session. Midterm grades returned today — with one paragraph of feedback per student, not just a number.

Sociological frame

  1. Racialized organizations (Victor Ray, "A Theory of Racialized Organizations", 2019): organisations can distribute resources and legitimacy along racial lines without anyone intending to, because their rules, routines and hiring patterns carry history. Hillman is an organisation with a budget, a board, and a donor base — and its constraints are not neutral.
  2. Resource dependence (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978): organisations do what their resource environment allows, and are shaped by whoever holds the resources. Put the revival's funding crisis next to the original's donor, scholarship and job plots and the structure is identical.
  3. HBCU funding as policy. Historically Black institutions have been financed through a long, documented pattern of unequal state support and reliance on private philanthropy and federal programmes. The course should not pretend this is background; it is the plot.
  4. Student debt as stratification. Debt decides who can stay, who takes the extra shift, who graduates and who leaves. Both shows put a working student at the centre of the money story.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Midterms back with a sentence of feedback each. Then: what did the exam show the room understood as a body of work, not individually
10–25 The pairing. Reconstruct the budget crisis from the episode's own details: who is in the room, who is absent, what the options are, what is not on the table
25–40 Racialized organisations and resource dependence, applied. Ask: which of Hillman's problems would exist if the institution were wealthy, and which are structural regardless?
40–55 The 1989 job plot. One student, one damaged car, no family money, paid work. Compare it to how the 2026 characters meet their financial squeeze. What has changed: the labour, the dignity, the options — or the depiction?
55–68 Organising. Students in Ep 9 refuse to "sit back and watch". Ask what a student body can actually change about an institution's finances — and what it cannot. Session 14 takes the other half of this question to the state
68–75 Assign homework 10 (which includes a data exercise on HBCU funding). Preview Session 11: the finale, and what the season is arguing about survival

Discussion questions

  1. Hillman's crisis is financial. Why is the episode's title about romance?
  2. When a college closes, who loses what — students, employees, the surrounding neighbourhood, the alumni, the idea?
  3. The 1989 episode treats paid work as a solution. Is that dignity, or a subsidy the institution should have provided?
  4. Which decisions in a budget crisis are made by people, and which by rules that no one in the room wrote?
  5. Student organising is aimed at administrators. If the resource constraint is external, is the target chosen correctly?

Instructor notes

If the room blames the administration. Useful, but incomplete. Push to the resource environment: who controls the money the college needs, and what do they get in return? This is the session where students learn that "the institution" is a set of relationships with a balance sheet, not a villain.

If someone says this is "just a business plot". Take it as the opening: the sociological content is precisely that a college is an organisation, with the same survival pressures as a business and different legitimacy claims than a business. Ask what a college is for if not profit — then test whether the budget lines match the answer.

The data exercise in homework 10 is the term's hardest. Students must find a current, citable figure about HBCU funding or enrolment and explain what it does and does not measure. Expect confusion between institution counts, enrolment shares and per-student appropriations; that confusion is the lesson, and it will come up again in Session 14.

If a student's own institution is in financial trouble. Let the structural analysis carry it, and do not allow the seminar to become a rumour exchange. Refer specifics to public documents, and note that the course's method — documented claim, source, tier — is exactly what a campus in crisis needs from its students.

Content note. None. This is the most "normal" reading load of the term; use the spare attention to run the data exercise in class if you have time, with laptops out.

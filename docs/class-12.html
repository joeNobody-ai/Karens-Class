Session 12 — Media, Image, and the Politics of Representation

Big question. Who controls the image — and what does an image do to the people it depicts?

Where we are. The episodic half of the course is finished. For the last four sessions we stop walking the season and start interrogating it. Today's subject is the medium itself: not what the shows say about Black life, but what it means that these shows exist, in these forms, made by these people, for these audiences.

Homework 11 is due at the start of this session.

Reading: Herman Gray, Watching Race (1995), introduction; Robin R. Means Coleman, African American Viewers and the Black Situation Comedy (1998), ch. 1; Stuart Hall, "Encoding/Decoding" (1973).

Sociological frame

  1. Encoding and decoding (Stuart Hall, 1973): a text carries a preferred reading, but audiences negotiate or oppose it. This is the theoretical licence for everything this course has done — reading against the grain of a show we also enjoy.
  2. Representation as a struggle, not a mirror (Gray, 1995): what appears on screen is the outcome of institutional fights over money, writers' rooms, network standards and audience research.
  3. Respectability and the image. Ruthless question for today: when a series is accused of being "positive" or "negative", who is the accusation for, and what does it assume about white viewers?
  4. The economics of who gets to be seen. A network spinoff in 1987 and a Netflix revival in 2026 are different industrial arrangements: different episode counts, different risk models, different audience measurement. Form follows finance.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 11 in: five one-sentence arguments read aloud, the room attacks them. Good, quick, brutal
10–30 Screen a sequence. Riggs' Color Adjustment (a 10–15 minute excerpt you choose), then compare it to the two opening episodes from Session 1. The room now has a historical frame
30–45 Hall's model on the board: encoding, preferred reading, negotiated reading, oppositional reading. Assign each table a different reading position and have them re-analyse the same scene
45–60 The industry question. Compare the two productions: 1987 network, 22-episode seasons, standards departments, live studio audience; 2026 streaming, 10 episodes, binge-release, global distribution. What does each arrangement make possible — and impossible?
60–72 The criticism question. Was the 2026 revival "a remake", "a legacy sequel", "nostalgia product"? What does each label assume? Students must use evidence from the episodes, the credits and the production history
72–75 Assign homework 12. Preview Session 13: family, class, and the word "culture"

Discussion questions

  1. Does an image of a Black college change anything off screen? What would the evidence for "yes" look like?
  2. Hall says texts have a preferred reading. What is the preferred reading of the revival — and what happens if you refuse it?
  3. Riggs documented how television has repeatedly absorbed and neutered Black cultural forms. Where do you see that pattern in either show?
  4. A legacy sequel both invokes and revises its original. Which does the 2026 show do more of?
  5. Who is the imagined audience? Answer for each show separately, and say how the text tells you.

Instructor notes

If the room defends the show against criticism. That is a fine place to start; require the defence to use evidence. "The show did X because it needed to reach a new generation" is a testable industrial claim — check the episode count, the release pattern, the casting and the streaming platform. Nothing deflates or confirms a suspicion faster than a documented production fact.

If the room treats representation as simply counting faces. Push to mechanism: writing staff, budgets, marketing decisions, time slots, distribution, who reads for the role, who directs. Gray's point is that images are outcomes. Counting is a starting move, not an argument.

If a student objects that this is "reading too much into it". Excellent opportunity: ask what "too much" would mean, and how much reading is exactly right. Then point out that the show itself invites the reading — the title echoes are deliberate jokes for people who know the original. The text is already doing close reading; we are just not pretending otherwise.

If your institution's library has Riggs. Screen it properly, on a real screen, with the lights down. The film is a piece of television history and deserves better than a laptop speaker.

Content note. Color Adjustment contains historical racist imagery, including minstrel-era material, as its subject. Warn the room in advance: the film uses offensive images to analyse them.

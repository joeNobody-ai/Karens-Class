Lingo & slang — the vocabulary both series run on

This is the course's vocabulary reference. It exists because the shows' dialogue lives in a register that most students in the room will recognise at sight and few will be able to date, place or analyse — and because "they just talk normally" is the most common unexamined assumption a seminar brings to a text about a Black campus.

The full tiered reference — 112 entries, every one sourced — is on the companion site: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/lingo.html (The Netflix-style view carries the same data: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/lingo.html)

Each entry now carries two things this course leans on directly:

  1. Senses — for 14 terms that genuinely mean more than one thing (shade, line, hook up, the Pit, probate, drag, mess, homecoming), the reference separates the senses rather than pretending a word has one meaning. That is the single best short exercise in the whole course for teaching that meaning is contextual: put four senses of line on the board and ask which one a scene is using, and how the room knows.
  2. Watch links — 64 verified video links (14 of them from official channels) so a student can see the term in use. Every link was checked through YouTube's own metadata endpoint, and each shows the uploader's own title and channel, labelled official or third-party. Note what these are and are not: they are video-level evidence (the clip exists, and this is what its maker called it), not quoted dialogue. Captions are blocked from the machine that builds this project — the reasoning and the eight routes tried are documented in scripts/fetch_youtube.py, which also ingests a transcript the moment someone can supply one, and which refuses to publish a quoted line that has no recorded transcript behind it.

Read it before Session 3. Assign it before Session 8.

1. The evidence rule, applied to vocabulary

The course's three tiers apply here exactly as they do to every other claim, and the site's reference is built on them:

Tier What it means for a word Example
Documented The term appears in the material we verify — an episode's published summary, or a 2026 episode title — and the citation is given "the dozens": the original's Mammy Dearest (S5E105, 1991-12-05) explicitly addresses its origins
Era lexicon Real vocabulary of the period, sourced to dictionaries and scholarship, not verified as said on screen bougie: Whitley's register, attested in the slang literature, unverified in dialogue because nobody on this course has watched with a transcript
Handle with care Racialised or derogatory vocabulary that belongs in analysis, never in speech Mammy: the imagery a 1991 episode put at the centre of a campus exhibit

Say this out loud in Session 1: we cannot verify what was said on screen, and we will not pretend otherwise. That admission is not a weakness of the course — it is the same discipline the course demands of students in every essay.

3. Three classroom exercises (each takes 10–15 minutes)

1. The vocabulary gap (Session 4). Put the era columns side by side. Ask the room to state, for one pair (going together → talking stage), what each term assumes about duration, exclusivity, formality, and who initiates. The room will discover that vocabulary is a theory of the relationship, and that the 2026 terms are shorter-lived on purpose — this is week four's whole argument, built from two words.

2. Who is allowed to say what (Session 5, with care). Take the handle-with-care section and ask the room to sort those terms by who uses them and about whom. The pattern that emerges — most were coined outside Black communities and about Black people — is the entire critique of the deficit tradition in one exercise. Protocol: the instructor does not pronounce the terms at all; they are on the slide, numbered, discussed by number. This also protects any student in the room from being recruited as a pronunciation authority.

3. The definition fight (Session 12). Give half the room a dictionary definition (Green's, the OED) and half the room the term's current in-use meaning as students know it. Ask which is "the" definition. They will produce the textbook argument about dictionaries being records of usage rather than law — which is the media-and-representation session's question in miniature.

4. The protocol for the instructors (read this before Session 4)

  1. Do not perform the vernacular. A tenured professor using students' slang to seem current converts data into costume. The course's method is to hold the vocabulary at analytic distance.
  2. Nobody is a consultant. No student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term — least of all a Black student in a mixed room. Definitions come from the cited sources.
  3. No term is ever an insult in the room. The handle-with-care vocabulary is numbered on slides and discussed by number and history.
  4. Date everything. A term's period matters: jive turkey was already dated when the original premiered in 1987; rizz post-dates the revival's characters' childhoods. Dating vocabulary is how a course teaches that language changes faster than institutions — which is the point of the callback method, in miniature.
  5. Never correct a student's vernacular. The subject is how language is used and judged in institutions, not whether a student's speech passes.

5. Why this belongs in a sociology course

Three of the field's standard moves are visible in vocabulary:

  • Stratification: bougie is a class judgement carried inside a racialised group; colourism and respectability are audible in the lexicon before they are visible in behaviour (Session 8).
  • Social control: signifying and the dozens are the community's own enforcement mechanisms — sanction without violence, reputation managed in public (Sessions 3 and 13).
  • Institutional power: who may say what, in which register, to whom is exactly the question in Sessions 2 and 12. Code-switching is not a quirk of individuals; it is what a stratified society requires of people who must be legible in more than one room.

And the honest limit: this reference is a lexicon, not a portrait. It cannot tell you how a character felt, and it must never stand in for watching the work.

6. Using it in assessment

  • Homework 4 (vocabulary as evidence) is built directly on section 3 of the reference — students must build their own table from the sources and mark which terms they can cite and which they cannot.
  • Homework 8 requires one documented consequence of colourism or appearance-based stratification from the research literature; the reference supplies the vocabulary, the scholarship supplies the evidence.
  • The final exam's Part A can ask a student to assess a claim built on this vocabulary ("colourism exists because Black people internalised racism") — the reference is the tool for taking it apart.

7. Sources

Every entry in the online reference carries its own sources, and they are the same ones this course uses: Green's Dictionary of Slang · the Oxford English Dictionary · the Linguistic Society of America's material on African American English and its 1997 resolution on Ebonics · the International Dialects of English Archive · Wikipedia's articles on AAVE, signifying, the dozens, stepping, the National Pan-Hellenic Council, Greek life, hazing, and the individual slang terms · and three standard works: Geneva Smitherman, Black Talk (1994); Clarence Major, Juba to Jive (1994); Rickford and Rickford, Spoken Soul (2000). Merriam-Webster's entries are cited too, with the note that their site refuses automated checks — verify in a browser.

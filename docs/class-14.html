Session 14 — The State, the Street, and the Ledger

Big question. How did a family sitcom put an uprising in its season premiere — and where is that tradition now?

Where we are. Session 13 cleared away the culture explanation. Today we put three structural systems on the table: policing, wealth, and the state that mediates both. The original opens its final season in September 1992 at the Rodney King trial and the Los Angeles uprising — and the revival's ninth episode, in 2026, is about students organising against institutional failure.

Before class: viewing and reading

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Original S6E120, Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) 1992-09-24 The season premiere opens on the Rodney King police-brutality trial; a discussion of the verdict sends a couple back to Los Angeles during the uprising that followed (the story continues into the following episode)
Revival Ep 9, Romancing the Unknown, revisited 2026-09-24 Students refuse to "simply sit back and watch" as Hillman's crisis unfolds
Revival Ep 2, O Captain! My Captain!, revisited 2026-09-24 The cost of speaking: a student publicly called out

Homework 13 is due at the start of this session.

Reading: Michelle Alexander, The New Jim Crow (2010), introduction; Richard Rothstein, The Color of Law (2017), introduction; Isabel Wilkerson, The Warmth of Other Suns (2010), epilogue; and one current data source you verify yourself (see the data exercise below).

Sociological frame

  1. Policing as social control. Contemporary sociology treats policing not as an individual encounter but as a system of governance with measurable distributional patterns. The 1992 premiere put that system at the centre of a comedy's season opening, in a network time slot, and trusted its audience with it.
  2. Racial wealth, not racial income. Wealth is inherited, extracted, and secured by law — through redlining, contract sales, expropriation, and unequal credit. Rothstein's point is that the architecture was legal. Income gaps can narrow while wealth gaps widen; this is the single most useful correction students can take from the session.
  3. State violence and legitimation. The state claims a monopoly on legitimate force. The 1992 verdict is a case study in the gap between the claim to legitimacy and the perception of it — which is what an uprising makes legible.
  4. Movement response. From the 1992 Los Angeles uprising to contemporary protest, the question for sociologists is what movements change, what they cost the people in them, and how they are narrated afterwards.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 13 in: the steel-man/falsify exercise. Two students state the strongest version of a culture argument and then the evidence that would settle it
10–30 The 1992 premiere. Screen the episode's opening and the trial discussion. Then place the uprising on the board in factual terms: verdict, uprising, the city's demographics, the response
30–45 Wealth versus income. Put a wealth-gap figure on the board with its source and year, then a median-income figure from the same year. Ask what they each measure, and why a policy can improve the second while the first worsens
45–58 The 2026 text. What does the revival's organising plot ask students to change — and is the target an institution, a policy, or the public? Compare its strategy to the 1992 premiere's act of narration
58–70 The data exercise, in the room. Students open their homework 14 sources on laptops and report: figure, source, year, denominator, and one thing the figure cannot tell us
70–75 Assign homework 14. Preview Session 15: the capstone and the final

Discussion questions

  1. A network sitcom in 1992 put a police-brutality trial and an uprising in its season premiere. What does that fact tell you about the audience the show assumed it had?
  2. Why can income gaps narrow while wealth gaps widen? Give the mechanism, not the slogan.
  3. The revival's students organise against a budget crisis. What can a campus movement actually move: money, policy, or public opinion?
  4. Who decides when a disturbance is a riot, an uprising, or a protest — and what does each word do?
  5. If Rothstein is right that the segregation architecture was legal, what follows for remedies that focus on individual discrimination?

Instructor notes

Use facts, not vibes, for the 1992 material. Have the dates, the verdicts and the timeline accurate. Students may arrive with strong second-hand narratives of the uprising; the discipline's contribution is to specify. Where accounts are contested, say so and show the disagreement rather than picking a side silently.

If the room wants to relitigate a recent case. Permit one pass, then move to patterns and sources. This course is not a forum for adjudicating individual incidents, and the interesting sociology is systemic. If a student is personally connected to a case, the same rule as every prior session: no one is a sample, and nothing here requires disclosure.

If the data exercise exposes honest confusion. That is the point of the session. Institution counts, enrolment shares, per-student appropriations, and median household wealth are four different things, and students who can distinguish them are already more literate than most public argument.

If someone asks why the revival does not put an uprising on screen. This is the sharpest question available and it should be the last one you take. Ask what a streaming show in 2026 can put in a drama that a network sitcom in 1992 put in a family comedy slot, and what the constraints suggest about audiences, revenue, and risk. Then note the honest answer: we do not know the writers' reasoning. What we can document is the difference in what got made.

Content note. Police violence, state violence, and the 1992 Los Angeles uprising. Announce this at the start of the previous session; students may have direct connections to these subjects, including loss. Do not show footage that is not required by the reading.

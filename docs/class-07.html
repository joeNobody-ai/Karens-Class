Session 7 — Diaspora: Who Belongs to the "Black Experience"?

Big question. What is at stake when a category that looks unified is contested from inside?

Where we are. Six sessions in, "Black" has been the common term. Today the text cracks it open: a 2026 student finds himself in an argument about African and diaspora identity, and the original answers from 1990 with a dilemma about anti-apartheid principle and one student's scholarship.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 7, Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You — first half: the identity argument, the talent show frame 2026-09-24 Kojo is caught up in arguments about African/diaspora identity and culture. (The second half of this episode is Session 8)
Original S3E60, A World Alike 1990-02-15 An exchange student in Hillman's anti-apartheid group; a scholarship funded by a company with South African investments; a proposed boycott that would cost a Black student her funding

Homework 6 is due at the start of this session.

Sociological frame

  1. Diaspora is not a synonym for shared descent. It names a relationship to a place of origin that is mediated by history, distance and power. Two people racialised the same way in the United States can hold incompatible accounts of what "Africa" means to them — that is the argument, and it is not a misunderstanding to be resolved.
  2. Racial formation (Michael Omi and Howard Winant, 1986/2014): race is a social process in which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed and destroyed. The revival's argument is formation happening on camera.
  3. The global colour line (W. E. B. Du Bois, 1903 and again in 1915/1920s writing): the colour line is international, and anti-colonial and anti-racist struggles are connected. The original's anti-apartheid plot takes that seriously as campus politics.
  4. Divestment as a social movement tactic (the 1980s US anti-apartheid campaign): a demand aimed at institutions — universities, pension funds, cities — to withdraw money. It is unusually good for teaching trade-offs, because the cost lands on named people.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 6 in. Two students present memory chains. Then: what did this show do with a memory it did not explain?
10–25 The pairing. Lay out the 1990 dilemma in its exact terms on the board: principle, funding, a named student, an institution with investments. Do the same for the 2026 argument
25–40 Diaspora vs descent. Ask three students — no more — to state what "African" has meant in their own secondary-school curriculum, and keep the answers structural, not confessional
40–55 The uncomfortable question. Do not let the room resolve the 1990 episode by making the principled choice costless. Someone has to say what the boycott would have done to Kim's scholarship. In 1990 the show made Whitley choose the friend
55–68 Movement tactics across time. Divestment then; what are the equivalent campus demands now (endowment, policing, labour, tuition)? Students must name the mechanism, and the party that pays
68–75 Assign homework 7. Preview Session 8 and note that the midterm is released after it

Discussion questions

  1. "Black" is doing two jobs in this episode: a description of a shared condition and a claim about a shared culture. Which job does the plot need?
  2. The 1990 episode makes a Black student's scholarship the price of an anti-apartheid demand. Is that a dilemma or a trap — and who set it?
  3. What does an institution's investment portfolio say that its mission statement does not?
  4. If diaspora is a relationship rather than a fact, what makes some accounts of it powerful and others marginal?
  5. Which is more sociologically useful here: the students' disagreement, or the institution's response to it?

Instructor notes

If the room mishears this as an African-American-versus-African argument. Correct the frame early: the session is not about which group is right, it is about what work the category "Black" is being asked to do in an American institution, and who is written out when it is defined tightly.

If a student asserts a strong claim about a whole continent or a whole diaspora. Ask for the mechanism and the source. Then take the sharpest version of their claim and see whether the episode supports it. Almost always the text is more careful than the room — that is the lesson.

If the conversation becomes contemporary international politics. Let it run, then anchor: this course follows the franchise, and the franchise's version of this question comes from 1990's anti- apartheid movement. What does it mean that a network sitcom in 1990 assumed its audience would follow a divestment debate?

Historical care. Do not date the anti-apartheid movement as "over". Its US campus phase has a precise context (the divestment campaigns of the 1980s, and the 1986 sanctions legislation), and the original's episode sits inside that period. If you put dates on the board, keep them minimal and accurate; the point is that the show's writers treated their audience as politically literate.

Content note. Discussion of apartheid and state violence against Black South Africans. No graphic depiction on screen. Flag it and move on.

Session 8 — Pageants, Colourism, and Hierarchy Inside Black Space

Big question. How does stratification work inside a group that is itself stratified against?

Where we are. Session 7 argued about who belongs to the diaspora. Today the same episode's second half puts a student on a stage to be judged, and the original supplies the definitive version of that story from February 1988 — which is also the origin story of its most complicated character.

The midterm is released after this session.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 7, Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You — second half: the pageant 2026-09-24 Hazel competes for Miss Gilbert Hall, giving her a chance to establish herself on campus
Original S1E17, Mr. Hillman 1988-02-25 Whitley enters the Miss Hillman pageant to win her mother's approval; a fellow student enters in protest at the pageant's elitism and is mocked by a fraternity
Original (clip) S1E1, Reconcilable Differences — Whitley's first scene 1987-09-24 Whitley introduces herself by her family's ownership of the dormitory: status as the opening joke

Homework 7 is due at the start of this session.

Sociological frame

  1. Colourism (Margaret Hunter, Race, Gender, and the Politics of Skin Tone, 2005): stratification within racialised groups along skin tone, hair texture and related features — with documented consequences for income, marriage, and perceived competence.
  2. The politics of respectability (Evelyn Brooks Higginbotham, 1993): a politics in which uplift requires the group to be seen as respectable, and which therefore polices Black behaviour, dress, speech and bodies. A pageant is that politics with a stage and a crown.
  3. Display and the gendered division of reputation. Men in these episodes acquire status by doing; women are placed where they can be looked at. The original makes this its subject by having a man enter the pageant — the joke is the structural reversal.
  4. Intraracial class. Whitley's family owns the dormitory named after them; Deborah's family are Hillman royalty; other characters work. The campus is not a class-free zone, and the franchise has always known it.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 7 in. Two students give the two accounts of "Black" they reconstructed — and say which one the text favours
10–25 The pairing. Describe both pageants. On the board: who is judging, on what criteria, and who pays the entry fee (literally and socially)
25–40 Colourism and respectability. Define both, then find them on screen: casting, costuming, the language of evaluation, who is described as beautiful
40–55 The reversal. In 1988 a man enters the pageant in protest and the fraternity that mocks him is the episode's real villain. In 2026 a woman competes for a title named after a family. What does each version's humour assume about its audience?
55–68 Is the pageant an institution or an embarrassment? Take a vote, then make each side defend it sociologically. This is the term's best rehearsal for the midterm's evaluation question
68–75 Explain the midterm (released now, due before Session 10), take questions, and set expectations for the write-up

Discussion questions

  1. A crown is a judgment. What does it judge — the person, the family, or the institution?
  2. Whitley enters to win her mother's approval. Where does a standard come from when the people enforcing it are also its subjects?
  3. The 1988 episode has a student protest the pageant from inside it. Is that co-optation or subversion?
  4. Does respectability politics protect a group or discipline it? Which does this text show?
  5. If colourism operates inside Black institutions, whose interests does it serve?

Instructor notes

This is the session most likely to get personal. Colourism and hair are embodied subjects, and students may have direct experience of them. Set the norm at the top: we analyse the mechanism, nobody is asked to testify, and no one's appearance is ever the topic. If a student offers their own experience, thank them and immediately convert it to structure: what system produced that, and who benefits from it?

If the room wants to declare the pageant simply sexist. Hold both readings: the event is undeniably gendered display, and it is also a Black institution's route to recognition and scholarship money inside a society that provides few others. That tension is the session.

If a white student says something clumsy. Do not let the room punish them into silence; get the claim restated precisely, answer the precise version, and move on. A seminar that cannot survive clumsy speech cannot teach anything.

Use the casting as evidence. Students can examine the on-screen representation of skin tone and hair in both eras. Keep it descriptive and comparative rather than evaluative; the point is patterned difference, not individual judgment.

Midterm release mechanics. Hand out the prompt at the end of class, in writing, with the due date on it. Take no questions about the midterm in the last five minutes — the room needs to leave with the document, not a paraphrase.

Content note. Body image, colourism, and gendered evaluation are discussed. Flag it plainly at the start so nobody is ambushed.

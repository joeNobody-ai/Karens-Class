SOC 2200 — Race and Ethnicity in American Society

2000-level survey · 15 modules · 75 minutes · no prerequisites (college-level reading and writing expected)

1. Catalogue description

This course is a survey of race and ethnicity in American society, taught through one television case: A Different World, the 1987–1993 NBC sitcom set at the fictional Hillman College and its 2026 Netflix revival. Race is not a biological fact but a social construction with real, material consequences — and the cleanest way to see both halves of that sentence at once is to watch a community make, inhabit, and contest racial categories on screen across two eras. Each module pairs one core concept (racial formation, prejudice, discrimination, institutional racism, intersectionality, colourism, the racial wealth gap, representation, mass incarceration) with a screened scene, and asks students to move from what they saw to what the sociology can claim. The course screens scenes, not seasons; it treats the show's published record as evidence to be handled with care; and it trains students to keep documented facts, interpretations, and hypotheses visibly separate — a discipline they carry into every reading of every statistic they will ever meet.

2. Learning outcomes

Code Outcome
LO1 Explain race as a social construction with real, material consequences
LO2 Distinguish race, ethnicity, prejudice, discrimination, and racism
LO3 Apply racial formation theory to a concrete case
LO4 Analyse how racial categories are made, inhabited, and contested
LO5 Evaluate empirical claims about racial inequality with primary data
LO6 Connect a specific case of racial dynamics to general sociological theory

3. How the course works

One case, fifteen concepts. The whole term returns to a single case — A Different World — and moves fifteen concepts across it. A survey course trades depth for breadth, so the discipline is this: each week you leave with one concept you can actually use, not a list of names.

Screen scenes, not seasons. We watch short, targeted scenes (never whole seasons) so the television works as a specimen under a microscope, not as a substitute for the readings.

The evidence rule. Everything in this course keeps three kinds of claim apart: (1) documented — an episode title, air date, credit, or published summary, which anyone can look up; (2) interpretation — your reading of what a scene argues, labelled as a reading; (3) hypothesis — a claim on the table for testing. We never invent dialogue or quotes, and we never pass a reading off as a fact.

The reference site. The screened scenes and their documented summaries live at https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/, which cites its own sources. It is the course's shared evidence base.

6. Policies

Content note. The material includes discussion of racism, police violence, the legacy of slavery, and health inequality. We handle it as evidence, not as spectacle; you may step out or flag a topic at any time with no penalty and no explanation required.

Discussion norms. No one is a spokesperson for their group, and no one is a consultant on their own language. Engage the strongest version of a view you disagree with. Silence is allowed; airtime is not the currency of credit.

AI policy. AI may be used to brainstorm or check grammar, but every claim you submit must be yours, sourced, and marked as your own work. Fabricated or hallucinated sources are an academic-integrity violation regardless of how they were produced.

Accessibility. Accommodations are honoured without a fight. Materials are provided in text form and screened with captions; ask for what you need.

Late work. Homework is due at the start of the module it is assigned for. Two lowest homework scores are dropped. Extensions are granted when asked in advance, not after the deadline.

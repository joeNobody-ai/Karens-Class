Module 11 — Race and Health

Big question. Why does health follow the color line — even when access is equal?

Where we start. Final Words interrupts the usual finals panic with a more serious crisis — one the published summary deliberately does not name — and that reframes what matters. The module uses that quiet, reframing moment to ask a harder question than "who has insurance": why do health outcomes track race even when the door to care is held open? If I Should Die Before I Wake (1991) asked the same question about a life cut short on screen decades earlier.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 5, Final Words 2026 A serious crisis interrupts finals and reframes what matters — a window onto health as more than medicine.
Original (1991) S4E92, If I Should Die Before I Wake 1991-04-11 The throwback that put mortality and grief at the center of a sitcom, decades ahead of its time.
Optional clip The moment the crisis lands amid the finals panic — To watch the frame shift from grades to something larger.

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Health disparities: systematic differences in health outcomes and access between groups, not explained by biology or individual choices alone.
  2. Structural racism: racial inequality produced by the interlocking operation of institutions and histories, so that no single actor owns the outcome — here, as it writes itself into the body.
  3. Stigma (Goffman, Stigma, 1963): a mark that discredits a person and changes how they are treated — including the stigma that keeps people from seeking care.
  4. Social determinants of health: the conditions in which people are born, live, work, and age — housing, work, stress, exposure — that shape health more than the clinic does.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and screen. The crisis in Final Words, then the mortality moment in the 1991 original. Facts first.
8–22 Health follows the color line. Lecture: disparities persist across education and income, so access alone cannot explain them.
22–36 Social determinants, not just medicine. Introduce the conditions — stress, exposure, care, environment — that write inequality into the body.
36–50 Stigma as a barrier. Use Goffman to ask how a mark (of illness, of a group) changes who seeks help and who gets it.
50–63 The crisis reframes. In groups, ask what the scene's unnamed crisis replaces — and what that shift says about what "matters" is socially ranked.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 11 (one health disparity, by the numbers).

Discussion questions

  1. Why do health disparities persist even when access to care is equalised? Name the mechanism.
  2. What is a social determinant the scene points to without naming it?
  3. How does stigma operate as a social barrier to care, not just a feeling?
  4. What does the crisis's interruption of "finals panic" argue about which worries are allowed to matter?
  5. What data would you need to show that a disparity is structural rather than individual?

Instructor notes

If a student says "it's just lifestyle choices." Push to the determinants: choices are made in conditions people did not choose. Ask what the scene shows about the conditions, not the choices.

If the unnamed crisis is a real-world event students recognise. Keep the discussion on the documented summary (what the published summary states) and the sociology, not on reconstructing details the summary does not give.

Key terms

health disparities · structural racism · stigma · social determinants · access

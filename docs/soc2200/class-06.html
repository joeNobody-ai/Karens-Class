Module 6 — Colourism and Intraracial Stratification

Big question. How does a hierarchy form inside a group that is itself stratified against?

Where we start. The second half of Ep 7 turns on Hazel's run for Miss Gilbert Hall — a pageant, a beauty standard, and a ranking of bodies inside one community. Colourism is the reminder that stratification never stops at the group boundary: it runs inside the group too. Mr. Hillman (1988) shows the same machinery of status and respectability at work decades earlier.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 7, Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You (second half) 2026 A pageant run and its winner turn a community's beauty standard into a ranking of its own members.
Original (1988) S1E17, Mr. Hillman 1988-02-25 The throwback that treats status, presentation, and respectability inside the campus.

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Colourism (Hunter, Race, Gender, and the Politics of Skin Tone, 2005): a hierarchy that ranks people within a racial group by skin tone, usually privileging lighter tones.
  2. Skin tone: the visible gradient of pigmentation that colourism turns into a status marker.
  3. Intraracial stratification: inequality that operates within a racial group, layered on top of the inequality the group faces from outside.
  4. The politics of respectability (Higginbotham, Righteous Discontent, 1993): the strategy of policing one's own group's presentation and behaviour to win acceptance from a hostile larger society.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and screen. The second half of Ep 7: the pageant and its judgment. Facts first.
8–22 Colourism defined. Lecture the tone hierarchy, and stress that it runs inside a group already stratified against.
22–36 Skin tone as social fact. Trace how a physical gradient becomes a social ranking: who sets the standard, who enforces it, who pays.
36–50 Respectability politics. Introduce Higginbotham and ask: is the pageant a bid for respect, or a policing of it?
50–63 Two eras of the same ranking. Place Mr. Hillman (1988) beside the 2026 scene and ask what changed in how the standard is enforced.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 6 (map one standard inside a group).

Discussion questions

  1. Who sets the beauty standard in the pageant, and who is measured against it?
  2. Is colourism a form of racism, or a distinct thing? Defend your answer.
  3. What does the politics of respectability promise its practitioners — and what does it cost?
  4. Why does a hierarchy inside a subordinated group often feel more painful than the one from outside?
  5. What evidence would tell you whether the tone hierarchy is weakening or persisting?

Instructor notes

If a student says "we don't do that anymore." Do not correct from authority; ask what documented fact about the scene shows the standard being enforced, then let them test whether the mechanism is period-specific or still running.

If the discussion becomes about particular students' bodies. Shut it down and redirect to the scene: the course analyses standards, never the bodies in the room.

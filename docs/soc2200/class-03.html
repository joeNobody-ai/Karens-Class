Module 3 — Prejudice, Discrimination, and Racism

Big question. What is the difference between what people feel and what institutions do?

Where we start. Deborah speaks her mind in public and is called out for it — an individual act with individual consequences. But the module's whole point is that the three terms in its title are not the same thing, and the most consequential one may be the one no single person intends. We pair the 2026 speech with the 1992 original that placed a honeymoon against the Los Angeles uprising.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 2, O Captain! My Captain! 2026 A student speaks in public and pays a price — an entry point into what is felt versus what is done.
Original (1992) S6E120, Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) 1992-09-24 A sitcom episode set against the 1992 Los Angeles uprising; a window onto discrimination at the scale of institutions and a city.
Optional clip Deborah's public speech and its aftermath — To isolate the moment where a feeling becomes a consequence.

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Prejudice: a pre-judgment — an attitude or feeling, usually negative, held about a group before or despite evidence.
  2. Discrimination: an action — treating people unequally on the basis of group membership.
  3. Institutional racism: racial inequality that is built into the routine practices of an organisation, regardless of any individual's intent.
  4. Structural racism: racial inequality produced by the interlocking operation of many institutions and histories at once, so that no single actor owns the outcome.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and screen. Deborah's public speech; then a scene or two from Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1). Ask what happened, factually.
8–22 Three definitions, one table. Prejudice (feelings), discrimination (acts), racism (systems). Put each on the board and force every example into a column.
22–36 Intent vs. outcome. Lecture the institutional turn: how a facially neutral rule can produce a racial result no one intended.
36–50 The speech under three lenses. In groups, read Deborah's moment once as prejudice, once as discrimination, once as institutional — and say which reading the scene best supports.
50–63 The city scale. Use the 1992 setting to show the difference between one person's attitude and a whole city's institutions.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 3 (feelings vs. institutions, on a real case).

Discussion questions

  1. Where in the screened scene does a feeling become an action? Name the exact turn.
  2. Can there be discrimination without prejudice? Institutional racism without either? Give a mechanism.
  3. Why does "I didn't intend to be racist" not settle the question of institutional racism?
  4. What does the 1992 Los Angeles setting let the show say that a campus scene cannot?
  5. Which of the three terms is hardest to prove, and what evidence would it take?

Instructor notes

If a student collapses the three terms ("it's all just racism"). That is the survey's central temptation. Insist on the columns: a claim must say whether it is about a feeling, an act, or a system, and the rest of the course depends on the distinction.

If discussion turns to "are these characters racist?" Gently refuse the character-verdict game; the module is not grading hearts, it is tracing mechanisms. Ask "what is the institution doing here?"

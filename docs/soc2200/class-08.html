Module 8 — Race and Gender

Big question. Why does race change how gender is done — and vice versa?

Where we start. We revisit the Ep 7 pageant, now read through gender: the standard being enforced on Hazel is not just a beauty standard but a gendered and raced one — Black womanhood performed for judgment. Mr. Hillman (1988) gives us the same intersection a generation earlier. The module's central concept, intersectionality, says these are not two things added together but one fused thing.

Sociological frame

  1. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989): the framework holding that race, gender, class, and other categories do not operate separately but interlock, producing distinct experiences of advantage and harm.
  2. Race and gender: two systems of social organisation that are mutually constituting — each shapes how the other is lived and judged.
  3. Black feminist thought (Collins, Black Feminist Thought, 1990): a body of theory built from Black women's experience that treats knowledge as situated and insists on analysing race and gender together.
  4. Controlling images (Collins): stereotypes — the mammy, the matriarch, the jezebel, the sapphire — that discipline Black women by fixing what they are allowed to be.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and re-screen. The Ep 7 pageant, now with the question: what standard is being enforced, and on whom?
8–22 Intersectionality, defined. Lecture Crenshaw's traffic-metaphor argument and why "race + gender" is the wrong formula.
22–36 Controlling images. Introduce Collins's images and ask which ones the pageant's standard summons or resists.
36–50 One fused standard. In groups, show that the standard applied to Hazel is neither purely racial nor purely gendered, and prove it from the scene.
50–63 Two eras, one intersection. Set Mr. Hillman (1988) beside the 2026 pageant and ask what changed about how Black womanhood is judged.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 8 (intersectionality in one scene).

Discussion questions

  1. What does the pageant standard demand of Black women that it does not demand of Black men?
  2. Why is "race plus gender" the wrong formula for what the scene shows?
  3. Which controlling image, if any, does the pageant summon — and how does a contestant resist it?
  4. How does the raced standard change the gendered standard (or vice versa)?
  5. What does Black feminist thought let you see in the scene that a single-axis reading would miss?

Instructor notes

If a student says "we've covered gender before — this is just race again." That is the single-axis reflex. Ask them to name one thing the standard demands that is both at once, and hold them to the "fused, not added" test.

If the controlling-images material draws discomfort. Keep it in the third person: these are historical images applied to a fictional scene, analysed, not re-performed. Number them on slides per the vocabulary protocol.

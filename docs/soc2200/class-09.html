Module 9 — Race and Media Representation

Big question. Who controls the image of a race — and what does the image do?

Where we start. We revisit Deborah's public speech in Ep 2, now read as a problem of representation: who gets to speak, whose image circulates, and what it costs to be seen. The module zooms out to the machine that produces the images — a sitcom about a Black college is itself a representation, made by specific people for a specific audience. The Show Must Go On (1988) lets us watch the original series reflect on its own making.

Sociological frame

  1. Representation (Hall, ed., Representation, 1997): the production of meaning through images, language, and signs — not a mirror of reality but a way of making it.
  2. Encoding and decoding (Hall, "Encoding and Decoding," 1973): producers encode meaning into a text, but audiences decode it in their own ways — dominant, negotiated, or oppositional.
  3. The gaze (hooks, Black Looks, 1992): the power relation built into who is allowed to look at whom, and on what terms — including the "oppositional gaze" that looks back.
  4. Stereotypes: simplified, repeated images of a group that reduce its members to a few fixed traits, and that circulate as if they were knowledge.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and re-screen. Deborah's speech and the reaction, now with the question: whose image is at stake?
8–22 Representation makes meaning. Lecture Hall: images do not reflect a race; they help construct what it is understood to be.
22–36 Encoding / decoding. Walk the circuit: what the producers encode, and the three ways an audience can decode it.
36–50 The gaze, on screen and off. Introduce hooks's oppositional gaze and ask who looks, and who looks back, in the scene.
50–63 The show watching itself. Use The Show Must Go On to ask who controlled the image of Black life in 1988 — and who controls it in 2026.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 9 (decode one image).

Discussion questions

  1. Who controls Deborah's image after she speaks — and what does that control do?
  2. Encode/decode the speech: what is the likely dominant reading, and what is an oppositional one?
  3. Where in the scene can you find the gaze — who is allowed to look at whom?
  4. What is a stereotype for? What work does it do for the people who circulate it?
  5. A sitcom about a Black college is itself a representation. Who made it, and what did they encode?

Instructor notes

If a student says "it's just a TV show, not reality." Agree — and then do the sociology: the point is that "just a TV show" is itself a meaning-making machine. Ask what the show produces, not whether it reflects.

If the room fixates on whether a stereotype is "true." Reframe: stereotypes are not evaluated by accuracy but by function — what they let people do (dismiss, control, flatten). Ask the function.

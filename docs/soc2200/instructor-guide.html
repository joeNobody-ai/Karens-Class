Instructor Guide — SOC 2200

This is the working guide for whoever teaches Race and Ethnicity in American Society. It assumes you know the sociology and need to know the course — how it is built, where it can go wrong, and what to do when the room gets difficult.

Why this course works (and what it is not)

A survey of race and ethnicity is usually taught with a textbook and a list of -isms. This course teaches the same syllabus through one object — A Different World, the 1987–1993 sitcom and its 2026 revival — because the show is a racial formation machine: it made a campus, a public, a set of categories, and then got contested about all of them. One object, fifteen concepts, two eras. The television is the specimen, not the syllabus.

What this course is not: it is not a media-studies course, not a fan course, and not a Black-history course. It is a sociology course that happens to screen television. Every minute of screen time is in service of a concept, and every concept is in service of a real sociological claim. If a session ever becomes "let's talk about the episode," you have drifted.

Before the term starts

  1. Read the contract. data/soc2200_catalog.json is the source of truth for all fifteen modules. Every module file is machine-checked against it. Do not invent episode titles, air dates, or anchors — they are fixed and cited.
  2. Read lingo.md, rubrics.md, and rights.md in the course directory. They are not decorative; the vocabulary protocol and the rubrics are load-bearing.
  3. Prepare the screens. Clip the exact scenes each module names, with captions on. Screened scenes are shorter than you think they should be — aim for two to five minutes each.
  4. Line up the data. Modules 3, 4, 7, 10, 11, and 13 ask students to handle real numbers. Check each figure's source and year before you teach it; the course cites kinds of sources, and the current number is a "check before you teach" item, not a fixed constant.
  5. Set the norm on day one. The vocabulary protocol (below) is stated on the first day and never relaxed. It is easier to set a norm than to restore one.

The evidence rule (the three tiers)

The course's core value is that documented facts, interpretations, and hypotheses never blur. State the table on day one and enforce it every week:

Tier What it is How it is handled
Documented An episode title, air date, credit, or published summary Stated plainly, with the source named
Interpretation Your reading of what a scene or season argues Labelled as a reading: "my reading is…", "the scene argues…"
Hypothesis A claim on the table for testing Framed as testable: "one hypothesis is…", "we could check this by…"

The rule that follows from the table: never invent dialogue, quotes, plot beats, or statistics. You may describe a scene using the published summary and mark it "the published summary states." If you are unsure whether a detail is documented, say so and stick to what is given.

The hard conversations

This course will, by design, reach racism, police violence, the Moynihan debate, colourism, and anti-Blackness. The preparation is not to avoid these; it is to run them as a sociologist would.

  1. The vocabulary protocol. Two rules, stated on day one and never relaxed: the instructor does not perform the vernacular, and no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term. Definitions come from the cited sources (see lingo.md), the "handle with care" terms are numbered on slides and discussed by number, and nobody in the room is ever the consultant on their own language. This is a protocol, not a preference: it protects students from being conscripted as native informants and keeps the discussion on evidence rather than performance.

  2. No spokespeople. No student represents their race, gender, or background. If a question would make a student a sample of one, it is the wrong question. Redirect to the text: "what does the scene show us, rather than what you as a person know?"

  3. Disagreement is about claims, not identities. A student may attack a reading with evidence; they may not attack the reader. Model this by restating the strongest version of a view you disagree with before answering it.

  4. Silence is data. Do not fill every pause. A room that can sit with a hard scene is doing more sociology than a room that performs certainty.

  5. Step-outs. Anyone may leave or flag a topic with no explanation and no penalty. Say this out loud early and honour it without comment.

Running the room on screened scenes

  • Screen before lecture, not after. The scene is the data; the concept names what they already saw.
  • Ask "what did you see?" before "what does it mean?" — fact first, interpretation second, every time. This is the evidence rule rehearsed aloud.
  • When a student asserts a population claim from a sample of one, or states an interpretation as fact, use the rubrics' automatic rewrite request: return it once, ungraded, with the rewrite condition named. Do not let it pass silently; it will spread.
  • Keep the clock. The module's 75-minute table is designed so the close lands on the homework assignment. Running long on discussion is the most common failure mode.

Adaptation notes

  • Trim breadth, never the evidence rule. If you must cut, cut a concept, not the discipline of separating fact from reading.
  • The data exercises (Modules 7 and 11) need current numbers. Update the figures the week you teach them; the assignment asks students to find their own, so yours is a model, not an answer key.
  • Revisit modules. Modules 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 13 re-screen earlier scenes. If students have not seen the first screening, run the original clip first; the revisit only works against a memory.
  • Small sections. In a seminar of under ten, collapse the discussion segments and lengthen the screening; the concept still lands.
  • Remote sections. Screen the clip asynchronously and spend live time on the discussion questions only; the 75-minute table becomes a 50-minute conversation plus a pre-watched scene.

Module 13 — Race, Crime, and Justice

Big question. How did a family sitcom put an uprising on prime time — and where is that tradition now?

Where we start. We revisit Ep 9, now read through the Rodney King precedent the reference site names: a sitcom about a Black college carrying the memory of the 1992 uprising and the verdict that followed. Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) (1992) is the original that did exactly that — put a honeymoon against the backdrop of a city in revolt. The module asks how criminalization and mass incarceration became a "racial state" project.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 9, Romancing the Unknown (revisit — the Rodney King precedent) 2026 The budget-crisis episode, re-screened, now read against the 1992 precedent it carries.
Original (1992) S6E120, Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) 1992-09-24 The sitcom that placed a honeymoon inside the 1992 Los Angeles uprising.
Optional clip The show's reference to the 1992 events — To see a comedy hold a crisis in the same frame as everyday life.

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Criminalization: the process by which behaviours and populations come to be defined and treated as criminal, rather than as social problems needing other responses.
  2. Policing: the organised enforcement of order that does not apply itself evenly — it concentrates on some places and people more than others.
  3. Mass incarceration (Alexander, The New Jim Crow, 2010): the extraordinary post-1970s expansion of imprisonment, concentrated on Black Americans, operating as a system of racial control.
  4. The racial state (Goldberg, 2002): the argument that the state's law-and-order institutions are structured by race, not merely occasionally biased.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and re-screen. Ep 9's crisis and the 1992 original's opening. Facts first.
8–22 From crime to criminalization. Lecture the shift: the question is not "who commits crime" but "who is made a criminal."
22–36 Policing is uneven. Show how enforcement concentrates on some places and bodies, regardless of offence rates.
36–50 Mass incarceration as a system. Introduce Alexander and the claim that the system works as racial control even without overt racists.
50–63 The sitcom's nerve. In groups, ask what it does for a family sitcom to carry a revolt on screen — and whether the 2026 revival still has that nerve.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 13 (one criminalization mechanism).

Discussion questions

  1. What is the difference between "crime" and "criminalization"? Which does the scene show?
  2. How does policing produce a racial pattern even when officers act without bias?
  3. Why does Alexander call mass incarceration a system of control rather than a response to crime?
  4. What did the 1992 original risk by putting an uprising in a comedy — and what did it gain?
  5. Does the 2026 revival carry the same nerve? What evidence from the screened scene?

Instructor notes

If a student wants to litigate the 1992 events. Keep the focus on what the documented summary of the episode states and on the sociology; do not let the room reconstruct a legal record the course cannot verify.

If the material is overwhelming. Honour the step-out norm without comment, and return to the concepts — criminalization, policing, mass incarceration — which can be analysed at arm's length.

Module 12 — Race and Family

Big question. How does race shape family — and who does the work of holding one together?

Where we start. In Insomnia and the Daydreamer, "the aunties" — the original cast — arrive, and the family is shown not as a nuclear unit but as a network of people who show up. The module reads that arrival sociologically: family is not one form but many, and in Black communities a great deal of the holding-together is done by people who are not the parents. Whitley's Last Supper (1990) already put the family — and its class tensions — at the table.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 6, Insomnia and the Daydreamer 2026 The aunties arrive and the family appears as a network, not a nuclear unit.
Original (1990) S4E73, Whitley's Last Supper 1990-10-11 The throwback that seats the family — and its class strains — at one table.
Optional clip The aunties' arrival and reunion — To see kin who are not parents doing the work of family.

Reference site for both, with sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/

Sociological frame

  1. Family forms: the many actual shapes families take — nuclear, extended, single-parent, chosen — against a culture that treats one of them as "normal."
  2. Othermothering (Collins, Black Feminist Thought, 1990): the practice, documented especially in Black communities, of women mothering children who are not biologically their own.
  3. Kinship: the social ties of relatedness — by blood, marriage, or choice — that organise care, obligation, and belonging.
  4. The Moynihan debate: the 1965 report that blamed Black family structure for Black poverty, and the long scholarly fight over whether it diagnosed pathology or pathologised a people.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and screen. The aunties' arrival in Ep 6, then the dinner table in Whitley's Last Supper.
8–22 One normal family? Lecture the gap between the normative nuclear family and the actual forms people build.
22–36 Othermothering and kinship. Introduce Collins and the idea that care is a network, not a single role.
36–50 The Moynihan debate. Present the 1965 report and its critics: is the "structure" the problem, or the story told about it?
50–63 Who holds it together? In groups, name who does the work of family in the scene, and whether the nuclear model can see it.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 12 (care work in one family network).

Discussion questions

  1. What does "the aunties" arrival show that a nuclear-family lens would miss?
  2. Is othermothering a deficit to be explained or a resource to be understood? How does the scene weigh in?
  3. What is kinship for in the scene — and who is obligated to whom?
  4. How does the Moynihan debate turn a description into a diagnosis? Where is the line?
  5. If family is a network of care, what happens when the network is strained?

Instructor notes

If a student invokes "broken families" as an explanation. Pause and flag the term as a claim, not a fact; redirect to the evidence rule and ask what the scene documents about who provides care.

If the Moynihan material gets tense. Present the report and its critics symmetrically, as a debate about stories told about a people, and keep the discussion on mechanisms, not moral verdicts.

Key terms

family forms · othermothering · kinship · the Moynihan debate · care

Module 4 — Racial Formation and the State

Big question. How does the state make race through law and policy?

Where we start. A budget crisis hits Hillman, students organise, and the institution that is supposed to be neutral is revealed as an actor with its own interests — interests that fall unequally on different students. The module's claim is that the state is not a referee standing outside race; it is one of the main places race is made. Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1), set against the 1992 uprising, shows the state acting on race at full force.

Before class: viewing

Sociological frame

  1. The state: the set of institutions — law, courts, police, agencies, budgets — that hold a monopoly on legitimate rule over a territory and population.
  2. Law: state-made rules that classify people into categories with different rights and duties.
  3. Policy: routine decisions and allocations (who is funded, who is not) that distribute advantage without ever naming race.
  4. The racial state (Goldberg, The Racial State, 2002): the argument that modern states do not merely respond to race but are structured by it — that racial categories are built into their design.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and screen. The budget crisis and the student response in Ep 9; a scene from the 1992 original. Facts first.
8–22 The state is not neutral. Lecture: law, policy, and budget as machines that sort people without ever saying the word "race."
22–36 Goldberg's racial state. Introduce the idea that the state is built on racial categories, not a neutral umpire over them.
36–50 Read the budget as racial. In groups, treat Hillman's budget decision as a racial project: what does it link, and who does it cost?
50–63 Organising as counter-formation. Students organise — read that as a competing racial project aimed back at the state.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 4 (find the state inside one real policy).

Discussion questions

  1. What makes a budget a racial document even when race is never mentioned?
  2. How does the state make a category "official" in a way that everyday talk cannot?
  3. In the scene, is the college acting as a neutral administrator or as a racial actor? What evidence?
  4. What does student organising do to the state's claim to be above the fray?
  5. If the state is structured by race, what would a non-racial state have to look like?

Instructor notes

If a student says "this is just about money, not race." Accept the challenge and run it: ask them to trace who loses when the budget is cut, and whether the pattern is colour-blind or colour-laden. The point is to make them do the tracing, not to pre-empt it.

If the 1992 material overwhelms the room. Slow down; separate the documented facts (what the episode's published summary states) from the interpretation, and let students take the step-out offered in the syllabus with no comment.

Module 5 — Immigration and the Racial Order

Big question. How does migration remake the boundaries of race and belonging?

Where we start. We revisit the Ep 7 diaspora argument, now read through migration: who is an immigrant, who is a native, and who gets to count as belonging are questions a single scene can complicate in seconds. A World Alike (1990) staged the same tangle a generation earlier, when the "African versus African-American" line was being drawn in a different America.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 7, Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You (revisit — the diaspora argument) 2026 The same scene, re-screened, now read as a story about migration and belonging.
Original (1990) S3E60, A World Alike 1990-02-15 The throwback that first staged who counts as "from here."

Sociological frame

  1. Immigration: the movement of people across borders, which always carries a question about who is owed membership in the receiving society.
  2. Assimilation (Gordon, Assimilation in American Life, 1964): the process by which newcomers are expected to adopt the receiving society's culture and, in the classic model, disappear into it.
  3. Diaspora: a people dispersed from a homeland who maintain a sense of connection to it across generations and borders.
  4. Racialized nativism: the fusion of racism and anti-immigrant hostility, by which "outsiders" are defined against a racial image of who the "real" members are.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Settle and re-screen. The Ep 7 diaspora argument, now with one question: who is treated as foreign?
8–22 Immigration as a sorting machine. Lecture: borders do not just move bodies; they sort people into membership and non-membership.
22–36 Assimilation and its critics. Present Gordon's model, then ask whether the scene imagines assimilation as the price of belonging or the erasure of it.
36–50 Diaspora vs. nation. Small groups map the scene's claims: who speaks for the homeland, and who speaks for "here"?
50–63 Racialized nativism, applied. Ask where in the argument an "outsider" is being defined against a racial image of the insider.
63–75 Close and assign. Restate the big question; assign Homework 5 (map one migration story).

Discussion questions

  1. In the scene, who is positioned as the immigrant — and by whom?
  2. Is assimilation offered as belonging or demanded as erasure? What does the scene show?
  3. What does diaspora give people that the nation-state's categories cannot?
  4. How does nativism borrow the language of "who belongs" and racialise it?
  5. If migration keeps remaking the racial order, why does that order feel so stable?

Instructor notes

If students treat "African" and "African-American" as natural categories. Push the construction point: both are made categories with histories; the scene is the making, not the reporting.

If the room splits along identity lines. Re-invoke norm 1 (no spokespeople) and redirect to the text — ask what the scene treats as authentic, not what anyone in the room is.

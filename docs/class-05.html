Session 5 — Crisis, Care, and the Sociology of Health

Big question. Why did a Black sitcom put an HIV diagnosis on prime time in 1991 — and what does a crisis mean on television now?

Where we are. Sessions 1–4 built a picture of campus life: arrival, conflict, ritual, intimacy. Today the season's fifth episode interrupts all of it with a serious crisis during finals, and we put that episode next to the most important hour in this franchise's history.

This is the heaviest session in the course. Read the notes below before you teach it.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 5, Final Words 2026-09-24 Finals panic, then a serious crisis reframes what everyone has been worrying about
Original S4E92, If I Should Die Before I Wake 1991-04-11 A speech-class assignment leads Josie to disclose that she is HIV-positive; the class has to re-examine what it believed about AIDS. Guest cast included Tisha Campbell and Whoopi Goldberg

Watch the revival episode yourself before you teach it. The published summary does not name the crisis. Do not introduce it to the room as anything other than what it is on screen; if you have not watched it, screen it in class rather than assigning it blind.

Homework 4 is due at the start of this session.

Sociological frame

  1. Stigma (Erving Goffman, 1963): a spoiled identity, managed in interaction. The 1991 episode is essentially a study in what happens when a disclosure is made and a room has to decide how to respond.
  2. Structural violence (Paul Farmer, 2004; the term from Johan Galtung, 1969): harm that is built into arrangements rather than intended by individuals. The original's episode arrives in a period when Black Americans were being told, by neglect and by silence, that HIV was someone else's crisis.
  3. The sitcom as public health intervention. In 1991, prime time was where a mass audience could be reached, and Black audiences were disproportionately affected by an epidemic that mainstream coverage largely framed as white and gay. Cathy Cohen's The Boundaries of Blackness (1999) is the canonical sociological account of how that boundary was drawn and contested.
  4. Care as labour, again. Who does the caring when someone is ill, and what does that cost? Both episodes leave the practical work with friends and family, not institutions.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 4 in. Names on the board. Then a plain content notice: today involves HIV, illness and death, and the revival's crisis scene lands hard — students may step out
10–25 The pairing. Describe both crises factually. Then separate the room's responses into emotional, practical and institutional. Keep the columns visible for the rest of class
25–40 Goffman on stigma: what does each character do with the information they now have, and what is the cost of each option — silence, disclosure, reassurance, avoidance?
40–55 1991, historically. What a Black prime-time audience was being told about HIV at the time, and who was saying it (cite Cohen). Ask: what did this episode make sayable?
55–68 2026, comparatively. The revival uses a crisis the summary does not name. Ask students to say what they saw and what, structurally, a 2026 streaming audience gets instead of a network special episode
68–75 Assign homework 5 (which requires pulling a current figure from a primary source). Preview Session 6. Give the one-minute reflection: what will you do differently the next time someone discloses something hard

Discussion questions

  1. Why does disclosure restore a relationship in one text and endanger it in another?
  2. In 1991, a sitcom took on an epidemic. What did that make possible, and what did it leave out?
  3. If stigma is managed in interaction, who pays the cost of managing it — the person with the diagnosis, or the people around them?
  4. The revival places its crisis inside finals week. What does the juxtaposition argue about what matters?
  5. What can television do about a health crisis that a public health campaign cannot?

Instructor notes

Before you begin: set the room. Say what is coming, say that stepping out is fine and carries no penalty, and identify the counsellor or service you would send someone to. Offer a five-minute break mid-session if the pairing lands hard. Do not ask anyone to share personal experience; if a student volunteers something serious, acknowledge it, thank them for trusting the room, and move the analysis to the structural level rather than following the personal story.

If the room goes quiet after the HIV episode. That silence is the pedagogical object. Do not fill it. Ask: what did the class in the episode do with the information, and what are we doing with it right now? The parallel usually unlocks the discussion honestly.

If a student asserts something false about HIV transmission. Correct it plainly, without humiliation, and cite the current public health source rather than your authority. This is the one session where you should have the CDC's current HIV pages open on your laptop.

If the discussion slides from sociology into therapy. Name the difference and be kind about it: "We can analyse why care work falls on the people who have least of it — that's the sociological question. Whether a particular person is okay is not something the seminar can carry." Have the counselling referral ready to hand over after class.

If someone asks why the revival's crisis is unnamed in the materials. Do not improvise a specification. Say exactly this: the published summaries do not name it, this course refuses to assert content it has not verified, and what we discuss is what is on screen. That is the course's evidence rule applied to itself, and it is worth saying out loud in Session 5.

Data point for the board (verify before teaching, and again each term). Both Sessions 5 and 14 involve numbers about Black health and Black wealth. Any figure you write on the board needs its source, its year, and its denominator on the same line. Homework 5 exists to make students do that rather than trust a handout.

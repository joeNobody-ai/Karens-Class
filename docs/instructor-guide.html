Instructor guide

Everything here is for you, not for students. It assumes you have taught for a long time and have strong instincts — so it focuses on the three things that are specific to this course: the show, the evidence rules, and the conversations that go sideways.

1. Why this course works (and what it is not)

It works because the franchise supplies 39 years of paired texts about one institution, which makes it a natural experiment for the core questions of the field — and because the students have already internalised how to watch it. The course is not a media-appreciation seminar and not a representative portrait of Black life. It is a sociology course that uses two deliberate, commercial, partial texts as its case, with the limitations of those texts as an explicit topic (Session 12).

The course's one-sentence claim, which you should say out loud in Session 1 and again in Session 15: Black experience is not a topic adjacent to American society; it is one of the terrains on which American society is organised, and therefore one of the places where general sociological theory is tested. Everything in the syllabus is in service of that.

2. Before the term starts

  1. Check availability of the original series in your region (library, DVD sets, streaming). The syllabus assumes the 2026 season (10 episodes) is streamable and that the original is at least partially accessible. Each session names its essential original episode so you can screen a clip if access is uneven.
  2. Watch the revival's Episode 5 yourself before assigning it. Its published summary does not name the crisis it depicts. You cannot run that session blind, and the materials here deliberately do not tell you what it is.
  3. Verify the numbers. Every figure in these materials is written as a "check before you teach" item, with a source and a capture date. Re-check before you put anything on a board.
  4. Open the reference site (https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/) and click into two or three callback cards. If you understand its three evidence tiers, you understand the course's standard.
  5. Decide about the exam keys. They live in this repository (course/exams/*-key.md and the instructor/ pages of the built site). If your repository is public, note that trade-off explicitly.

Archives worth a browser tab

These three are the right places to send students hunting for primary material, but the course's automated link check cannot reach them from a server — they block automated clients (the Smithsonian's own homepage returns 403 to the same checker, which is how we know it is a bot filter and not a dead link). Verify each in your browser, then put them on a handout if they resolve:

  • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture — nmaahc.si.edu
  • Library of Congress, Civil Rights History Project — loc.gov/collections/civil-rights-history-project/ (loc.gov itself answers 200; only the collection path is filtered)
  • US Department of Education HBCU programme pages — sites.ed.gov/whhbcu/

The reference site's own sources page (course/sources.md) lists every link the checker could confirm, and separates them from these.

3. The evidence rule (teach it in Session 1, enforce it all term)

Tier Meaning Test
Documented Episode title, air date, director, writer, cast credit, published summary Can be looked up; the reference site cites its source
Interpretation A reading of what a scene or a season argues Labelled as a reading; you can disagree and still be right
Instructor hypothesis A claim offered for the room to test Stated as a guess, and it can be falsified

Students are graded on keeping these apart. When a student says "the show is arguing X", the standard reply is: is that documented, or is that you? Both are welcome; only confusion between them is penalised. This is also the fastest way to handle a confident wrong claim: "Let's sort that — documented or interpretation?"

4. The hard conversations

Norms to set in Session 1 and enforce every week.

  1. No spokesperson rule. Nobody speaks for a group. You will be asked directly, at least once, to justify your position without being asked to represent anyone.
  2. Text first. Claims must come from the episodes, the readings, or the data — not from general impressions.
  3. Mechanism over moral. "How", "by what rule", "who pays" beat "should".
  4. Nobody is a sample. Personal experience is welcome as a reason to ask a question, never as proof of a population claim.
  5. Clumsy speech is survivable. Restate the claim precisely, answer the precise version, move on. A seminar that punishes imprecision with exile teaches nothing.

Six situations and what to do.

Situation Move
"It's just a sitcom." Ask what the alternative source is for public knowledge of a Black campus, then move to the distribution-of-knowledge argument
The Cosby problem surfaces Do not resolve; teach it as the gap between an image and the institution that made it, and forecast Session 12
Confession instead of analysis "Bring me the general claim, not the case. What would have to be true of everybody's situation for yours to be typical?"
A student asserts a false empirical claim Correct it plainly, cite the current source, keep the person
The room goes silent after heavy material Do not fill the silence. Ask what the characters did with the information — and what we are doing now
Two camps harden (free speech / harm, culture / structure) Convert to a cost-allocation or mechanism question: "Who is paying, and who decided?"

Heavy sessions, flagged in advance. Session 5 (HIV, illness, death — the heaviest), Session 8 (colourism, body image, gendered judgment), Session 13 (the 1965 report and its uses), Session 14 (police violence, state violence, uprising). Announce each one in the previous session, name the campus service, and make stepping out costless.

5. Teaching with the reference site

The reference site is the course's shared evidence base. Three uses:

  1. Pre-class. Students arrive having seen the callback cards, so you can go straight to analysis instead of plot.
  2. In-class. When a student disputes a fact, send them to the card and its source. This saves ten minutes and teaches the habit.
  3. Homework. Every assignment links to specific cards (deep links, e.g. …/v2/ep09.html#ep09-rodney-king-precedent), and students are expected to check them.

Teach the site critically. It is a fan-made documentation project with explicit tiers, and it says so. Ask the room: what would make you distrust it? Their answers are a methods lesson.

6. Assessment mechanics

  • Homework (15, drop lowest 2). Grade quickly and in writing: one thing that worked, one thing to fix, and whether the evidence tier was handled honestly. Rubrics are in rubrics.md; they are deliberately short.
  • Midterm. Released after Session 8, due before Session 10, take-home. Covers Sessions 1–8. It is designed to be written in about four hours by a student who has done the work.
  • Final. Comprehensive, weighted to Sessions 9–15, with an applied question using unseen material. Format options are in exams/final.md — the in-class option keeps the term's rigor with less temptation to outsource.
  • Capstone memo. 1,200 words, public-facing, due Session 15, presented in 90 seconds. This is where LO5 gets assessed properly.
  • Participation. One session led by each student (sign up in week one), plus quality of contribution. The rubric rewards facilitation, not airtime.

7. Adapting the course

If you have Do this
10 weeks Run Sessions 1–11 as the spine and fold 12–15 into two extended sessions (media + family in one; state + capstone in the other)
15 weeks, more room Add a student-led session on an episode you assign, and a library session on archival sources for Session 13
A predominantly non-Black room Keep the norms; add the "how do you know what you know about this campus" opening in Session 1
A room with HBCU experience Formalise it: students document one difference between televised and actual campus practice, with a source, as an optional bonus for homework 3
No Netflix access for students Screen episodes in class (75 minutes covers one episode plus discussion if you trim the lecture arc), or use the reference site's documented summaries as the shared text and assign the original episodes as clips
A methods course to feed Homework 5, 10 and 14 are data-literacy exercises that transfer directly; the evidence tiers are the beginnings of a coding scheme

8. If you only have time to read two things in this repository

  1. Session 1 — it sets the method, and it is the session that decides whether the term works.
  2. Session 15 — it states the claim and includes the strongest objection to the course. If you agree with that session's answer, you can teach this.

9. Support and content notes

  • Campus counselling for students, and your own institution's employee assistance programme for you. Session 5 in particular can land on the instructor: HIV and death are not abstract for everyone in the room, including the person teaching.
  • The reference site's sources section documents where every factual claim came from and when it was checked. Use the same habit in your own slides: source, year, capture date.

Module 11 — Audiences and Reception

Big question. What do audiences do with a text that its makers never intended?

Where we start. Encoding (Module 4) meets its other half: reception. This module shifts from what a text means to what audiences do with it — the active-audience tradition that refuses to treat viewers as passive recipients. The revival's Insomnia and the Daydreamer gives us two audience-shaped facts: the published summary states that Deborah questions whether her imagined "ideal partner" is right for her, and that a club night goes chaotic when "the aunties" (the original cast) arrive. We pair it with Dr. War Is Hell (1988-10-06), whose title (a reading, ours) frames conflict as its subject. The question throughout: who is doing the interpreting, and what does the arrival of the original audience do to the text?

Sociological frame

  1. Reception: the study of how audiences make meaning of a text — reading is an active production of meaning, not a decoding of a fixed message.
  2. Fandom (Henry Jenkins, Textual Poachers, 1992): audiences that do not merely consume but appropriate — reworking, extending, and claiming a text the makers never fully controlled.
  3. Interpretive communities (Stanley Fish, 1980): groups whose shared strategies of reading make meanings converge — the same text means differently to different communities because they read differently.
  4. The active audience (Ien Ang, 1985; Janice Radway, 1984): the view that viewers are never blank; they negotiate, resist, and use texts for their own purposes — a corrective to the effects tradition.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 What did you do with it? Ask the room to name something they did with a text — rewatched, argued, wrote, shipped, quit. Establish that consumption is already activity.
8–22 From decoder to poacher. Bridge Hall's decoding to Jenkins's fandom: an audience that accepts is one position, but audiences also appropriate. Distinguish reading against the grain from reworking the text.
22–36 The imagined ideal partner. The published summary states Deborah questions whether her imagined partner is right for her. Ask: what is the "imagined" here — the product of whose reading? What does it mean for an audience to daydream a text into something new?
36–50 The aunties arrive. The published summary states the club night goes chaotic when the original cast arrives. Ask what it does to a text when its original audience enters it — fandom, memory, and authority all at once.
50–63 Communities of reading. Bring Fish in: the "aunties" and the new students are two interpretive communities meeting in one scene. What do they read differently, and what does that do? Use Dr. War Is Hell (1988-10-06) as a second text to receive.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: the audience is a co-producer of meaning, never a blank screen. Assign Homework 11; preview Module 12's turn from how people read to how taste marks them — Bourdieu.

Discussion questions

  1. Jenkins's fan "poaches" a text the makers never controlled. Is Deborah's imagined ideal partner an act of poaching, or of submission to the text's preferred reading? What distinguishes the two?
  2. The original cast's arrival makes the audience into content. What does that collapse do to the boundary between text and reader?
  3. Fish says meaning is a property of a community's reading strategies, not of the text alone. What two communities meet in this episode, and what would each read differently?
  4. Radway and Ang argued audiences use texts for their own ends. What "use" is a daydream, and is it a legitimate object for sociology?
  5. If the audience is active, is the maker's intended meaning simply irrelevant? Or does encoding (Module 4) still constrain what audiences can do? Reconcile the two.

Instructor notes

If the room celebrates the "active audience" into meaninglessness. Tax it: active does not mean unconstrained. Ask what the text still refuses to let a reader do — where encoding pushes back. Hall and Jenkins both insist on limits.

If a student reports private viewing habits as evidence. Fine as data, but flag the tier: "I and my friends felt X" is an anecdote, not a documented fact about an audience. Ask how one would test it.

Module 3 — Cultural Production: Who Makes Culture

Big question. Who gets to make the culture the rest of us consume?

Where we start. The previous modules treated culture as something a group does and feels; this module asks the production question — how a cultural object actually gets made, and who is positioned to make it. The case is unusually legible: A Different World began as a spin-off of The Cosby Show and was later chaired by Debbie Allen (both documented in the reference site), and the revival opens with Irreconcilable Differences, whose published summary states that Deborah Wayne — Dwayne and Whitley's youngest — starts her freshman year at Hillman following her parents. We pair it with The Show Must Go On (1988-02-18), an original about putting a show on at all.

Sociological frame

  1. The production of culture (Richard Peterson): the view that cultural objects are shaped less by lone genius than by the social arrangements — organizations, markets, conventions — that make them.
  2. The culture industry (Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, 1944): the argument that industrialized cultural production standardizes products and pacifies audiences, turning culture into a commodity.
  3. Creative labor: the work of making culture, unequally rewarded and unequally visible — whose labor is credited, and whose is absorbed into the product.
  4. Gatekeeping (Becker's Art Worlds; Bourdieu's field): the social positions — executives, producers, chairs, networks — that select which objects get made and which creators get access.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Who made the thing you're watching? Ask the room to reconstruct the chain of production behind a single scene; expose how little of it we normally see.
8–22 The spin-off and the chair. Using the reference cards, establish two documented facts: the show's Cosby spin-off origin, and Debbie Allen's chairing. Ask what each reveals about who controls production.
22–36 The show must go on. Read the original's premise (putting a show on) as a miniature of the production question: what has to be in place — money, venue, labor, permission — before a cultural object exists?
36–50 Production as inheritance. The published summary states that Deborah starts freshman year "following her parents." Ask: is a campus culture made or inherited, and who is the "maker" in each account?
50–63 Culture industry or art world? Stage the debate: does this case better fit Adorno/Horkheimer's standardization-and-commodity story or Becker's cooperative-art-world story? Require each side to name a documented fact it explains.
63–75 Close and assign. Summarize: culture does not self-assemble; it is produced under constraint. Assign Homework 3; preview the turn in Module 4 to what happens after production — the meeting of maker and audience.

Discussion questions

  1. The spin-off origin and the chairing are both documented. What does the existence of a "chair" tell you about how culture is organized versus how we imagine it is made?
  2. Adorno and Horkheimer wrote about standardized, industrialized culture. Does a sitcom — a genre of strict conventions — fit their critique, or does its regularity do something else?
  3. Whose creative labor is visible in a credited show, and whose is not? How would the production-of-culture perspective explain that invisibility?
  4. The published summary says Deborah is "following her parents" to Hillman. What does that inheritance story imply about who can make a campus's culture versus who can only enter it?
  5. Gatekeeping decides what gets made. What gate is being watched in The Show Must Go On (1988-02-18), and who holds it?

Instructor notes

If the room drifts into "auteur" talk (one genius made it). Re-anchor in the documented production facts: the spin-off, the chair, the network, the conventions of the sitcom. Genius is an interpretation; the production chain is documented.

If a student wants the "real reason" a decision was made behind the scenes. That is typically not in the record. Hold the line: we can document that the show was a spin-off and that Allen chaired it; why specific creative choices were made is often hypothesis. Say so rather than guess.

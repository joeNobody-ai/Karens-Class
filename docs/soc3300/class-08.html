Module 8 — Language, Slang, and Identity

Big question. What does the way a community talks reveal about who it is and who belongs?

Where we start. Language is the most ordinary evidence of the social: who may say what to whom, in what register, with what consequence. This module revisits the revival's Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You through its language-and-belonging thread — the published summary states that Kojo is caught up in arguments over African/diaspora identity — and pairs it with the original A World Alike (1990-02-15), whose very title (a reading, ours) frames sameness and difference as its subject. The discipline here matters as much as the theory: all talk about the vernacular runs through the course's vocabulary protocol and lingo.md.

Sociological frame

  1. Language: not a neutral code for pre-existing thoughts but a social practice that marks membership, status, and boundary — a way of doing identity, not just expressing it.
  2. Code-switching (John Gumperz; Jan Blommaert): the practice of shifting between varieties or registers according to context and audience — competence in when to switch, not just in two codes.
  3. The vocabulary of race (Geneva Smitherman, 1994; Rickford and Rickford, 2000): the specific lexicon through which a community names itself — documented as period vocabulary in lingo.md, never claimed as dialogue from the show.
  4. Linguistic capital (Pierre Bourdieu, Language and Symbolic Power, 1991): the value a way of speaking carries in a given "linguistic market" — some registers command authority, others are devalued, and speakers are unequally positioned to profit from their speech.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Set the protocol. Restate norm 0: the instructor does not perform the vernacular; no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce, or perform a term; definitions come from cited sources. State why this matters here.
8–22 Language as social practice. Lay out code-switching and linguistic capital. Ask: when does switching register signal competence (skill) and when does it signal subordination (survival)?
22–36 Diaspora and belonging. The published summary states Kojo is caught in arguments over African/diaspora identity. Ask how language — names, accents, registers — becomes the terrain of that argument. Keep every claim tiered.
36–50 "A World Alike," read carefully. Working from title and air date only (no invented plot), ask what a title naming likeness promises a viewer about language and difference. Mark it clearly as a reading of the title.
50–63 The linguistic market at Hillman. Bring Bourdieu in: which ways of speaking carry capital at this institution, and who profits? Use documented facts (the show's own speech, the diaspora argument) and label the rest.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: language is where identity and hierarchy meet, and we handle it with care. Assign Homework 8; preview Module 9's turn from words to bodies — music and the step.

Discussion questions

  1. Code-switching can be read as skill or as survival. What decides which reading fits a given instance — and can it be both at once?
  2. The published summary says the diaspora argument catches Kojo. Why would language be the site where African and diaspora identity get argued, rather than ancestry or politics?
  3. Bourdieu's "linguistic market" asks who profits from a way of speaking. What is the currency at Hillman, and who holds it, on the documented evidence?
  4. A title like A World Alike (1990-02-15) invites a viewer to expect sameness — or to expect sameness to be tested. Which reading better fits a show about a historically Black college, and why?
  5. The vocabulary protocol forbids anyone from "performing" a term. What is the sociological argument for that rule — what does performing a term do to the analysis?

Instructor notes

If a student slips into performing the vernacular (or asks a classmate to). Hold the line gently and by name: "we discuss terms by number, not by performance." Offer the numbered term from lingo.md instead. This is accuracy, not delicacy — say so.

If the room wants to know "what a word really means." Send it to the cited sources, not to the nearest student. Definitions come from Smitherman, Rickford & Rickford, the OED — and no one in the room is the consultant on their own language.

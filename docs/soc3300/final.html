SOC 3300 — Culture, Media, and Representation — Final Examination

Session: Module 15 · Weight: 30% of the course grade · Format: take-home, open-book, cumulative · Total: 100 points

Instructions

  • The evidence rule is the course's core value and is graded in every answer. Keep three kinds of claim apart: documented (an episode title, air date, credit, or published summary), interpretation (a reading, labeled as yours), and hypothesis (a claim on the table). Never invent dialogue, a plot beat, or a statistic. If the record does not give you a detail, say so and argue from what it does give.
  • AI note. You may use generative tools to check facts or polish prose, and you must disclose any such use. The analytical work — definitions, applications, readings, and the synthesis — must be yours. Undisclosed machine-written analysis is an academic-integrity matter.
  • Cite episodes with the titles and air dates as given in the course. The final is cumulative: an answer earns more when it shows the connections among modules, not just recall of one.

Part A — Short answers (5 × 12 points = 60 points)

Answer each in a focused paragraph or two. Each answer must (1) state the concept(s) accurately, (2) apply them to the documented fact(s) named, and (3) keep documented and interpretive claims visibly separate.

A1. The revival's finale A Gift & The Curse (2026) and the original's series finale A Rock, a River, a Lena (1993-07-02) both close something. Using Boym's distinction between restorative and reflective nostalgia and Halbwachs's account of collective memory, argue which kind of "closing" each finale performs — and say what a series finale owes its audience that a season finale does not. The published summary states that the gala coincides with the crisis, the relationships, and the family situation reaching breaking points, and that major decisions are made about the college's future.

A2. Bourdieu argues taste is produced by social position and does the work of distinction. Apply his three forms of cultural capital (institutionalized, objectified, embodied) to one documented fact from the course — for example, the return of Dwayne and Whitley with "visible tensions" (published summary of Center de Stage) or the headliner tradition. What boundary does the preference or display draw, and who is kept outside it?

A3. Hall's encoding/decoding and Cohen's moral panic are both, at bottom, arguments that meaning is contested and consequential rather than delivered and inert. State the core claim of each, then show how they can be used together on a single documented fact: Deborah speaking her mind in public and being "called out" (O Captain! My Captain!). Where does Hall's model stop and Cohen's begin?

A4. Across the term you met several claims that culture constitutes identity rather than merely expressing it — Goffman's performance, the step as embodied culture, hooks's oppositional gaze. Choose two of these and apply them to the same documented fact of your choosing (named episode and air date), showing what each reveals that the other cannot.

A5. Gray's "double burden" (Module 7), Gilroy's diaspora-as-route (Module 14), and the "aunties" arrival in Insomnia and the Daydreamer (Module 11) each concern who owns a community's image across time. State each position in one sentence, then argue which of the three best explains what happens when the original cast arrives in the revival — and defend the choice.

Part B — Scene analysis (40 points)

Read one documented scene as culture.

Choose one documented scene from the course — the homecoming of Center de Stage, the call-out of O Captain! My Captain!, the aunties' arrival in Insomnia and the Daydreamer, the diaspora argument of Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You, or the gala of A Gift & The Curse — and analyze it as a cultural object. Your analysis must:

  1. State the documented fact accurately (episode title, air date, and what the published summary states), clearly separated from everything that follows.
  2. Use three concepts from three different modules, each doing distinct work on the scene.
  3. Identify the scene's structure and agency — what constrains the people in it, and what they do anyway — and name the course's central tension it displays.
  4. Name one honest limit — something this scene cannot show about itself or about the world.
  5. Keep the evidence tiers visible throughout, and cite your readings for the work they do, not decoratively.

A strong answer is not a summary of the scene; it is an argument that uses the scene to make a claim about culture, media, and representation that you can defend.

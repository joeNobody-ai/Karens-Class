SOC 3300 — Culture, Media, and Representation

Upper-division (3000-level) · 15 modules · 75 minutes each · Prerequisite: one prior sociology course (SOC 1000 recommended); no media-studies background assumed · Taught through A Different World (1987–1993 and the 2026 revival)

1. Catalogue description

Culture is not a backdrop to social life; it is the machinery by which groups make meaning, draw boundaries, and reproduce or contest power. This seminar treats culture sociologically — as something produced, distributed, and consumed under conditions of constraint, rather than as a neutral pool of "content." Using a single, richly layered case — the sitcom A Different World in both of its eras, the 1987–1993 original and the 2026 revival — students work through the major traditions in the sociology of culture and media: Durkheim's account of ritual and collective effervescence; Swidler's and Geertz's competing theories of what culture is; the production-of-culture perspective and the culture-industry critique; Hall's encoding/decoding model and the study of representation and the gaze; Gray's and Means Coleman's accounts of the Black sitcom and its audience; Bourdieu's theory of cultural capital, taste, and distinction; and the study of reception, fandom, memory, nostalgia, and culture-as-resistance. The course is not a survey of the series and not a fan seminar: every module takes one documented episode fact as its anchor and asks a genuine sociological question of it. By term's end, students build and defend a cultural analysis of a single text — the capstone.

3. How the course works

One case, fifteen concepts. We do not survey television. We hold one object — A Different World in two eras — up against roughly fifteen concepts, one or two per module, and ask what the concept can make visible in the case and what the case can test in the concept.

Screen scenes, not seasons. A television season is not a unit of analysis. We work from specific, documented episode facts — a title, an air date, a published summary, a verified reference-site card — and we read scenes (as summarized in the published record) rather than claiming to know whole seasons. Where we look at a whole form (the sitcom, the half-hour), we do so through named episodes.

The evidence rule. Every claim in this course falls into one of three tiers, and we keep them visibly apart: documented (an episode title, air date, credit, or published summary — look-up-able); interpretation (a reading of what a scene or season argues, labeled as a reading); and hypothesis (a claim on the table for testing). We never invent dialogue, plot beats, or statistics. When the record does not tell us a detail, we say so. This discipline is the course's core value and is graded.

The reference site. All documented episode facts are drawn from the project's reference site, which cites its own sources: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/ . Vocabulary and slang are handled through a tiered reference (see lingo.md) under a strict protocol set out in the instructor guide.

6. Policies

Content note. The series and the era it depicts include material about race, gender, sexuality, and violence that we will treat as evidence, not as entertainment. Some module topics (sexual health, the 1992 uprising and its media coverage, moral panic) may be difficult. No student is required to disclose anything personal; anyone may step out or pass at any time.

Discussion norms. No one in the room is a spokesperson for a community. We engage the strongest version of a view we disagree with, we allow silence, and we let evidence — not airtime — win. The vocabulary protocol (norm 0 in the instructor guide) governs all talk about language.

AI policy. Generative tools may be used to check facts or polish prose, and any such use must be disclosed. They may not substitute for the analytical work — the claim, the reading, the evidence discipline — that this course grades. Undisclosed machine-written analysis is an academic-integrity matter.

Accessibility. Materials are text-first. Accommodations are honored on request without requiring documentation up front; contact the instructor early so arrangements can be made.

Late work. One-page homeworks may be submitted up to 48 hours late for half credit; the lowest two are dropped regardless of reason. Exams and the capstone are not accepted late except by prior arrangement.

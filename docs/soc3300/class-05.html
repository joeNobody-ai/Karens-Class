Module 5 — Representation and the Gaze

Big question. Who is looking at whom, and who gets to be the one looking?

Where we start. Representation is not a mirror: media do not reflect people, they construct them within regimes of looking. This module turns to the second half of the revival's Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You, whose published summary states that Hazel runs for Miss Gilbert Hall while the students also deal with sexual health and relationships, and pairs it with the original pageant episode Mr. Hillman (1988-02-25). A pageant is the ideal case for this question: a structured scene of being looked at, with judges, an audience, and a title at stake.

Sociological frame

  1. Representation (Stuart Hall, 1997): the production of meaning through language and images — how a group is made to appear, always within power relations, never a simple reflection of reality.
  2. The gaze (Laura Mulvey, 1975; adapted): the structuring of looking by power — who looks, who is looked at, and who controls the terms of display.
  3. Display: the act of presenting oneself to be seen — which can be an assertion of agency or a submission to another's terms, depending on who sets the frame.
  4. Being seen versus being watched (John Berger, 1972; bell hooks, 1992): the difference between appearing as a subject who acts and being positioned as an object of another's look — hooks's "oppositional gaze" names the refusal to accept that positioning.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 A pageant is a theory of looking. Ask: who is looking, who is looked at, and who profits from the arrangement? Establish that a pageant is a structured scene of the gaze.
8–22 The concepts, laid out. Distinguish representation (the construction) from the gaze (the structure of looking) from display (the act). Introduce Berger's seen/watched distinction and hooks's oppositional gaze.
22–36 Running for Miss Gilbert Hall. The published summary states Hazel runs for the title. Ask: when Hazel campaigns, is she asserting display (choosing to be seen) or submitting to a watch (being judged)? Mark the tension, don't resolve it.
36–50 Mr. Hillman, forty years earlier. Read Mr. Hillman (1988-02-25) as the same structure of looking. Ask what the pageant makes visible about who gets to look, and what has changed — or not — across eras.
50–63 The oppositional gaze. Bring hooks in: can the looked-at look back and refuse the frame? Ask whether either pageant leaves room for a subject who controls the terms, using documented facts only.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: representation is power made visible; the gaze is its mechanism. Assign Homework 5; preview Module 6's move from who looks to the form (the sitcom) that stages the looking.

Discussion questions

  1. The published summary states Hazel runs for the title. Is "running" an act of agency or an entry into a structure she does not control — or both? What does your answer turn on?
  2. Berger's "men act and women appear" was written in 1972. Does it travel to a pageant whose participants are, in these cases, at a historically Black college? What must be adjusted?
  3. hooks's oppositional gaze argues that looking back is a form of resistance. Where, in a pageant, could such a look even happen?
  4. The second half also involves "sexual health and relationships" per the summary. How does that content connect to the question of who is watched and who is seen?
  5. What is the difference between a pageant and a talent show (Module 2's homecoming) as regimes of looking? Use documented facts from both.

Instructor notes

If the room collapses "the gaze" into "men look at women." Mulvey's account is gendered and heteronormative in its original form; broaden, don't drop it. The question here is any structure where one party looks and another is displayed. Keep the concept and widen the cases.

If a student asserts how a character "feels" about being watched. We do not have that in the record. Redirect to what is shown (the campaign, the title, the judging) and ask what the structure implies, clearly labeled as interpretation.

SOC 3300 — Culture, Media, and Representation — Midterm Examination

Released: after Module 7 · Due: before Module 9 · Weight: 20% of the course grade Format: take-home, open-book · Total: 100 points · Time: you may use the reference site and your notes; the essay is the timed part (see Part B).

Instructions

  • The evidence rule applies and is graded. Keep three kinds of claim apart in every answer: documented (an episode title, air date, credit, or published summary), interpretation (a reading, labeled as yours), and hypothesis (a claim on the table). Never invent dialogue, a plot beat, or a statistic. If the record does not give you a detail, say so and argue from what it does give.
  • AI note. You may use generative tools to check facts or polish prose, and you must disclose any such use. The analytical work — the definitions, the applications, the readings — must be yours. An undisclosed machine-written answer is an academic-integrity matter.
  • Cite episodes using the titles and air dates as given in the course. Answer what is asked, in the space it asks for.

Part A — Short answers (5 × 12 points = 60 points)

Answer each in a focused paragraph. Each answer must (1) define the concept accurately, and (2) apply it to the documented fact named, keeping documented and interpretive claims visibly separate.

A1. Geertz and Swidler give competing theories of culture. Define both in one sentence each, then apply them to the homecoming in Center de Stage (2026): the published summary states that Deborah tries to secure a headlining performer while Dwayne and Whitley return with visible tensions in their marriage. What does each theory make visible in that fact that the other does not?

A2. Define Durkheim's collective effervescence and state the causal claim he attaches to it (assembly → feeling → solidarity). Then apply it to A Stepping Stone (1988-12-01): what would a Durkheimian look for in a step routine to test whether effervescence is being generated, and what would count against the claim?

A3. Define gatekeeping in the production-of-culture sense, and contrast it briefly with the culture-industry account of Adorno and Horkheimer. Then apply both to two documented facts: that A Different World began as a Cosby Show spin-off and was later chaired by Debbie Allen. Which of the two theories explains those facts better, and why?

A4. State Hall's three decoding positions (preferred/dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional) and explain what distinguishes the negotiated from the oppositional. Then, using the published summary of O Captain! My Captain! — that Deborah speaks her mind in public and is called out — construct a negotiated reading of the call-out and an oppositional one, and show how they differ.

A5. Herman Gray argues the Black sitcom is an argument about blackness, not a window onto it. Explain what that distinction means, and what the "double burden" is that Gray and Means Coleman attribute to the genre. Then apply the distinction to one documented production fact of your choosing from the course, stating what your chosen fact shows the argument to be.

Part B — Applied essay (40 points)

"Who gets to make the culture the rest of us consume?"

Write a tight essay (roughly 700–900 words) that answers this question using two of the following concepts — encoding/decoding (Hall), the production of culture (Peterson), representation and the gaze (Mulvey/hooks), or the sitcom as a cultural form — applied to at least two documented facts from the course (named episodes and air dates). Your essay must:

  1. Open with a plain, arguable thesis in one or two sentences.
  2. Let each concept do distinct work on the evidence — not two names for the same point.
  3. Keep documented facts and your interpretations visibly separate.
  4. Name one honest limit — something the evidence cannot show.

A strong essay is not a summary of the course; it is an argument that uses the course to say something you can defend.

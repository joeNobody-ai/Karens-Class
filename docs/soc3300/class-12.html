Module 12 — Cultural Capital and Taste

Big question. How does what you like become who you are — and who you are not?

Where we start. Taste feels private — "I just like this." Bourdieu's Distinction (1979) argues the opposite: taste is socially produced, it runs along class lines, and it does boundary work, sorting people by the very preferences they experience as personal. This module revisits the homecoming of Center de Stage through the lens of taste and status — the published summary states that Dwayne and Whitley return with visible tensions in their marriage — and pairs it with Whitley's Last Supper (1990-10-11), whose title (a reading, ours) stages taste as a dinner-party affair: hosting, refinement, and the work of distinction.

Sociological frame

  1. Bourdieu (Pierre Bourdieu, Distinction, 1979): taste is not free preference but the product of one's position in social space — it is distinction in both senses: refined judgment and social separation.
  2. Taste: the embodied system of preferences — in food, music, décor, partners — that classifies the classifier; we reveal our position by what we claim to like.
  3. Cultural capital: the stock of legitimate knowledge, tastes, and dispositions one possesses — institutionalized (degrees), objectified (books, art), and embodied (accent, ease, know-how) — that converts into advantage.
  4. Distinction: the mechanism by which taste draws and polices boundaries — the aesthetic judgment ("vulgar," "classy") is a social judgment about who belongs.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 "I just like it." Ask the room to defend a preference without mentioning themselves. Watch it fail. Introduce the hypothesis: preference is position.
8–22 Bourdieu, carefully. Lay out habitus → capital → field, then the punch: taste is where class gets felt as nature. Distinguish the three forms of cultural capital.
22–36 Taste inside a marriage. The published summary states Dwayne and Whitley return with visible tensions. Ask (as a hypothesis): if taste is distinction, what does a couple's visible tension have to do with status — and what would the evidence have to show?
36–50 The dinner party as distinction. Read Whitley's Last Supper (1990-10-11) through its title: a "last supper" is a hosting event — food, setting, guests, all legible as taste. Ask what a dinner party classifies. Mark readings as readings.
50–63 Capital at Hillman. Bring the three capitals to the homecoming: whose taste is "legitimate" at this institution, and what does the headliner tradition (documented) do to that hierarchy?
63–75 Close and assign. Land: liking is sorting; distinction is the boundary it draws. Assign Homework 12; preview Module 13's turn to how the revival uses memory to trade on the original.

Discussion questions

  1. Bourdieu says taste classifies the classifier. What would a sociologist need to observe — not assume — to test that claim in this case? Name the data you'd want.
  2. The published summary says the marriage shows "visible tensions." Is a marital tension a taste difference, a status difference, or neither? What distinguishes those readings?
  3. A dinner party (the original's title, our reading) makes taste public. Why is hosting such a pure site of distinction — what is being evaluated, and by whom?
  4. Embodied cultural capital is carried in the body: accent, ease, posture. Where in the homecoming do you see embodied capital doing boundary work, on the documented evidence?
  5. If taste is produced by position, can anyone learn legitimate taste — or does that very question smuggle in a distinction? Explain.

Instructor notes

If the room insists taste is just personal. Don't argue from authority; run the test. Ask them to locate their own preference socially (who shares it, who scorns it, what it costs to signal). Let the pattern do Bourdieu's work.

If a student attributes taste to a character's "personality." That is a pre-sociological explanation. Ask: what shaped that personality's preferences — and what does the preference do for the character's standing? The question is social, not psychological.

Module 14 — Culture as Resistance

Big question. When is a cultural act a political act?

Where we start. Culture is not only what a group has or does (Module 1) — it can be what a group uses to push back. This module revisits the revival's Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You through its resistance thread: the published summary states that Kojo is caught up in arguments over African/diaspora identity — an argument that is at once cultural and political. We connect it to the student organizing the revival later depicts (documented in the summary of Romancing the Unknown: "students organize" around the budget crisis), and to the original A World Alike (1990-02-15), whose title (a reading, ours) frames sameness and difference as the terrain of contest.

Sociological frame

  1. Counter-culture (Dick Hebdige, Subculture, 1979): a group's styling of itself against a dominant culture — style as a coded refusal that mainstream culture then absorbs or stigmatizes.
  2. Resistance (James C. Scott, Weapons of the Weak, 1985): not only open revolt but everyday, low-visibility acts — foot-dragging, parody, refusal of terms — through which the less powerful push back without announcing it.
  3. Cultural politics: the contest over meaning itself as a political struggle — who gets to define the group, the past, the "we" — which is what diaspora arguments are fundamentally about.
  4. Diaspora (Paul Gilroy, The Black Atlantic, 1993; Stuart Hall): identity as formed through dispersal, not rooted fixity — a "route" rather than a "root," which reframes belonging as an ongoing negotiation rather than a birthright.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 When culture fights back. Ask the room to name a cultural act (a style, a phrase, a song) that doubled as a political act. Establish the question: where is the line?
8–22 Resistance, more than revolt. Lay out Hebdige's style-as-refusal and Scott's everyday resistance. The key move: resistance is often cultural — fought over meaning, not just power — and often disguised.
22–36 The diaspora argument. The published summary states Kojo is caught in arguments over African/diaspora identity. Ask: why is identity the battlefield — what is actually at stake when "who counts as us" is contested?
36–50 From argument to action. The summary of Romancing the Unknown states that students organize around the budget crisis (documented). Ask how a cultural argument (identity, belonging) becomes political action — and vice versa.
50–63 Diaspora as a critical concept. Bring Gilroy and Hall in: diaspora reframes belonging as route, not root. Ask how that reframing is itself a resistance to fixed, exclusive definitions of the group. Use A World Alike (1990-02-15) as the echo.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: cultural politics is politics, and resistance often wears the clothing of style and speech. Assign Homework 14; preview the capstone's demand to read a whole text.

Discussion questions

  1. Hebdige says subcultural style is a "coded refusal" that mainstream culture then absorbs. Where, in the diaspora argument, do you see refusal — and where do you see the risk of absorption?
  2. Scott's "weapons of the weak" are low-visibility. What would everyday resistance look like in a campus argument over identity, as opposed to open protest?
  3. The published summary says the argument is over "African/diaspora identity." What is the difference between root and route models of identity, and why is that difference politically loaded?
  4. Student organizing (documented in the later episode) is collective and explicit. Is that still culture, or has it become politics — and what, if anything, turns the one into the other?
  5. Is a claim about who belongs ever merely cultural? Defend your answer with a case from the module.

Instructor notes

If the room reduces resistance to protest marches. Re-anchor in Scott: much resistance is low-visibility and everyday. Ask what a cultural refusal looks like when it does not take the form of a rally.

If a student asserts what a character "really believes" politically. We do not have interiority in the record. Redirect to the documented argument and its stakes; label any inference about motive as interpretation.

Module 9 — Music, Performance, and Identity

Big question. How does a step routine or a theme song carry identity?

Where we start. Words (Module 8) are one register of identity; bodies and music are another, and they carry what words often cannot. This module revisits the homecoming step of Center de Stage and its 1988 throwback A Stepping Stone (1988-12-01), reading the step — a percussive, synchronized, embodied tradition associated with historically Black Greek-letter organizations — as a performance in the sociological sense: a doing that constitutes the identity it appears merely to express. We pair the step with the show's own musical identity: the reference card documents the revival's theme song (the "Brandy" theme).

Sociological frame

  1. Performance (Erving Goffman, The Presentation of Self, 1959): social life as enacted — identity is not a fixed interior revealed by action but something done in front of others.
  2. The step (Elizabeth Fine, Soulstepping, 2003): a synchronized, percussive, chanted-and-stepped tradition rooted in historically Black Greek-letter organizations — a coded display of history, affiliation, and discipline.
  3. Music and identity (Simon Frith; Tia DeNora): music does not merely reflect a pre-existing identity; listening and performing make and manage identity — a theme song is a claim about who "we" are.
  4. Embodied culture: knowledge and identity carried in the body — rhythm, movement, posture — that cannot be fully transferred as text; the step is culture you must be to transmit.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Why bodies? Ask: what can a step communicate that a speech cannot? Establish that some identity is carried in the body, not just spoken.
8–22 Performance, not expression. Lay out Goffman's performance metaphor and distinguish it from "faking." Performance is constitutive: the doing makes the identity, it does not just vent it.
22–36 Read the step. Using the homecoming-step card, identify what the step does: synchronization, call-and-response, lineage. Ask what identity is being made in the doing — and what discipline it demands.
36–50 "A Stepping Stone," forty years earlier. Read the original's title and the documented step as the same argument: the step is a stepping stone — a ritual of becoming. Ask what a step transmits across forty years.
50–63 The theme song as signature. Using the Brandy-theme card, ask what a theme song does for a show's identity — what it promises, what it calls up, who it hails. Compare song-as-identity to step-as-identity.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: identity is performed and embodied, not merely stated. Assign Homework 9; preview Module 10's turn to the effects question — does media change behavior?

Discussion questions

  1. Goffman's "performance" is often misread as insincerity. How does the step — where discipline and repetition are the point — correct that misreading?
  2. The step is documented as a tradition. What would be lost if it were written down instead of stepped? What does that tell you about embodied versus textual culture?
  3. A "stepping stone" (the original's title, 1988-12-01) names becoming, not being. What does reading the step as ritual of becoming change about how we understand it?
  4. A theme song is heard by everyone, but only some can perform the step. What is the difference between listening to and doing culture, for identity?
  5. If performance constitutes identity, who controls the script — the performer or the tradition? Use the step and the theme song as your two cases.

Instructor notes

If the room reduces the step to "impressive choreography." Re-anchor: the question is not whether it is skilled but what identity the skill constitutes — affiliation, lineage, discipline. Ask what a viewer who misses the code misses.

If a student wants to demonstrate or imitate the step. Redirect, consistent with the vocabulary protocol's spirit: we analyze the documented practice, we do not perform it. Offer the reference card instead.

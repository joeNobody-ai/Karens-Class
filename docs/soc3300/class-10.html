Module 10 — Media Effects and Moral Panics

Big question. Does media change behavior, or does it change what we fear?

Where we start. Every generation worries that media will corrupt the young. This module asks whether that worry is itself the phenomenon: does media cause behavior, or does the framing of media — and of the people who consume it — produce a panic that is itself a social fact? We revisit the revival's O Captain! My Captain! (the published summary states that Deborah speaks her mind in public and is called out) and pair it with Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) (1992-09-24), an original that aired months after the 1992 Los Angeles uprising — a fact the reference card documents under the "Rodney King precedent." The juxtaposition is deliberate: a speech that gets "called out," and a city whose media image became the story.

Sociological frame

  1. Media effects: the long tradition asking whether media directly cause attitudes and behavior — from "hypodermic" models to limited-effects and cultivation accounts; the course's job is to weigh the tradition, not to assume it.
  2. Moral panics (Stanley Cohen, Folk Devils and Moral Panics, 1972): an episode of exaggerated public alarm in which a group is cast as a threat ("folk devils") and the alarm itself becomes the story — Cohen's question is who panics, and what the panic does.
  3. Cohen: the sociologist who named the moral panic and showed that panics are periodic and patterned — they recur around different targets while keeping the same shape.
  4. Framing (Erving Goffman, Frame Analysis, 1974): the selection and emphasis that turns an event into "a story of this kind" — what is in frame, what is out of frame, and who controls the frame.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Name the discomfort. Flag that this module touches the 1992 uprising and media coverage of Black people as a threat. Restate the pass. Ask the opening question: what does media actually do to people?
8–22 Effects and panics, distinguished. Lay out the effects tradition, then Cohen's moral panic. The pivot: a panic is itself a media effect — on the public's fear — whatever the target's real behavior.
22–36 The called-out speech. The published summary states Deborah speaks publicly and is called out. Ask: is the "call-out" a response to the speech, or a framing of it? Who controls the frame, and what gets left out?
36–50 L.A., 1992. Using the Rodney-King-precedent card, establish the documented context: the original aired 1992-09-24, months after the uprising. Ask how the media framing of a city becomes a premise — and whose fear is in the frame.
50–63 Does media cause, or does it frame? Debate: for a student speech and for a city's image, does media change behavior or change fear? Require each side to distinguish the two effects and cite a documented fact.
63–75 Close and assign. Land: the more testable claim is often about framing and fear, not direct causation. Assign Homework 10; preview Module 11's turn from what media does to people to what audiences do with media.

Discussion questions

  1. Cohen showed that moral panics recur with the same shape around different targets. What is the shape, and what would a 2026 panic about student speech have in common with a 1992 panic about a city?
  2. The published summary says Deborah is "called out." Is a call-out a moral panic in miniature, or a legitimate use of public speech to answer public speech? What distinguishes the two?
  3. "Framing" selects and emphasizes. For Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1) (1992-09-24), what is in the frame by the title alone, and what is out of it? Mark your answer as interpretation.
  4. The effects tradition wants to prove media causes behavior. What would conclusive evidence even look like, and why is it so hard to produce? Name the methodological problem.
  5. If media mostly changes fear rather than behavior, who benefits from that fear, and who pays for it? Argue from the two cases.

Instructor notes

If a student claims media "makes people" do something. Push for the mechanism and the tier: which claim is documented (the air date, the title, the call-out) and which is hypothesis (a causal effect)? Do not let "media effects" stand as a self-evident premise.

If the L.A. material is met with silence. Honor the silence; do not fill it. Offer the documented facts again, slowly, and let the framing question do the work. The point is the media story, not a re-litigation of the event.

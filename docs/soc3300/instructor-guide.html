Instructor Guide — SOC 3300 — Culture, Media, and Representation

This guide is for the instructor. It is not distributed to students. It assumes you have read the syllabus, the rubrics (rubrics.md), and the vocabulary reference (lingo.md).

1. Why this course works (and what it is not)

What it is. An upper-division seminar in the sociology of culture and media that uses one object — A Different World in two eras — to make fifteen concepts do real work. The case is well suited to the theory: it is a cultural object with a documented production history (the Cosby spin-off, Debbie Allen's chairing of it), a legible genre (the sitcom, the Black sitcom), two audiences separated by forty years, and a revival whose very existence is a question about memory, nostalgia, and what culture is for.

What it is not. It is not a survey of the series; students are not asked to have seen everything. It is not a fan seminar — appreciation is welcome but is not the activity. It is not media-studies lite: the theory is real (Durkheim, Bourdieu, Hall, Gray) and the questions are demanding. It is not a course about Black culture delivered to outsiders as spectacle; it is a course about how culture, media, and representation work, using a case the room will hold respectfully. Keep all three of those negations in front of you — they are the most common ways this course goes wrong.

2. Before the term starts

  • Confirm the reference site is reachable and that the anchors your modules cite resolve: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/ . Each module's "Further reading" lists the exact anchor strings; spot-check three of them.
  • Read the catalog for your course (data/soc3300_catalog.json) — it is the source of truth for titles, air dates, and concept lists, and the verifier machine-checks the class files against it.
  • Skim lingo.md and the protocol in §4 below. You do not need the whole vocabulary reference; you need the discipline.
  • Print the three-tier evidence rule (§3) on a one-pager. It goes on the wall, or on every slide deck.
  • Decide your capstone mechanics early (see §7): what "a single text" may be, and the checkpoint dates.

3. The evidence rule — three tiers

Every claim in the room lands in one tier. The tiers are not "strong vs. weak evidence"; they are different kinds of claim, and the course grades the difference.

Tier What it is Example
documented An episode title, air date, credit, or published summary — look-up-able A Stepping Stone aired 1988-12-01; the published summary of Center de Stage states that Dwayne and Whitley return with visible tensions in their marriage
interpretation A reading of what a scene or season argues, labeled as a reading "I read the homecoming step as a display of collective identity" — marked as the speaker's reading
hypothesis A claim on the table for testing "If the revival's callbacks work, they work only for viewers who hold the original in memory"

The discipline: never fabricate dialogue or quotes; describe scenes from the published summary and mark the source ("the published summary states"); never invent a plot beat or a statistic. If you are unsure whether a detail is documented, say "the published summary states that…" and stop there. When a student asserts a population claim from a sample of one, or passes an interpretation off as fact, the rubric authorizes a single ungraded rewrite request — state the condition, return it once.

4. The hard conversations (including norm 0)

Some modules will surface race, gender, class, sexuality, and the violence of the era (the 1992 uprising and its media coverage in Module 10). Prepare for these by name; do not let them ambush you.

  1. The vocabulary protocol. Two rules, stated on day one and never relaxed: the instructor does not perform the vernacular, and no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term. Definitions come from the cited sources (see lingo.md), the "handle with care" terms are numbered on slides and discussed by number, and nobody in the room is ever the consultant on their own language.
  2. The no-spokesperson rule. No student represents a community. Redirect "what do Black people think" to "what does this text's audience do," and "as a [identity]" to "what does the evidence show." Restate the person's claim in its strongest form and ask for the evidence tier.
  3. The care tier. Terms used about Black people in the era's media and criticism are analyzed in the third person and never spoken, defined, or performed. They are numbered on slides and discussed by number. This is not delicacy; it is accuracy about who is being described and by whom.
  4. The named discomfort. When the material is heavy (Module 10's moral-panic material, Module 5's gaze), say so at the top of the hour, name what is coming, and offer the pass. Silence is allowed and is not a failure of participation.

5. Running the room on screened scenes

We screen scenes from the published summaries, not full episodes, and we do not rely on anyone's private memory as a source. Before any discussion: (a) state the documented fact in play; (b) name the summary it comes from; (c) ask the room to sort each subsequent claim into the three tiers. Keep the room on the text — "which episode, which scene, which summary" is the standing question. When someone reaches for a generalization about "the show," ask them to name the episode. The 75-minute tables in each class file are a floor plan, not a script; hold the evidence rule and let the concept drive.

6. Adaptation notes

  • 3000-level calibration. Unlike the survey, this course expects students to hold two or three theorists in tension (Swidler vs. Geertz, Durkheim vs. the culture industry, Hall vs. a effects tradition). Push for the difference between positions, not the definitions. The midterm and final demand define-and-apply plus a defended reading, not recall.
  • The revisit modules. Roughly half the modules revisit a 2026 episode through a second lens (Module 2 revisits the homecoming for ritual; Module 12 revisits it for taste). Do not re-screen; re-ask. Point the second lens at the same documented facts.
  • Shortening or lengthening. To compress, drop the optional-clip row, not the evidence rule. To extend, add a second original episode to a module — but only one from the approved list, and always with title and air date verbatim.
  • Substitution. The approved originals are fixed (the verifier checks title and air date). Do not swap in unlisted episodes. If a screen is unavailable, teach from the published summary and say so.

7. The capstone (15%)

The capstone is a defended cultural analysis of a single text — one episode, one scene (as summarized), or one run of the reference-site cards — read through at least three course concepts, with documented facts separated from interpretation, an honest limit named, and the readings cited for work, not decoration. Set the text menu at Module 8, a one-page proposal at Module 11, and the final at Module 15. Grade with rubric 5 in rubrics.md. The rubric's "plain claim" is the whole game: if you cannot state the claim in one sentence, the analysis has not started.

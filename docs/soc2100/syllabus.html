SOC 2100 — Social Problems

2000-level survey · 15 modules · 75 minutes each · no prerequisites (Introduction to Sociology recommended)

1. Catalogue description

This course is a survey of how social problems get made, measured, and contested — taught through a single recurring case, the television series A Different World (1987–1993) and its 2026 revival. We treat the fictional Hillman College as a laboratory: the same institution, across thirty-nine years, is the stage on which poverty, racial and gender inequality, health and stigma, crime, media panic, immigration, and housing are repeatedly named as problems by different people with different stakes. Students learn the constructionist approach (why a condition becomes a problem only when someone claims it is one), the structural accounts that compete with individual and deficit explanations, and the evidence discipline required to keep documented fact, interpretation, and hypothesis apart. The course is an introduction to the sociological imagination: no student is asked to fix a problem; every student is asked to say precisely what the problem is, who is claiming it, and what their account leaves out.

2. Learning outcomes

ID Outcome
LO1 Explain how a condition becomes defined as a social problem (claims-making and constructionism)
LO2 Apply sociological evidence to a specific social problem
LO3 Distinguish structural accounts of problems from individual and deficit accounts
LO4 Separate documented facts from interpretations when reading a text about a problem
LO5 Evaluate an empirical claim about a social problem using primary data
LO6 Propose and defend a response to a social problem, with its costs and beneficiaries

3. How the course works

One case, fifteen concepts. Every module reads the same object — A Different World in both of its eras — through a different lens. Week by week we add concepts (constructionism, inequality, racial formation, doing gender, stigma, credentialing, moral panic, precarity, segregation, framing, intersectionality, and more), so that by the capstone you can hold several of them at once against the same scene.

Screen scenes, not seasons. We do not treat the series as a single argument to be summarized. We screen and discuss scenes, named through the reference project's documented cards, and we never infer a "message of the show" that the evidence does not support.

The evidence rule. Every claim in this course is one of three kinds, and they are never mixed: (1) documented — an episode title, air date, credit, or published summary that you can look up; (2) interpretation — a reading of what a scene or season argues, which you must label as a reading; (3) hypothesis — a claim put on the table to be tested. We never fabricate dialogue, quotes, or statistics, and we never present a reading as a fact.

The reference site. Both eras are documented at https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/ (text view: https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/). Every module's "Further reading" links to the relevant cards.

6. Policies

Content note. Social problems are, by definition, difficult material: poverty, racism, sexism, illness, death, sexual violence, policing, and housing precarity all appear in this course, in the readings and in the series we screen. We do not avoid them, and we do not treat them as spectacle. If a screening or reading is likely to be difficult for you, you may step out or complete an alternative without penalty — tell me before or after, your choice, no explanation required.

Discussion norms. No one is a spokesperson for a group; everyone speaks from their own position. We engage the strongest version of a view we disagree with. We separate what is documented from what we are arguing. Silence is allowed and respected.

AI policy. Generative tools may be used to brainstorm and to check grammar, and any such use must be disclosed. They may not be used to generate a claim, an analysis, a figure, or a citation that you cannot stand behind from a primary source. Fabricated statistics or sources — human or machine — are an academic-integrity violation and a failure of the course's core skill.

Accessibility. Materials are provided in accessible formats. If you need an accommodation, tell me what would help; I will work with you and, where applicable, the accessibility office.

Late work. Homework may be submitted up to 48 hours late for partial credit; after that it is not accepted unless we have agreed in advance. The two lowest homework grades are dropped, so an occasional miss is already built in.

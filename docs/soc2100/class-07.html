Module 7 — Family Problems and Family Change

Big question. When families change, who calls it a problem — and what is actually changing?

Where we start. "Cuffing season" arrives and the students' relationships get messier, while Dwayne and Whitley's marriage shows visible strain — two generations of family in the same frame. We separate what is actually changing about families from what people claim is wrong with them.

Sociological frame

  1. Family (OpenStax 2021): a socially recognized unit of kinship and care — whose definition has itself changed across eras, which is the point.
  2. The marriage market: the idea that partnering operates partly like a market — people match on resources, status, and timing — a structural account of who ends up with whom.
  3. Family change: the documented shifts in family form — later marriage, more single-parent households, dual-earner couples — description, before anyone calls it a problem.
  4. The culture-versus-structure debate: whether family change is driven by changing values (culture) or changing conditions (structure) — and why the answer is rarely one or the other.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Opening poll. "What is wrong with the family today?" — collect claims, then ask who is claiming each one.
8–22 Description before verdict. Family change as data: the class lists what has measurably changed, separated from whether it is good or bad.
22–36 The marriage market. Read the published summary of Those Who Can...Cuff: how do timing, money, and status enter the students' relationships?
36–50 Two eras of the ideal. Dream Lover (1988-11-03) and Whitley's Last Supper (1990-10-11): what each treats as the "problem" in a relationship.
50–63 Culture versus structure. Small groups argue one side: is family change a story about values or about conditions?
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 7 (a culture-versus-structure analysis); remind the midterm is due before Module 9.

Discussion questions

  1. When a family "changes," what exactly is changing — the form, the values, or the conditions?
  2. The marriage market is a structural account of romance. What does it explain that "chemistry" cannot?
  3. Who gets to call a family arrangement a "problem," and what do they stand to gain from the claim?
  4. What does Dream Lover (1988-11-03) treat as wrong with an imagined partner, and what does that say about the era's ideal?
  5. Is family change driven by culture or structure? Defend one, then name what it cannot explain.

Instructor notes

If the room moralizes about a family form. Return to the module's move: describe before you judge. Ask what is documented about the change and what is a claim about it.

If a student generalizes from their own family. Remind the room of the no-spokesperson norm and ask for the structural pattern rather than the single case.

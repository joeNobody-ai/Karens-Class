Module 14 — Intersectionality: Problems Overlap

Big question. What happens when race, class, and gender are the same problem, not three?

Where we start. We return to Ep 7 whole, this time holding all of it at once: the diaspora argument, the Miss Gilbert Hall run, the sexual-health and relationship material. Separately, these are three problems; together, they are one person's situation. We use the full episode to teach intersectionality — the refusal to treat race, class, and gender as separate lanes.

Sociological frame

  1. Intersectionality (Crenshaw 1989): the framework holding that race, class, gender, and other axes are not separate problems but mutually constituting — experienced together, and misread when analyzed apart.
  2. Compounding disadvantage: the way disadvantages on multiple axes do not simply add but multiply — a person at two intersections faces more than the sum of two problems.
  3. Matrix of domination (Collins 1990): the interlocking organization of power along multiple axes, such that no single axis explains a person's position.
  4. Single-axis analysis (Crenshaw 1989): the error intersectionality corrects — analyzing one axis in isolation and thereby rendering the people at the intersections invisible.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Opening poll. "Are race, class, and gender three problems or one?" — collect votes and reasons.
8–22 Single-axis analysis and its blind spot. Define the error; the class finds a place where a single-axis account misses a person entirely.
22–36 The full episode, held together. Re-read Ep 7's summary: where do race, gender, and class meet in one character's situation?
36–50 Compounding, not adding. Lecture: why intersection is multiplicative; a worked example from housing + gender + race.
50–63 The matrix of domination. Collins defined; the class maps the episode's characters onto the matrix, not onto single lanes.
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 14 (an intersectional re-reading); set up the capstone.

Discussion questions

  1. What does a single-axis account of the Miss Gilbert Hall run miss?
  2. Why is "compounding" a better word than "adding" for disadvantage at an intersection?
  3. Can you name a problem in Ep 7 that is only about race, or only about gender? What does your answer show?
  4. What does the matrix of domination capture that a list of separate "isms" does not?
  5. A World Alike (1990-02-15) predates the term "intersectionality" in common use. What does the episode show anyway?

Instructor notes

If the room wants to rank "which axis matters most." Refuse the ranking as the wrong question: intersectionality is precisely the claim that the axes cannot be ranked because they are lived together.

If a student says "this is just identity politics." Redirect to the analytic payoff: the framework explains outcomes (who is invisible in a single-axis account) — it is a tool, not a slogan.

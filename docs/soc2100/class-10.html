Module 10 — Media and the Construction of Social Problems

Big question. Does the media report problems, or manufacture them?

Where we start. We revisit Ep 2, focusing on the public call-out: Deborah speaks her mind and is called out in public, an event that exists because it is witnessed and amplified. We use it to study how media — and the audience it assembles — turns an incident into a "problem" with a shape, a villain, and a moral.

Sociological frame

  1. Media (OpenStax 2021): the institutions that produce and distribute representations — which select, frame, and amplify rather than simply mirror.
  2. Moral panics (Cohen 1972): episodes in which a group or condition is defined as a threat to social values, out of proportion to the evidence — a media-driven cycle.
  3. Framing (Goffman 1974): the selection and emphasis that tells an audience what kind of thing an event is — a problem, a joke, a tragedy.
  4. Claims-making (Best 2021): the activity, here conducted through media, of asserting a condition is a problem and demanding a response.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Opening poll. "Name a problem you heard about only through media." — collect; ask how you know it is real.
8–22 Selection, framing, amplification. Three verbs defined; the class applies each to a recent headline.
22–36 The call-out as a media event. Read the published summary: who witnesses, who amplifies, and what frame is imposed on the incident?
36–50 Moral panic anatomy. Cohen's cycle — definition, folk devil, disproportionate response — traced on a contemporary example, then on The Show Must Go On (1988-02-18).
50–63 The show's own frame. Using the ep01-cosby-origin and ep01-debbie-allen-chair cards, ask whose position the series itself is built from.
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 10 (a framing analysis of one story).

Discussion questions

  1. What is the difference between an incident and a media event, and who makes the difference?
  2. Does the media report problems or manufacture them — or is that a false either/or?
  3. What does a moral panic need to get going, and who benefits from it?
  4. How is a "frame" different from a "bias," and why does the distinction matter?
  5. The Show Must Go On (1988-02-18) is about staging a show. What does that reveal about how problems get staged?

Instructor notes

If a student says "the media is all lies." Reframe: the interesting question is not truth versus lies but selection and emphasis — what gets shown, from whose position, and what gets left out.

If the discussion fixates on one outlet. Generalize to the mechanism: framing and moral panic are properties of how media works, not of one bad actor.

media · moral panics · framing · claims-making · amplification

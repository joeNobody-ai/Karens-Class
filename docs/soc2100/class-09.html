Module 9 — Immigration and the Boundaries of Belonging

Big question. Who gets to belong, and who gets written out when a category is defined tightly?

Where we start. We revisit Ep 7, this time focusing on the diaspora argument: Kojo is caught in a dispute over African and diaspora identity, a fight about who is inside a category and who is kept outside. We use it to study how boundaries of belonging are drawn — and what it costs the people they exclude.

Sociological frame

  1. Immigration (OpenStax 2021): the movement of people across borders to settle — and, sociologically, the categorization of those people once they arrive.
  2. Diaspora: a dispersed population that retains ties to a homeland — an identity that spans borders, complicating any single national "inside."
  3. Nativism: the claim that the native-born deserve priority over immigrants — a boundary claim, not a neutral description.
  4. Belonging (OpenStax 2021): membership in a group or place, granted or withheld by others — the social fact underneath every argument about who is "really" one of us.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Opening poll. "Who counts as 'one of us' on this campus?" — collect answers; hold them for the belonging frame.
8–22 Belonging is granted, not had. Define belonging; the class identifies who grants it in each poll answer.
22–36 The diaspora argument revisited. Read the published summary: what boundary is being drawn, and who is drawing it on whose behalf?
36–50 Nativism as a claim. Introduce nativism; ask where its logic appears in the argument over who is "authentically" part of a diaspora.
50–63 Two eras of the boundary. A World Alike (1990-02-15) poses "who is like us" — compare what the 1990 and 2026 framings include and exclude.
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 9 (a boundary-of-belonging analysis with a response).

Discussion questions

  1. What is the difference between being part of a group and being granted belonging to it?
  2. Who is doing the excluding in the diaspora argument — and what do they stand to gain?
  3. Nativism is a claim about who belongs. What makes it sound like a fact?
  4. Why does a diaspora complicate the nation-state's assumption that everyone has one "inside"?
  5. What changed between the 1990 framing of "who is like us" and the 2026 one?

Instructor notes

If the argument over "authentic" identity gets personal. Apply the protocol: no student adjudicates their own group. Return to the boundary question — who draws it, and with what effect.

If a student says "this is just semantics." Push back: boundaries of belonging decide who gets resources, recognition, and safety — "semantics" is where the stakes are hidden.

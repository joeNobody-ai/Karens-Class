Module 6 — Education and Unequal Opportunity

Big question. What does a college do when the money runs out — and who does it sort?

Where we start. We revisit Ep 9, this time reading Hillman's budget crisis as an education story rather than a poverty story: the school is a machine for sorting people, and a funding shortfall reveals whose access it is built to protect. We ask what a college is for when its resources are finite.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival (2026) Ep 9 revisited — Romancing the Unknown (the budget crisis) 2026 The crisis exposes the funding question at the heart of unequal opportunity
Original (1989) S2E41, Take This Job and Love It 1989-03-16 The work-versus-school tradeoff shows credentialing as a cost, not a gift

Sociological frame

  1. Education (OpenStax 2021): the formal system that transmits knowledge and credentials — and, sociologically, also sorts people into positions.
  2. Hidden curriculum (Jackson 1968): the unstated lessons of schooling — deference, punctuality, compliance — learned alongside the stated curriculum.
  3. School funding: the resources a school can draw on, historically tied to local wealth, so that where a student lives shapes what their school can offer.
  4. Credentialing (Collins 1979): the process by which degrees function as sorting devices — tickets to positions — more than as measures of what one has learned.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–8 Opening poll. "What is a college degree for?" — sort answers into "knowledge" and "ticket" columns; name the tension.
8–22 The hidden curriculum. Jackson defined; students list the unstated lessons of their own first year.
22–36 Funding and sorting. Lecture: how school funding ties to local wealth, and what that means for who a school can admit and keep.
36–50 The budget crisis revisited. Read the published summary of Romancing the Unknown: who is said to be affected first, and what does the organizing reveal?
50–63 Credentialing as a cost. Using Take This Job and Love It (1989-03-16), ask what a degree costs a working student beyond tuition.
63–75 Close and assign. Assign Homework 6 (a school-funding data exercise); announce the midterm release.

Discussion questions

  1. When the money runs out, who is sorted out first — and by what mechanism, if not by individual merit?
  2. What does the hidden curriculum teach that the stated curriculum does not?
  3. Is a degree a measure of learning or a ticket to a position? What evidence would distinguish the two?
  4. Why does school funding follow where people live, and what does that do to "equal opportunity"?
  5. What would a structural account of the budget crisis say that an individual account would miss?

Instructor notes

If a student says "Hillman just needs better management." Do not settle it; ask what a funding account adds — where the money comes from is prior to how well it is managed.

If the hidden curriculum exercise turns cynical. Reframe: the point is not that schooling is a trick, but that schooling teaches two curricula at once, and noticing both is the sociological move.

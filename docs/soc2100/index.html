A survey of the problems sociologists study — poverty, racial and gender inequality, health, education, family, crime, work, housing, media — each tested against a scene from A Different World, each asked as a question: who says this is a problem, and what would count as a solution?

The 15 modules

Module 1

The Sociological Study of Social Problems

What makes a condition a social problem — the condition, or the claim that it is one?

Module 2

Poverty and Economic Inequality

Is poverty a failure of people, or a feature of the structure — and how would you know?

Module 3

Racial Inequality

How does racial inequality persist even when nobody in the room is a racist?

Module 4

Gender Inequality

What does a pageant reveal about how gender inequality is produced and judged?

Module 5

Health, Illness, and Stigma

Why does a health crisis become a moral problem — and who pays for the label?

Module 6

Education and Unequal Opportunity

What does a college do when the money runs out — and who does it sort?

Module 7

Family Problems and Family Change

When families change, who calls it a problem — and what is actually changing?

Module 8

Crime, Deviance, and the Justice System

Who gets labelled a criminal, and what does the label do?

Module 9

Immigration and the Boundaries of Belonging

Who gets to belong, and who gets written out when a category is defined tightly?

Module 10

Media and the Construction of Social Problems

Does the media report problems, or manufacture them?

Module 11

Work, the Economy, and the Working Poor

Why do people who work hard still struggle — and whose problem is that?

Module 12

Cities, Housing, and the Color of Place

Why does where you live determine so much of how you live?

Module 13

Social Movements and Social Change

How does a grievance become a movement — and a movement become change?

Module 14

Intersectionality: Problems Overlap

What happens when race, class, and gender are the same problem, not three?

Module 15

Capstone: Naming a Problem, Proposing a Response

Can you name a problem precisely, and defend a response to it?

How to use this site

Students: start with the syllabus, then Module 1. Homework is due at the start of the following module and each assignment says exactly what to hand in and how it is marked.

Instructors: read the instructor guide before Module 1. Exam keys are in this repository under soc2100/exams/ and, if built, at instructor/index.html — deliberately not linked from the navigation.

Evidence rule

The whole course runs on keeping three things apart: documented facts (episode titles, dates, credits, published summaries), interpretations (readings of what a scene or season argues), and hypotheses (claims on the table for testing). Students are graded on it from week one. The reference site does the same thing, which is why homework points there.

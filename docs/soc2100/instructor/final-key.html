SOC 2100 — Social Problems · Final Examination — Answer Key

Do not distribute to students. Instructor material.

Part A — Short answers (10 points each)

1. Labeling theory

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definition: deviance is created by the social response; a label, once applied, sticks and reshapes identity and opportunities (Becker).
3 A documented anchor: the public call-out in O Captain! My Captain! (Ep 2) as stated in the published summary.
3 Application: someone names the transgression; the label does work beyond the act (reputation, standing, what follows).

Common errors: defining deviance as the act itself; inventing dialogue from the call-out; treating the label as a consequence of the act rather than of the response.

2. Moral panics and framing

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definitions: a moral panic is a disproportionate, media-driven definition of a threat (Cohen); framing is the selection/emphasis that tells an audience what kind of thing an event is (Goffman).
3 A documented anchor: the call-out (Ep 2) and The Show Must Go On (S1E16, 1988-02-18) with the original's air date.
3 Application: the incident becomes a "problem" through selection, amplification, and a frame — not merely through the event itself.

Common errors: using "moral panic" as a synonym for "overreaction" without the media-driven cycle; omitting the air date; treating framing and bias as the same thing.

3. Precarity

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definition: precarity is unstable, insecure work — no guaranteed hours, benefits, or safety net.
3 A documented anchor: Take This Job and Love It (S2E41, 1989-03-16) with its air date.
3 Application: the insecurity is in the arrangement (hours, pay, protections), not in the worker's effort — so working hard and being poor can coexist.

Common errors: blaming the worker's choices; defining precarity as mere low pay (it is instability, not just amount); no air date.

4. Neighborhood effects

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definition: a place itself shapes outcomes (schools, safety, networks, health) above and beyond individual traits (Wilson; Sampson).
3 A documented anchor: Hillman as a place (Ep 1) and Whitley's Last Supper (S4E73, 1990-10-11) with air date.
3 Application: the setting — campus, hall, household — does sorting and shaping work; "where" is a causal variable, not a backdrop.

Common errors: treating place as scenery; reducing the argument to individual mobility ("people can just move"); omitting the air date.

5. Resource mobilization

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definition: movements are explained by resources — money, people, organization, media — more than by grievance alone (McCarthy & Zald).
3 A documented anchor: the student organizing in Romancing the Unknown (Ep 9) and Radio Free Hillman (S2E32, 1989-01-05) with air date.
3 Application: a "voice" (a station, a platform) is itself a resource that lets a movement frame and coordinate.

Common errors: explaining the movement by passion/righteousness alone; omitting what the movement has; no air date.

6. Intersectionality

Points A full-credit answer must contain
4 Definition: race, class, gender, and other axes are mutually constituting — experienced together and misread when analyzed apart (Crenshaw).
3 A documented anchor: the full Ep 7 (Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You) as given in the published summary.
3 Application: a single-axis account of that episode makes the people at the intersections invisible; the axes compound, not add.

Common errors: treating intersectionality as a list of identities rather than an analytic claim about how they interact; claiming an axis "matters most"; inventing a scene detail.

Part B — Applied question (40 points)

Points A full-credit answer must contain
6 A precise name for the problem: the condition, who is claiming it is a problem, and what the account leaves out.
8 Two concepts — one from the first half, one from the second — each doing distinct analytic work (not named decoratively).
6 At least one documented fact about the chosen scene, kept separate from interpretation.
6 Interpretations clearly marked as the student's reading, with no fabricated dialogue or statistics.
6 One response defended: who it benefits, what it costs, and who bears the cost.
8 Evidence discipline and prose: tiers visible throughout; readable by a non-specialist; sources cited for something.

Common errors: a problem name that is a slogan rather than a claim; two concepts that do the same work; a response with no stated cost or beneficiary; an unmarked slide from documented fact to reading; fabricating a scene beat to support the argument.

Marker's note: this question tests synthesis, so award credit for holding concepts together, not for choosing the "right" problem. The automatic rewrite request applies to any answer that presents an interpretation as a documented fact or invents a statistic; return it once, ungraded, with the condition stated.

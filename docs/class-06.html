Session 6 — Generation, Memory, and "the Aunties"

Big question. How is a generation made — and who does the work of holding one together?

Where we are. Session 5 put a crisis in the middle of campus. Today the older generation arrives: the revival's sixth episode puts the students' night out in the same room as the women who used to be students, and the original introduces the professor who became one of its most durable characters.

Before class: viewing

Text Episode First aired Why this one
Revival Ep 6, Insomnia and the Daydreamer 2026-09-24 Deborah questions her "ideal partner"; a club night is wrecked by the aunties showing up
Original S2E23, Dr. War Is Hell 1988-10-06 Col. Bradford Taylor arrives as the professor students call "Dr. War"; the second-season intake introduces Kim Reese and Freddie Brooks to Whitley's dorm

Homework 5 is due at the start of this session. Read two of them aloud — the data-check homework is where the course's evidence rule either holds or doesn't.

Sociological frame

  1. Othermothering and kin work (Patricia Hill Collins, 1990; drawing on Black feminist scholarship generally): care that is organised through community relationships rather than the nuclear family. The "aunties" are not a joke about age; they are an institution with a job — custody of standards.
  2. Collective memory (Maurice Halbwachs, 1925/1950): groups remember through members, and memory is maintained by practice. The returning characters carry the original's events as living reference points; ask what happens to a story when its carriers are still in the room.
  3. Age and authority. In the original, Col. Taylor is the authority — the hard professor whose standards are the plot. In the revival, authority arrives socially (the auntie at the club) rather than officially. What has moved, and what has not?
  4. Generational transmission of advantage and expectation. What gets handed down: money, status, standards, wounds, and the pressure not to waste what someone else fought for.

The 75 minutes

Min What
0–10 Homework 5 read aloud, two students. Class checks: source, year, denominator. This is the drill that makes Session 14 possible
10–25 The pairing. Describe both episodes. Then list every way an older person shapes a younger one: teaching, judging, funding, embarrassing, protecting, remembering
25–40 Othermothering: why does the show place care in the community rather than the family or the state? Whose labour disappears from the frame?
40–55 Memory at work. Take the returning characters and list the original events they carry. For each, ask how the 2026 show uses a memory it does not have to explain
55–68 Authority comparison. Col. Taylor's classroom versus the aunties' club appearance. Same function — standards enforcement — different mechanism. Which one can a student refuse?
68–75 Assign homework 6. Preview Session 7: the diaspora argument, and the original's divestment episode

Discussion questions

  1. Why do "the aunties" arrive as a comic disruption and leave as an authority?
  2. What does a college campus do that a family cannot, and vice versa, in the work of raising a young adult?
  3. Col. Taylor is feared for his standards. Is fear the same thing as respect in an educational institution?
  4. If memory is carried by people rather than archives, what is lost when a show's original cast does not return?
  5. Deborah is told who her ideal partner is by a generation with a different marriage market. Whose standard is being applied?

Instructor notes

If the room enjoys the aunties as pure comedy. Take the enjoyment seriously and then ask what the joke is for. Comedy is a licence to enter a room where authority normally would not be welcome. Ask what the scene would look like with the same characters played straight — that is usually enough to show students that the humour is doing structural work.

If a student challenges othermothering as sentimental. Good. Push it: what does the concept explain that the nuclear-family frame misses, and what does it risk hiding — especially the way care work falls disproportionately on women? Collins' own work makes this critique.

If a student in the room is themselves an older student or a caregiver. Acknowledge the knowledge in the room without making anyone a case. The best version of this session ends with the class able to state the difference between valuing communal care and relying on it because nothing else is funded.

Where this session sets up later arguments. Authority-through-relationship (this session) is what makes Session 12's representation questions interesting and Session 13's family debate tractable. Say so, so the room sees the arc.

Content note. Social drinking, adult language, and relationship talk. No graphic content.

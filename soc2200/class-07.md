# Module 7 — Race and Class

**Big question.** Where do race and class meet — and where do they come apart?

**Where we start.** We revisit the Ep 9 budget crisis, now read as a class story: when the money runs
out, it does not run out evenly, and who can absorb the shock is a question of wealth, not just race —
though race and wealth are tangled. *Whitley's Last Supper* (1990) set a class contrast (old money vs.
working class) inside a Black campus, which is precisely where race and class refuse to line up neatly.

## Sociological frame

1. **Race–class intersection**: the way racial position and class position shape each other, so that
   neither can be read as a simple cause of the other.
2. **The racial wealth gap** (Oliver and Shapiro, *Black Wealth / White Wealth*, 1995): the persistent
   gap in assets — not income, but accumulated wealth — between racial groups, produced by policy and
   inheritance over generations.
3. **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw, "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex," 1989): the
   framework holding that overlapping identities produce experiences of advantage and disadvantage that
   cannot be understood by adding single categories together.
4. **Wealth vs. income**: income is a flow (what you earn); wealth is a stock (what you own and can
   pass on) — and the gap in the stock is the more durable one.

## Discussion questions

1. In the budget crisis, whose hardship is racial and whose is simply economic? Can you separate them?
2. Why is the wealth gap more durable than the income gap? What mechanism makes it persist?
3. What does *Whitley's Last Supper* prove about race that class alone cannot explain — and vice versa?
4. If race and class are tangled, what would a policy aimed at one but not the other miss?
5. What would you need to measure to know whether the wealth gap is closing?

## Instructor notes

**If a student reduces everything to class ("it's really about money").** Concede the kernel and then
ask: why does the money run along racial lines? The module exists to hold both, not to pick one.

**If a student reduces everything to race.** Same move, reversed: point at the class split *inside* the
Black campus in the throwback and ask how race alone explains it.

# Module 8 — Race and Gender

**Big question.** Why does race change how gender is done — and vice versa?

**Where we start.** We revisit the Ep 7 pageant, now read through gender: the standard being enforced
on Hazel is not just a beauty standard but a *gendered and raced* one — Black womanhood performed for
judgment. *Mr. Hillman* (1988) gives us the same intersection a generation earlier. The module's
central concept, intersectionality, says these are not two things added together but one fused thing.

## Sociological frame

1. **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw, 1989): the framework holding that race, gender, class, and other
   categories do not operate separately but interlock, producing distinct experiences of advantage and
   harm.
2. **Race and gender**: two systems of social organisation that are mutually constituting — each shapes
   how the other is lived and judged.
3. **Black feminist thought** (Collins, *Black Feminist Thought*, 1990): a body of theory built from
   Black women's experience that treats knowledge as situated and insists on analysing race and gender
   together.
4. **Controlling images** (Collins): stereotypes — the mammy, the matriarch, the jezebel, the sapphire —
   that discipline Black women by fixing what they are allowed to be.

## Discussion questions

1. What does the pageant standard demand of Black women that it does not demand of Black men?
2. Why is "race plus gender" the wrong formula for what the scene shows?
3. Which controlling image, if any, does the pageant summon — and how does a contestant resist it?
4. How does the raced standard change the *gendered* standard (or vice versa)?
5. What does Black feminist thought let you see in the scene that a single-axis reading would miss?

## Instructor notes

**If a student says "we've covered gender before — this is just race again."** That is the single-axis
reflex. Ask them to name one thing the standard demands that is *both at once*, and hold them to the
"fused, not added" test.

**If the controlling-images material draws discomfort.** Keep it in the third person: these are
historical images applied to a fictional scene, analysed, not re-performed. Number them on slides per
the vocabulary protocol.

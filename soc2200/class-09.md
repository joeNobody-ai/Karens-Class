# Module 9 — Race and Media Representation

**Big question.** Who controls the image of a race — and what does the image do?

**Where we start.** We revisit Deborah's public speech in Ep 2, now read as a problem of *representation*:
who gets to speak, whose image circulates, and what it costs to be seen. The module zooms out to the
machine that produces the images — a sitcom about a Black college is itself a representation, made by
specific people for a specific audience. *The Show Must Go On* (1988) lets us watch the original series
reflect on its own making.

## Sociological frame

1. **Representation** (Hall, ed., *Representation*, 1997): the production of meaning through images,
   language, and signs — not a mirror of reality but a way of *making* it.
2. **Encoding and decoding** (Hall, "Encoding and Decoding," 1973): producers encode meaning into a
   text, but audiences decode it in their own ways — dominant, negotiated, or oppositional.
3. **The gaze** (hooks, *Black Looks*, 1992): the power relation built into who is allowed to look at
   whom, and on what terms — including the "oppositional gaze" that looks back.
4. **Stereotypes**: simplified, repeated images of a group that reduce its members to a few fixed
   traits, and that circulate as if they were knowledge.

## Discussion questions

1. Who controls Deborah's image after she speaks — and what does that control *do*?
2. Encode/decode the speech: what is the likely dominant reading, and what is an oppositional one?
3. Where in the scene can you find the gaze — who is allowed to look at whom?
4. What is a stereotype *for*? What work does it do for the people who circulate it?
5. A sitcom about a Black college is itself a representation. Who made it, and what did they encode?

## Instructor notes

**If a student says "it's just a TV show, not reality."** Agree — and then do the sociology: the point
is that "just a TV show" is itself a meaning-making machine. Ask what the show *produces*, not whether
it reflects.

**If the room fixates on whether a stereotype is "true."** Reframe: stereotypes are not evaluated by
accuracy but by *function* — what they let people do (dismiss, control, flatten). Ask the function.

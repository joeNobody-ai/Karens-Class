# Module 5 — Immigration and the Racial Order

**Big question.** How does migration remake the boundaries of race and belonging?

**Where we start.** We revisit the Ep 7 diaspora argument, now read through migration: who is an
immigrant, who is a native, and who gets to count as belonging are questions a single scene can
complicate in seconds. *A World Alike* (1990) staged the same tangle a generation earlier, when the
"African versus African-American" line was being drawn in a different America.

## Sociological frame

1. **Immigration**: the movement of people across borders, which always carries a question about who is
   owed membership in the receiving society.
2. **Assimilation** (Gordon, *Assimilation in American Life*, 1964): the process by which newcomers are
   expected to adopt the receiving society's culture and, in the classic model, disappear into it.
3. **Diaspora**: a people dispersed from a homeland who maintain a sense of connection to it across
   generations and borders.
4. **Racialized nativism**: the fusion of racism and anti-immigrant hostility, by which "outsiders" are
   defined against a racial image of who the "real" members are.

## Discussion questions

1. In the scene, who is positioned as the immigrant — and by whom?
2. Is assimilation offered as belonging or demanded as erasure? What does the scene show?
3. What does diaspora give people that the nation-state's categories cannot?
4. How does nativism borrow the language of "who belongs" and racialise it?
5. If migration keeps remaking the racial order, why does that order feel so stable?

## Instructor notes

**If students treat "African" and "African-American" as natural categories.** Push the construction
point: both are *made* categories with histories; the scene is the making, not the reporting.

**If the room splits along identity lines.** Re-invoke norm 1 (no spokespeople) and redirect to the
text — ask what the *scene* treats as authentic, not what anyone in the room is.

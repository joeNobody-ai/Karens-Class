# Module 15 — Capstone: Racial Formation, Applied

**Big question.** Can you hold the whole course against one case and argue it?

**Where we start.** The season closes with *A Gift & The Curse*, in which the crisis, the relationships,
and the family situation all reach a breaking point and major decisions are made about the college's
future. The module asks you to do the whole course at once: hold racial formation, the state, class,
gender, and belonging against a single case and argue a claim. *A Rock, a River, a Lena* (1993) closes
the original series, and gives us a second case to set beside the first.

## Sociological frame

1. **Synthesis**: the act of holding multiple concepts together against one case, so that each does
   distinct work rather than decorating the same sentence.
2. **Racial formation** (Omi and Winant, 2014): the course's spine — the process by which racial
   categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed, returned to one last time.
3. **Public sociology** (Burawoy, "For Public Sociology," 2005): sociology that speaks beyond the
   seminar to a public it is accountable to — the discipline of making a claim a stranger can follow.

## The 75 minutes

| Min | What |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Settle and screen.** The finale's decision point, then the original series' close. Facts first. |
| 8–22 | **Reassemble the spine.** Re-state racial formation and the course's three tiers of evidence; check the room can still name them. |
| 22–36 | **One case, three concepts.** Model a synthesis: pick the college's future and run racial formation, the racial state, and class across it. |
| 36–50 | **Student synthesis, in pairs.** Each pair argues one claim about the finale using two concepts minimum, aloud, in four sentences. |
| 50–63 | **Public sociology.** Ask what the claim would have to look like to persuade a stranger who has never seen the show. |
| 63–75 | **Close.** Set the final, hand the self-assessment prompt, and close the term on the evidence rule. |

## Discussion questions

1. What single claim about race does the finale's decision point best support — and what is your evidence?
2. Which two concepts do the most *distinct* work on the finale? Where would a third be redundant?
3. How does the 1993 finale change your reading of the 2026 one — or not?
4. What would a stranger who never watched the show need to believe your claim? What would you owe them?
5. What is the strongest argument that this course's method — one show, fifteen concepts — cannot reach?

## Instructor notes

**If a student's synthesis is just a list of terms.** Return it once, ungraded, with the condition: each
concept must do distinct work on a specific moment, not ornament a summary.

**If the room is sentimental about the finale.** Honour it, then redirect: the capstone is not the
ending of a show but the closing of an argument. Ask what the ending *claims*, not how it feels.

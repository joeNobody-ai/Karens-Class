# Module 1 — Race as a Social Construction

**Big question.** If race is not biological, why are its consequences so real?

**Where we start.** The revival opens by returning to Hillman College, the fictional campus the
original series built in 1987 — a place that exists because a show decided it did. That is the whole
lesson in miniature: the category "race" is a human invention, and invented things still order who gets
in, who belongs, and who is seen as a problem. We open with the title's echo and the campus itself.

## Sociological frame

1. **Race** (Omi and Winant, *Racial Formation in the United States*, 2014): a concept that sorts human
   bodies into groups by real or imagined physical difference, and then treats those groups as if they
   were facts of nature.
2. **Social construction**: the process by which something made by people comes to look natural,
   inevitable, and beyond argument.
3. **The color line** (Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903): the durable boundary that divides
   society by race and that structures opportunity, contact, and belonging.
4. **Essentialism versus constructionism**: essentialism says a category has a fixed inner nature;
   constructionism says the category's meaning is produced by social arrangements and can change.

## The 75 minutes

| Min | What |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Settle and screen.** Show the revival's opening minutes and the original pilot's title card; ask students to name what they actually saw, no interpretation yet. |
| 8–22 | **Race has no biology.** Lecture: why geneticists find more variation within than between racial groups, and what that does (and does not) dissolve about race. |
| 22–36 | **The construction loop.** Define social construction; walk the class through one loop — a category is made, enforced, internalised, and then looks natural. |
| 36–50 | **The color line, applied.** Du Bois's line drawn across Hillman: who is inside the campus, who is imagined outside it, and what the line costs. |
| 50–63 | **Essentialism vs. constructionism debate.** Two short student arguments, each assigned a side, each forced to cite the screened scene. |
| 63–75 | **Close and assign.** Restate the big question; assign Homework 1 (your own "invented category" walk). |

## Discussion questions

1. If race has no biological basis, what exactly is the "difference" the show keeps marking?
2. What does the echo of *Reconcilable Differences* / *Irreconcilable Differences* tell us about what
   has and has not changed in forty years?
3. Where in the screened scene can you see a category being *made* rather than simply *described*?
4. What is the difference between saying "race is constructed" and saying "race is not real"?
5. Name one consequence of the color line that would persist even if everyone stopped believing in it
   tomorrow.

## Instructor notes

**If a student insists race is "just biological" because they can see it.** Do not argue biology;
redirect to the evidence rule. Ask what *documented* fact about the scene supports the claim, and
separate the observable (phenotype) from the meaning (the category built on top of it).

**If the room goes quiet when asked to name race aloud.** Hold the silence; remind them that the
vocabulary protocol means no one is asked to perform or define a term. Offer the terms from the frame
instead and let them point.

# Module 12 — Race and Family

**Big question.** How does race shape family — and who does the work of holding one together?

**Where we start.** In *Insomnia and the Daydreamer*, "the aunties" — the original cast — arrive, and
the family is shown not as a nuclear unit but as a network of people who show up. The module reads that
arrival sociologically: family is not one form but many, and in Black communities a great deal of the
holding-together is done by people who are not the parents. *Whitley's Last Supper* (1990) already put
the family — and its class tensions — at the table.

## Sociological frame

1. **Family forms**: the many actual shapes families take — nuclear, extended, single-parent, chosen —
   against a culture that treats one of them as "normal."
2. **Othermothering** (Collins, *Black Feminist Thought*, 1990): the practice, documented especially in
   Black communities, of women mothering children who are not biologically their own.
3. **Kinship**: the social ties of relatedness — by blood, marriage, or choice — that organise care,
   obligation, and belonging.
4. **The Moynihan debate**: the 1965 report that blamed Black family structure for Black poverty, and
   the long scholarly fight over whether it diagnosed pathology or pathologised a people.

## Discussion questions

1. What does "the aunties" arrival show that a nuclear-family lens would miss?
2. Is othermothering a deficit to be explained or a resource to be understood? How does the scene weigh in?
3. What is kinship *for* in the scene — and who is obligated to whom?
4. How does the Moynihan debate turn a description into a diagnosis? Where is the line?
5. If family is a network of care, what happens when the network is strained?

## Instructor notes

**If a student invokes "broken families" as an explanation.** Pause and flag the term as a claim, not a
fact; redirect to the evidence rule and ask what the *scene* documents about who provides care.

**If the Moynihan material gets tense.** Present the report and its critics symmetrically, as a debate
about *stories told about a people*, and keep the discussion on mechanisms, not moral verdicts.

# Module 4 — Encoding and Decoding

**Big question.** Why does a text mean different things to the people who made it and the people who watch it?

**Where we start.** Production (Module 3) is only half the story: a cultural object means nothing until
someone receives it. This module introduces Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model — the claim that a
message is *encoded* with a preferred meaning at production and *decoded* by audiences who may accept,
negotiate, or oppose it — and tests it on the revival's *O Captain! My Captain!*, whose published summary
states that Deborah speaks her mind in public and is called out. We pair it with the show-within-a-show
original *The Show Must Go On* (1988-02-18), where the gap between intended and received meaning is the
plot itself.

## Sociological frame

1. **Stuart Hall** (*Encoding/Decoding*, 1973): communication is not a straight pipe; meaning is
   produced at two moments — encoding (production) and decoding (reception) — that need not coincide.
2. **Encoding and decoding**: the producer inscribes a "preferred reading" through codes and
   conventions; the audience decodes through its own codes, yielding three positions.
3. **Polysemy**: the capacity of a sign or text to carry multiple meanings; texts are open to many
   readings, but not infinitely — structure constrains the range.
4. **Preferred reading**: the reading the text is organized to invite; Hall's three decoding positions
   are the **dominant-hegemonic** (accept), the **negotiated** (accept in part, resist in part), and the
   **oppositional** (reject the frame outright).

## Discussion questions

1. The published summary says Deborah is "called out." Does "called out" itself assume a preferred reading — that the speech was wrong or right? What would an oppositional reading of the *call-out* look like?
2. Hall's three positions assume a text has a dominant meaning to accept or resist. What if a text is so polysemous it has no clear preferred reading? Is that liberating or just vague?
3. How do the codes a producer uses (genre, casting, music) steer decoding without determining it? Name one documented convention from the case.
4. In *The Show Must Go On*, who encodes and who decodes? Are the students inside the story producers, audiences, or both?
5. What would a *negotiated* decoding of Deborah's public speech look like — accept in part, resist in part — using only the published summary?

## Instructor notes

**If the room treats decoding as "everyone has their own opinion."** That flattens Hall into relativism.
His point is that readings are *socially structured* — they come from codes and positions, not whims.
Ask: which readings are *available* to which viewers, and why?

**If a student invents dialogue to prove a reading.** Stop and apply the evidence rule: we have the
published summary, not a transcript. The reading must rest on documented facts; if the fact is missing,
say "the published summary states…" and argue from that.

# Module 10 — Media Effects and Moral Panics

**Big question.** Does media change behavior, or does it change what we fear?

**Where we start.** Every generation worries that media will corrupt the young. This module asks whether
that worry is itself the phenomenon: does media *cause* behavior, or does the *framing* of media — and of
the people who consume it — produce a panic that is itself a social fact? We revisit the revival's
*O Captain! My Captain!* (the published summary states that Deborah speaks her mind in public and is
called out) and pair it with *Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1)* (1992-09-24), an original that aired months
after the 1992 Los Angeles uprising — a fact the reference card documents under the "Rodney King
precedent." The juxtaposition is deliberate: a speech that gets "called out," and a city whose media
image became the story.

## Sociological frame

1. **Media effects**: the long tradition asking whether media directly cause attitudes and behavior —
   from "hypodermic" models to limited-effects and cultivation accounts; the course's job is to weigh
   the tradition, not to assume it.
2. **Moral panics** (Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*, 1972): an episode of exaggerated
   public alarm in which a group is cast as a threat ("folk devils") and the alarm itself becomes the
   story — Cohen's question is who panics, and what the panic does.
3. **Cohen**: the sociologist who named the moral panic and showed that panics are *periodic* and
   *patterned* — they recur around different targets while keeping the same shape.
4. **Framing** (Erving Goffman, *Frame Analysis*, 1974): the selection and emphasis that turns an event
   into "a story of this kind" — what is in frame, what is out of frame, and who controls the frame.

## Discussion questions

1. Cohen showed that moral panics recur with the same shape around different targets. What is the *shape*, and what would a 2026 panic about student speech have in common with a 1992 panic about a city?
2. The published summary says Deborah is "called out." Is a call-out a moral panic in miniature, or a legitimate use of public speech to answer public speech? What distinguishes the two?
3. "Framing" selects and emphasizes. For *Honeymoon in L.A. (part 1)* (1992-09-24), what is in the frame by the title alone, and what is out of it? Mark your answer as interpretation.
4. The effects tradition wants to prove media *causes* behavior. What would conclusive evidence even look like, and why is it so hard to produce? Name the methodological problem.
5. If media mostly changes *fear* rather than behavior, who benefits from that fear, and who pays for it? Argue from the two cases.

## Instructor notes

**If a student claims media "makes people" do something.** Push for the mechanism and the tier: which
claim is documented (the air date, the title, the call-out) and which is hypothesis (a causal effect)?
Do not let "media effects" stand as a self-evident premise.

**If the L.A. material is met with silence.** Honor the silence; do not fill it. Offer the documented
facts again, slowly, and let the framing question do the work. The point is the *media* story, not a
re-litigation of the event.

# Module 13 — Memory, Nostalgia, and the Callback

**Big question.** What does it mean for a new show to keep calling back to an old one?

**Where we start.** The revival does not simply continue the story; it *remembers* it — the reference
card documents the "39 years" between the original's debut and the revival, and the revival's first
episode is titled *Irreconcilable Differences*, echoing the original's S1E1 *Reconcilable Differences*
(1987-09-24). This module asks what a *callback* is sociologically: an act of collective memory, an
instrument of nostalgia, and an intertextual move that asks the audience to hold two texts at once. We
pair the revival's opening with the original *Gift of the Magi* (1987-12-17) as a text the revival might
call back to.

## Sociological frame

1. **Collective memory** (Maurice Halbwachs, 1925): memory is not individual recall but a social
   achievement — groups reconstruct the past in the present, selectively, for present purposes.
2. **Nostalgia** (Svetlana Boym, 2001): a longing for a lost time — in Boym's terms, *restorative*
   (which wants to rebuild the past) versus *reflective* (which dwells on the distance itself).
3. **The callback**: a device that references an earlier text or moment, asking the audience to
   recognize and supply the memory — it *hails* a knowing audience and rewards accumulated history.
4. **Intertextuality** (Julia Kristeva, 1980): the principle that every text is built from other texts;
   a "new" work is always a tissue of prior ones, and meaning is produced in the relations among them.

## Discussion questions

1. Halbwachs says groups reconstruct the past for present purposes. What present purpose does the revival's title-echo serve, and for whom?
2. Boym's *restorative* nostalgia wants to rebuild; *reflective* nostalgia dwells on loss. Which better describes a revival that recasts the next generation (Deborah) rather than the originals? Defend the choice.
3. A callback requires a knowing audience. What happens to a viewer who lacks the memory — are they excluded, or is their confusion itself the point?
4. Intertextuality says nothing is fully new. If the revival is "a tissue" of the original, is it still a *distinct* text? What makes it one?
5. The 39-years card documents the gap. Is forty years a *natural* span for a revival, or is the gap itself doing ideological work — and what work?

## Instructor notes

**If nostalgia gets romanticized (the past was better).** Re-anchor in Boym: nostalgia has a politics —
it can be critical or restorative, and "restoring" the past can serve present power. Ask which kind is on
display, not whether the past was good.

**If a student "remembers" a detail that isn't documented.** Apply the tiers: personal memory is not a
documented fact. Check it against the reference site; if it isn't there, label it interpretation and move
on.

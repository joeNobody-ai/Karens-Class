# Module 9 — Music, Performance, and Identity

**Big question.** How does a step routine or a theme song carry identity?

**Where we start.** Words (Module 8) are one register of identity; bodies and music are another, and they
carry what words often cannot. This module revisits the homecoming step of *Center de Stage* and its 1988
throwback *A Stepping Stone* (1988-12-01), reading the step — a percussive, synchronized, embodied
tradition associated with historically Black Greek-letter organizations — as a *performance* in the
sociological sense: a doing that constitutes the identity it appears merely to express. We pair the step
with the show's own musical identity: the reference card documents the revival's theme song (the "Brandy"
theme).

## Sociological frame

1. **Performance** (Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self*, 1959): social life as enacted — identity
   is not a fixed interior revealed by action but something *done* in front of others.
2. **The step** (Elizabeth Fine, *Soulstepping*, 2003): a synchronized, percussive, chanted-and-stepped
   tradition rooted in historically Black Greek-letter organizations — a coded display of history,
   affiliation, and discipline.
3. **Music and identity** (Simon Frith; Tia DeNora): music does not merely reflect a pre-existing
   identity; listening and performing *make* and *manage* identity — a theme song is a claim about who
   "we" are.
4. **Embodied culture**: knowledge and identity carried in the body — rhythm, movement, posture — that
   cannot be fully transferred as text; the step is culture you must *be* to transmit.

## Discussion questions

1. Goffman's "performance" is often misread as insincerity. How does the step — where discipline and repetition are the point — correct that misreading?
2. The step is documented as a tradition. What would be lost if it were written down instead of stepped? What does that tell you about *embodied* versus *textual* culture?
3. A "stepping stone" (the original's title, 1988-12-01) names becoming, not being. What does reading the step as *ritual of becoming* change about how we understand it?
4. A theme song is heard by everyone, but only some can *perform* the step. What is the difference between *listening to* and *doing* culture, for identity?
5. If performance constitutes identity, who controls the script — the performer or the tradition? Use the step and the theme song as your two cases.

## Instructor notes

**If the room reduces the step to "impressive choreography."** Re-anchor: the question is not whether it
is skilled but what identity the skill *constitutes* — affiliation, lineage, discipline. Ask what a
viewer who misses the code misses.

**If a student wants to demonstrate or imitate the step.** Redirect, consistent with the vocabulary
protocol's spirit: we analyze the documented practice, we do not perform it. Offer the reference card
instead.

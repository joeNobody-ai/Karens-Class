# Module 12 — Cultural Capital and Taste

**Big question.** How does what you like become who you are — and who you are not?

**Where we start.** Taste feels private — "I just like this." Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) argues the
opposite: taste is socially produced, it runs along class lines, and it *does* boundary work, sorting
people by the very preferences they experience as personal. This module revisits the homecoming of
*Center de Stage* through the lens of taste and status — the published summary states that Dwayne and
Whitley return with visible tensions in their marriage — and pairs it with *Whitley's Last Supper*
(1990-10-11), whose title (a reading, ours) stages taste as a dinner-party affair: hosting, refinement,
and the work of distinction.

## Sociological frame

1. **Bourdieu** (Pierre Bourdieu, *Distinction*, 1979): taste is not free preference but the product of
   one's position in social space — it is *distinction* in both senses: refined judgment and social
   separation.
2. **Taste**: the embodied system of preferences — in food, music, décor, partners — that classifies the
   classifier; we reveal our position by what we claim to like.
3. **Cultural capital**: the stock of legitimate knowledge, tastes, and dispositions one possesses —
   institutionalized (degrees), objectified (books, art), and *embodied* (accent, ease, know-how) — that
   converts into advantage.
4. **Distinction**: the mechanism by which taste draws and polices boundaries — the aesthetic judgment
   ("vulgar," "classy") is a social judgment about *who belongs*.

## Discussion questions

1. Bourdieu says taste classifies the classifier. What would a sociologist need to *observe* — not assume — to test that claim in this case? Name the data you'd want.
2. The published summary says the marriage shows "visible tensions." Is a marital tension a *taste* difference, a *status* difference, or neither? What distinguishes those readings?
3. A dinner party (the original's title, our reading) makes taste public. Why is *hosting* such a pure site of distinction — what is being evaluated, and by whom?
4. Embodied cultural capital is carried in the body: accent, ease, posture. Where in the homecoming do you see embodied capital doing boundary work, on the documented evidence?
5. If taste is produced by position, can anyone *learn* legitimate taste — or does that very question smuggle in a distinction? Explain.

## Instructor notes

**If the room insists taste is just personal.** Don't argue from authority; run the test. Ask them to
locate their own preference socially (who shares it, who scorns it, what it costs to signal). Let the
pattern do Bourdieu's work.

**If a student attributes taste to a character's "personality."** That is a pre-sociological explanation.
Ask: what *shaped* that personality's preferences — and what does the preference *do* for the character's
standing? The question is social, not psychological.

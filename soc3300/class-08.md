# Module 8 — Language, Slang, and Identity

**Big question.** What does the way a community talks reveal about who it is and who belongs?

**Where we start.** Language is the most ordinary evidence of the social: who may say what to whom, in
what register, with what consequence. This module revisits the revival's *Sometimes You Get the
Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You* through its language-and-belonging thread — the published
summary states that Kojo is caught up in arguments over African/diaspora identity — and pairs it with the
original *A World Alike* (1990-02-15), whose very title (a reading, ours) frames sameness and difference
as its subject. The discipline here matters as much as the theory: all talk *about* the vernacular runs
through the course's vocabulary protocol and `lingo.md`.

## Sociological frame

1. **Language**: not a neutral code for pre-existing thoughts but a social practice that marks membership,
   status, and boundary — a way of *doing* identity, not just expressing it.
2. **Code-switching** (John Gumperz; Jan Blommaert): the practice of shifting between varieties or
   registers according to context and audience — competence in *when* to switch, not just in two codes.
3. **The vocabulary of race** (Geneva Smitherman, 1994; Rickford and Rickford, 2000): the specific
   lexicon through which a community names itself — documented as period vocabulary in `lingo.md`, never
   claimed as dialogue from the show.
4. **Linguistic capital** (Pierre Bourdieu, *Language and Symbolic Power*, 1991): the value a way of
   speaking carries in a given "linguistic market" — some registers command authority, others are
   devalued, and speakers are unequally positioned to profit from their speech.

## Discussion questions

1. Code-switching can be read as skill or as survival. What decides which reading fits a given instance — and can it be both at once?
2. The published summary says the diaspora argument catches Kojo. Why would *language* be the site where African and diaspora identity get argued, rather than ancestry or politics?
3. Bourdieu's "linguistic market" asks who profits from a way of speaking. What is the currency at Hillman, and who holds it, on the documented evidence?
4. A title like *A World Alike* (1990-02-15) invites a viewer to expect sameness — or to expect sameness to be *tested*. Which reading better fits a show about a historically Black college, and why?
5. The vocabulary protocol forbids anyone from "performing" a term. What is the *sociological* argument for that rule — what does performing a term do to the analysis?

## Instructor notes

**If a student slips into performing the vernacular (or asks a classmate to).** Hold the line gently and
by name: "we discuss terms by number, not by performance." Offer the numbered term from `lingo.md`
instead. This is accuracy, not delicacy — say so.

**If the room wants to know "what a word really means."** Send it to the cited sources, not to the
nearest student. Definitions come from Smitherman, Rickford & Rickford, the OED — and no one in the room
is the consultant on their own language.

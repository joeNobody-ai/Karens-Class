# Module 14 — Culture as Resistance

**Big question.** When is a cultural act a political act?

**Where we start.** Culture is not only what a group *has* or *does* (Module 1) — it can be what a group
*uses to push back*. This module revisits the revival's *Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the
Culture Gets You* through its resistance thread: the published summary states that Kojo is caught up in
arguments over African/diaspora identity — an argument that is at once cultural and political. We connect
it to the student organizing the revival later depicts (documented in the summary of *Romancing the
Unknown*: "students organize" around the budget crisis), and to the original *A World Alike*
(1990-02-15), whose title (a reading, ours) frames sameness and difference as the terrain of contest.

## Sociological frame

1. **Counter-culture** (Dick Hebdige, *Subculture*, 1979): a group's styling of itself *against* a
   dominant culture — style as a coded refusal that mainstream culture then absorbs or stigmatizes.
2. **Resistance** (James C. Scott, *Weapons of the Weak*, 1985): not only open revolt but everyday,
   low-visibility acts — foot-dragging, parody, refusal of terms — through which the less powerful push
   back without announcing it.
3. **Cultural politics**: the contest over meaning itself as a political struggle — who gets to define
   the group, the past, the "we" — which is what diaspora arguments are fundamentally about.
4. **Diaspora** (Paul Gilroy, *The Black Atlantic*, 1993; Stuart Hall): identity as formed through
   dispersal, not rooted fixity — a "route" rather than a "root," which reframes belonging as an ongoing
   negotiation rather than a birthright.

## Discussion questions

1. Hebdige says subcultural style is a "coded refusal" that mainstream culture then absorbs. Where, in the diaspora argument, do you see refusal — and where do you see the risk of absorption?
2. Scott's "weapons of the weak" are low-visibility. What would *everyday* resistance look like in a campus argument over identity, as opposed to open protest?
3. The published summary says the argument is over "African/diaspora identity." What is the difference between *root* and *route* models of identity, and why is that difference politically loaded?
4. Student organizing (documented in the later episode) is collective and explicit. Is that still *culture*, or has it become *politics* — and what, if anything, turns the one into the other?
5. Is a claim about *who belongs* ever merely cultural? Defend your answer with a case from the module.

## Instructor notes

**If the room reduces resistance to protest marches.** Re-anchor in Scott: much resistance is
low-visibility and everyday. Ask what a *cultural* refusal looks like when it does not take the form of a
rally.

**If a student asserts what a character "really believes" politically.** We do not have interiority in
the record. Redirect to the documented argument and its stakes; label any inference about motive as
interpretation.

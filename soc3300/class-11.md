# Module 11 — Audiences and Reception

**Big question.** What do audiences do with a text that its makers never intended?

**Where we start.** Encoding (Module 4) meets its other half: reception. This module shifts from what a
text *means* to what audiences *do* with it — the active-audience tradition that refuses to treat viewers
as passive recipients. The revival's *Insomnia and the Daydreamer* gives us two audience-shaped facts:
the published summary states that Deborah questions whether her imagined "ideal partner" is right for
her, and that a club night goes chaotic when "the aunties" (the original cast) arrive. We pair it with
*Dr. War Is Hell* (1988-10-06), whose title (a reading, ours) frames conflict as its subject. The
question throughout: who is doing the interpreting, and what does the *arrival of the original audience*
do to the text?

## Sociological frame

1. **Reception**: the study of how audiences *make meaning* of a text — reading is an active production
   of meaning, not a decoding of a fixed message.
2. **Fandom** (Henry Jenkins, *Textual Poachers*, 1992): audiences that do not merely consume but
   *appropriate* — reworking, extending, and claiming a text the makers never fully controlled.
3. **Interpretive communities** (Stanley Fish, 1980): groups whose shared strategies of reading make
   meanings converge — the same text means differently to different communities because they read
   differently.
4. **The active audience** (Ien Ang, 1985; Janice Radway, 1984): the view that viewers are never blank;
   they negotiate, resist, and use texts for their own purposes — a corrective to the effects tradition.

| Min | What |
|---|---|

| 8–22 | **From decoder to poacher.** Bridge Hall's decoding to Jenkins's fandom: an audience that *accepts* is one position, but audiences also *appropriate*. Distinguish reading against the grain from reworking the text. |
| 22–36 | **The imagined ideal partner.** The published summary states Deborah questions whether her imagined partner is right for her. Ask: what is the "imagined" here — the product of whose reading? What does it mean for an audience to *daydream* a text into something new? |

## Discussion questions

1. Jenkins's fan "poaches" a text the makers never controlled. Is Deborah's imagined ideal partner an act of poaching, or of submission to the text's preferred reading? What distinguishes the two?
2. The original cast's arrival makes the *audience* into *content*. What does that collapse do to the boundary between text and reader?
3. Fish says meaning is a property of a community's reading strategies, not of the text alone. What two communities meet in this episode, and what would each read differently?
4. Radway and Ang argued audiences *use* texts for their own ends. What "use" is a daydream, and is it a legitimate object for sociology?
5. If the audience is active, is the maker's intended meaning simply irrelevant? Or does encoding (Module 4) still constrain what audiences can do? Reconcile the two.

## Instructor notes

**If the room celebrates the "active audience" into meaninglessness.** Tax it: active does not mean
unconstrained. Ask what the *text* still refuses to let a reader do — where encoding pushes back. Hall and
Jenkins both insist on limits.

**If a student reports private viewing habits as evidence.** Fine as data, but flag the tier: "I and my
friends felt X" is an anecdote, not a documented fact about an audience. Ask how one would *test* it.

# Module 5 — Representation and the Gaze

**Big question.** Who is looking at whom, and who gets to be the one looking?

**Where we start.** Representation is not a mirror: media do not reflect people, they *construct* them
within regimes of looking. This module turns to the second half of the revival's *Sometimes You Get the
Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You*, whose published summary states that Hazel runs for Miss
Gilbert Hall while the students also deal with sexual health and relationships, and pairs it with the
original pageant episode *Mr. Hillman* (1988-02-25). A pageant is the ideal case for this question: a
structured scene of being looked at, with judges, an audience, and a title at stake.

## Sociological frame

1. **Representation** (Stuart Hall, 1997): the production of meaning through language and images — how a
   group is *made to appear*, always within power relations, never a simple reflection of reality.
2. **The gaze** (Laura Mulvey, 1975; adapted): the structuring of looking by power — who looks, who is
   looked at, and who controls the terms of display.
3. **Display**: the act of presenting oneself to be seen — which can be an assertion of agency or a
   submission to another's terms, depending on who sets the frame.
4. **Being seen versus being watched** (John Berger, 1972; bell hooks, 1992): the difference between
   appearing as a *subject* who acts and being positioned as an *object* of another's look — hooks's
   "oppositional gaze" names the refusal to accept that positioning.

## Discussion questions

1. The published summary states Hazel runs for the title. Is "running" an act of agency or an entry into a structure she does not control — or both? What does your answer turn on?
2. Berger's "men act and women appear" was written in 1972. Does it travel to a pageant whose participants are, in these cases, at a historically Black college? What must be adjusted?
3. hooks's oppositional gaze argues that looking back is a form of resistance. Where, in a pageant, could such a look even happen?
4. The second half also involves "sexual health and relationships" per the summary. How does that content connect to the question of who is watched and who is seen?
5. What is the difference between a pageant and a talent show (Module 2's homecoming) as *regimes of looking*? Use documented facts from both.

## Instructor notes

**If the room collapses "the gaze" into "men look at women."** Mulvey's account is gendered and
heteronormative in its original form; broaden, don't drop it. The question here is *any* structure where
one party looks and another is displayed. Keep the concept and widen the cases.

**If a student asserts how a character "feels" about being watched.** We do not have that in the record.
Redirect to what is shown (the campaign, the title, the judging) and ask what the *structure* implies,
clearly labeled as interpretation.

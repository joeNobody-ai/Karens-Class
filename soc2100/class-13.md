# Module 13 — Social Movements and Social Change

**Big question.** How does a grievance become a movement — and a movement become change?

**Where we start.** We revisit the student organizing in Eps 2 and 9: a campus under pressure turns a shared complaint into collective action. We use it to study the machinery of movements — how a grievance is *framed*, how resources are *mobilized*, and what separates a movement that changes things from one that does not.

## Sociological frame

1. **Social movements** (McCarthy & Zald 1977): organized, sustained collective efforts to promote or resist change — distinct from one-off outbursts.
2. **Framing** (Snow & Benford 1988): the work of defining a grievance, attributing blame, and proposing a remedy in a way that recruits others.
3. **Resource mobilization** (McCarthy & Zald 1977): the account of movements that centers *resources* — money, people, organization, media — over grievance alone.
4. **Change** (OpenStax 2021): the alteration of social arrangements — whose *direction and pace* a movement's success is measured against.

## Discussion questions

1. Why are grievances everywhere but movements rare? What is the missing ingredient?
2. What is a *frame*, and why does a movement live or die by its frame?
3. What resources does a student movement have, and which does it lack?
4. *Radio Free Hillman* (1989-01-05) treats "a voice" as a resource. What does that voice let a movement do?
5. How would you know a movement has "worked"? Name the outcome you would measure.

## Instructor notes

**If the room romanticizes movements.** Re-anchor to resource mobilization: movements are *organized* and *resourced*, not spontaneous. Ask what any successful movement had to assemble.

**If students disagree about whether the campus organizing "worked."** Keep it empirical: what would count as success, and what does the evidence (the summaries) actually show?

# Module 12 — Cities, Housing, and the Color of Place

**Big question.** Why does where you live determine so much of how you live?

**Where we start.** We revisit Ep 1, reading Hillman not as a plot but as a *place* — a campus, a hall, a neighborhood — and asking what that place does to the people inside it. Housing and segregation are the quietest problems in the course, and the most consequential, because they sort people before any other problem can begin.

## Sociological frame

1. **Segregation** (Massey & Denton 1993): the separation of groups across space — residential and otherwise — that concentrates advantage and disadvantage by place.
2. **Housing** (OpenStax 2021): the stock of shelter and the *market and policy* that allocate it — who can live where, and on what terms.
3. **Neighborhood effects** (Wilson 1987; Sampson 2012): the ways a place itself shapes outcomes — schools, safety, networks, health — above and beyond any individual's traits.
4. **Place** (Sampson 2012): the durable, located character of a setting — its institutions and histories — that makes "where" a social fact, not a backdrop.

## Discussion questions

1. What exactly does a place *do* to a person, beyond what the person does there?
2. Why does segregation concentrate advantage rather than just sort people neutrally?
3. Is Hillman a "place" or a "neighborhood"? What turns on the distinction?
4. What would "equal opportunity" mean in a system where places are unequal?
5. *Whitley's Last Supper* (1990-10-11) treats a home as a site of strain. What does that say about housing as a social problem?

## Instructor notes

**If the room treats place as mere scenery.** Push the neighborhood-effects point: the same person, moved to a different place, faces different schools, networks, and odds — "where" is a causal variable.

**If a student says "people just need to move."** Ask who can afford to move, and to where — mobility is itself distributed by place.

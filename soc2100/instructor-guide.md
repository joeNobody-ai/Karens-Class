# Instructor Guide — SOC 2100

This guide is for you, not for students. It assumes you have taught a survey course before and are looking for how *this* course is built and where it needs care.

## Why this course works (and what it is not)

The course works because every social problem is taught through one concrete, repeatable object — *A Different World* in two eras — rather than through a stack of disconnected "issue weeks." The same institution, Hillman College, appears across 1987 and 2026, so students can watch the *claim* change while the *condition* stays recognizably the same. That single contrast does more constructionist work in a term than any textbook example.

What it is not: it is not a media-studies course, and it is not a fan course. The series is evidence, not the object of affection. We never ask students whether they "like" a character or "agree with" a plot; we ask what a scene documents and what a viewer reads into it. It is also not a course that assigns the instructor to "fix" social problems — the course's discipline is description and claim-testing, and you should model that discipline even when (especially when) a student wants you to declare the right answer.

## Before the term starts

- Read the catalog file for this course (`data/soc2100_catalog.json`) and confirm each module's episode, original(s), concepts, and anchors against the reference site. The verifier checks the prose against it.
- Open the reference site (https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/) and read the cards your modules cite. You are the person in the room who can say "here is what the summary actually states."
- Review `lingo.md` and `rubrics.md`; both are shared with the upper-division seminar and you should use the same discipline.
- Pre-screen every episode yourself. The published summaries name some content but not all of it; a scene that is a footnote in the summary can be a hard conversation in the room. Know it before you show it.
- Verify any statistic you plan to cite against a primary source. The course flags figures as "check before you teach" for a reason.

## The evidence rule (three tiers)

Every claim in the room is one of three kinds, and you enforce the separation out loud, every week, until it is automatic:

| Tier | What it is | Example | How it is handled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **documented** | An episode title, air date, credit, or published summary | "The 2026 premiere is titled *Irreconcilable Differences*" | Look-up-able; state it plainly |
| **interpretation** | A reading of what a scene or season argues | "The title frames the breakup as unfixable" | Label it as a reading |
| **hypothesis** | A claim put on the table to test | "The revival frames problems more structurally than the original did" | Held open, tested against evidence |

The single most important habit: when a student states an interpretation as fact, you do not correct them — you ask "is that documented, or is that your reading?" until they learn to do it themselves. Fabricated dialogue and invented statistics are the two things this course will not tolerate, and the evidence rule is how we prevent them without ever saying "you can't say that."

## The hard conversations

This course is about race, class, gender, health, policing, immigration, and housing, taught to a room of people who do not share one position on any of them. The norms below are the floor, not aspirational.

**Norm 0 — the vocabulary protocol.** Two rules, stated on day one and never relaxed: the instructor does not perform the vernacular, and no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term. Definitions come from the cited sources (see `lingo.md`), the "handle with care" terms are numbered on slides and discussed by number, and nobody in the room is ever the consultant on their own language.

**No one is a spokesperson.** A student speaks for themselves. "As a [member of a group]…" is gently returned to "as yourself."

**Separate the claim from the claimant.** We argue about accounts of a problem, not about whether a student is a good person for holding one.

**Handle the "deficit account" reflex.** The course's through-line is structural versus individual explanation. When a student reaches for "people just need to…", do not veto it — ask what the structural account would add, and what each account explains and leaves out.

**Screen content warnings are structural, not optional.** Say them plainly and in advance (see the syllabus content note), and honor the opt-out without requiring explanation.

## Running the room on screened scenes

Screen short scenes, not whole episodes, and always with a question on the board before the lights go down. The 75-minute plan in each module is a real plan: the opening poll, the close reading, and the close-and-assign are load-bearing, not filler. When discussion stalls, return to the documented fact — "what does the summary actually say?" — because that question is almost always answerable and almost always re-opens the room.

## Adaptation notes

- The module timing assumes you can show clips. If you cannot screen in class, assign the viewing as homework and use the segment time for the same close reads from the reference cards.
- The midterm is released after Module 7 and due before Module 9; do not let it slip, or the second half of the term crowds the capstone.
- The capstone (Module 15) needs runway: remind students by Module 12 that it asks them to name a problem and defend a response, so their earlier homework is raw material.
- If your institution has no formal accessibility office, the accommodation policy still stands; you make the alternative, quietly, when a student asks.

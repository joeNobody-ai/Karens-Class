## Part A — Short answers (6 × 10 = 60 points)

Answer each in a paragraph, defining the concept and applying it to the named, documented episode fact.

1. **Labeling theory.** Define *labeling theory* (Becker) and apply it to the public call-out in *O Captain! My Captain!* (2026, Ep 2). Who is doing the labeling, and what does the label do beyond the act itself?

2. **Moral panics and framing.** Define *moral panic* (Cohen) and *framing* (Goffman), and use both to explain how an incident becomes a "problem" in the call-out (Ep 2), drawing on *The Show Must Go On* (S1E16, 1988-02-18) for the machinery of public staging.

3. **Precarity.** Define *precarity* and use *Take This Job and Love It* (S2E41, 1989-03-16) to explain why a person can work hard and still be poor. What is it about the *job*, not the worker, that produces insecurity?

4. **Neighborhood effects.** Define *neighborhood effects* (Wilson; Sampson) and apply the idea to Hillman as a *place* (2026, Ep 1), contrasting with *Whitley's Last Supper* (S4E73, 1990-10-11). What does a setting do to the people in it?

5. **Resource mobilization.** Define *resource mobilization* (McCarthy & Zald) and apply it to the student organizing in *Romancing the Unknown* (2026, Ep 9), drawing on *Radio Free Hillman* (S2E32, 1989-01-05) for what a "voice" is as a resource.

6. **Intersectionality.** Define *intersectionality* (Crenshaw) and apply it to the full episode *Sometimes You Get the Culture...Sometimes the Culture Gets You* (2026, Ep 7). What does a single-axis account of that episode miss?

## Part B — Applied question (40 points)

Name one social problem shown in a single 2026 scene from anywhere in the season, and answer:

**How should this problem be understood, and what should be done about it?**

Your essay must: (a) name the problem precisely — the condition, who is claiming it is a problem, and what your account leaves out; (b) explain it using two concepts, one from the first half of the course and one from the second, each doing distinct work; (c) state at least one documented fact about the scene; (d) mark your interpretations as interpretations; and (e) defend one response, stating who it would benefit, what it would cost, and who bears that cost.

(part 1)* (S6E120, 1992-09-24); *A Rock, a River, a Lena* (S6E141, 1993-07-02).

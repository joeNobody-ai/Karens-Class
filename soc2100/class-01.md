# Module 1 — The Sociological Study of Social Problems

**Big question.** What makes a condition a social problem — the condition, or the claim that it is one?

**Where we start.** Deborah Wayne begins her freshman year at Hillman hoping to start fresh, only to find an old boyfriend already there — a private problem that the episode's title invites us to read against a public one. We open the course by asking what turns a private trouble into a *social* problem, and we read the 2026 revival against its 1987 namesake to see how the same institution gets framed as the site of a problem across thirty-nine years.

## Sociological frame

1. **Social problems** (Mills 1959): a condition people identify as harmful and as something that ought to be changed, distinguished from a merely *personal trouble* by its scale and its causes.
2. **Constructionism** (Spector & Kitsuse 1977): the view that social problems are produced through claims-making — the work of naming a condition, attributing blame, and demanding action — rather than existing as self-evident facts.
3. **Claims-making** (Best 2021): the rhetorical and organizational activity of asserting that some condition is a problem; the object of study for constructionists.
4. **Structure and agency** (Giddens 1984): the two-sided question of how much a condition follows from durable arrangements (structure) versus people's choices (agency) — and how the two produce each other.

## Discussion questions

1. Whose job is it to decide that a condition is a "social problem" — the people living it, or the people describing it?
2. The 2026 title says *Irreconcilable*; the 1987 title says *Reconcilable*. What changed in how the show frames the same family situation?
3. Is a "fresh start" a choice (agency) or an arrangement (structure)? Defend one reading, then the other.
4. What would a constructionist ask about the phrase "social problems" itself?
5. Where in the published summary of Ep 1 do we see a claim being made, and where do we see a condition simply described?

## Instructor notes

**If a student says "this isn't really a social problem, it's just a personal problem."** Do not settle the dispute; put it on the board as a testable hypothesis and return to it in Module 6. The Mills distinction is doing its work.

**If the room wants to treat the title difference as trivia.** Slow it down: the two titles are the cleanest single piece of evidence in the course for constructionism, because the *condition* (a family, a college) is the same and only the *claim* changed.

# Session 12 — Media, Representation, and the Sitcom as Text

**Big question.** Who controls the image, and what does it do?

**Where we start.** The show has spent the term teaching us sociology. Now we turn the lens on the show
itself: who makes it, who it depicts, and what an image does to the people it depicts. A sitcom is not a
window onto a community; it is a site where a community's image is fought over.

## Sociological frame

1. **Encoding and decoding** (Stuart Hall): a text is *encoded* with meaning by its makers and *decoded*
   by its audience — and the two readings never perfectly match.
2. **Representation**: the act of depicting a group — and the power question of who depicts whom, for
   whom, and with what consequences.
3. **Framing**: the selection and emphasis that shapes how an image or issue is understood — what is
   included, what is left out, whose angle is "normal."
4. **The Black sitcom**: a specific battleground — a genre that has carried both visibility for Black
   life and the constraints of what a network or a streamer will sell.

## Discussion questions

1. Who is "the audience" for this show — and how do you know?
2. What does it mean to be *seen* by a text but not *controlled* by it?
3. Name something the framing leaves out, and what changes when you add it back.
4. How should the show's origins (a spinoff whose creator was later convicted) shape how we read it?
5. Who controls the image of a community — the community, the creators, or the market?

## Instructor notes

**If the room wants to dismiss the show as "Hollywood."** That is the starting point, not the end: the
show is a commercial product, and *because* of that it is a perfect object for studying how images are
produced and sold. The commodity nature is data, not disqualification.

**If the Cosby question returns.** This is the module for it. Teach it: the gap between an image and the
institution that produced it is itself a sociological finding. Do not resolve it into comfort.

**If a student asks whether representation "matters."** Hold both sides: representation is not a
substitute for policy, and it is also not nothing — images shape who is imaginable as what. The evidence
for each is different; keep them apart.

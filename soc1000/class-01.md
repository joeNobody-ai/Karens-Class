# Session 1 — The Sociological Imagination

**Big question.** What does it mean to see your own life as history?

**Where we start.** With two screens showing the same story 39 years apart. Sociology is not a list of
facts about society; it is a way of looking — the habit of connecting a private trouble to a public
issue. We start with the show because it makes that connection visible in ninety seconds.

## Sociological frame

1. **The sociological imagination** (C. Wright Mills, 1959): the capacity to see "personal troubles" as
   "public issues". A first-year student who cannot afford to stay is a private problem — until you ask
   how many first-years cannot afford to stay, and why.
2. **Structure and agency**: the course's standing tension. Structure is what we are born into (an
   institution, a family reputation, an economy); agency is what we choose inside it. The show is built
   on this tension.
3. **Biography and history**: Mills's two halves. Every biography — Deborah's, yours — unfolds inside a
   history the person did not choose.
4. **The social versus the personal**: the difference this course will keep returning to. Something is
   *social* when it would be true even if a different person were standing in the same place.

## Discussion questions

1. What is a college — a setting, a character, or an argument — in each opening?
2. Deborah is the daughter of the original's central couple. What does the revival gain and lose by making its lead an inheritance rather than an arrival?
3. Name one thing in your own life that felt personal but that you could also describe as a public issue.
4. The two shows premiered on the same date 39 years apart. Is that evidence of intent, or scheduling? What would settle it?
5. What would a sociologist need that a television scene cannot give?

## Instructor notes

**If the room says "it's just a sitcom."** Take it seriously and give it teeth: ask what television has
historically done to public knowledge — who learned what about which places from which shows. If the only
fictional historically Black college most Americans can name came from a 1987 sitcom, that is itself a
finding about how knowledge is distributed.

**If a student says "my life is nothing like this."** That is data about portrayal, not a refutation. Ask
what the show would have to show for them to recognise it — and hold the difference between a sample of
one and a claim about a population.

**If someone asks about the show's origins.** The original began as a *Cosby Show* spinoff, and one of its
originators was later convicted of sexual offences. Do not resolve this; name it and note the course will
return to it in Module 12 when it takes up image and representation directly. The 2026 revival was made by
a largely new creative team with several original cast members returning.

**Watch for.** The student who already knows the franchise is an asset; give them the job of correcting
the room *with sources*, using the reference site.

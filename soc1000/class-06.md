# Session 6 — Deviance, Stigma, and Social Control

**Big question.** Who gets labelled deviant, and what does the label do?

**Where we start.** Finals week, and then something worse arrives that the show's own summary does not
name. We pair it with the episode the original made famous: a 1991 prime-time sitcom put an HIV
diagnosis at its centre. Deviance is not a property of an act — it is a label applied by people with the
power to apply it.

## Sociological frame

1. **Deviance**: behaviour that violates a group's norms — but the key move is that deviance is *labelled*,
   not inherent. The same act is deviant or not depending on who does it, where, and who is watching.
2. **Labeling theory**: the label, once applied, sticks and reshapes the person — a "deviant" identity
   becomes a master status that overrides everything else.
3. **Stigma** (Goffman): a mark that spoils identity — an attribute that makes a person "less than" in
   the eyes of others. HIV carried a stigma that became its own second illness.
4. **Social control**: the mechanisms — formal and informal — a society uses to keep behaviour in line,
   from a raised eyebrow to a sanction.

## Discussion questions

1. Is the deviant the person who breaks the rule, or the person who gets labelled? Argue with an example.
2. What did putting an HIV disclosure on prime-time television in 1991 *do* — and what could it not do?
3. Name a label that has stuck to you or someone you know. What did it change about how you were treated?
4. What is the difference between a medical label and a moral label — and who decides which one applies?
5. Why might a show choose *not* to name its own crisis? What does the silence do?

## Instructor notes

**Watch Ep 5 yourself before teaching this.** Its published summary does not name the crisis it depicts.
Run the module on the *documented* material (the 1991 episode's facts, the 2026 episode's structural
position in the season) and be explicit that the 2026 crisis is unnamed in the sources. Do not fill the
gap from memory — the evidence rule exists for exactly this.

**If a student discloses something personal.** Do not make them the example. Thank them, move the room back
to the text, and follow your campus's care procedures. The material is heavy and the module says so up front.

**If someone argues HIV was "a 1990s thing."** The episode is a historical document of stigma, but stigma
is not historical — ask what conditions carry the same weight now. The concept outlives the example.

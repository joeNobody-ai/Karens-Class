# Session 5 — Groups, Organizations, and Networks

**Big question.** What do groups do to the people in them?

**Where we start.** Back at homecoming, but now looking at the *organizations*: the fraternities and
sororities, the step squad, the alumni. A campus is not a crowd; it is a lattice of groups, and each group
reshapes the people who pass through it.

## Sociological frame

1. **Primary group**: small, face-to-face, emotionally close — a friend group, a family. The people you
   are *someone* to.
2. **Secondary group**: larger, goal-oriented, impersonal — a class, a workplace, a chapter. The people
   you are a *role* to.
3. **In-groups and out-groups**: the groups we belong to and the ones we define ourselves against. The
   "we" only exists because there is a "they".
4. **Formal organizations** and **reference groups**: organizations have written rules and a hierarchy; a
   reference group is the group you measure yourself against even if you never join it.

## The 75 minutes

| Min | What |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | **Screen the step line.** Ask: when does a set of individuals become *one* group, and what marks the boundary? |
| 10–24 | **Define the terms.** Primary versus secondary; in-group versus out-group; formal organization versus reference group |
| 24–38 | **The Greeks as a formal organization.** Membership, initiation, hierarchy, and the identity it confers. What joining *does* to a person |
| 38–52 | **In-groups and out-groups.** Who is on the line and who is not; the boundary work; the cost of being outside |
| 52–64 | **Pair drill.** Each pair takes one group in the scene and classifies it: primary or secondary, and for whom it is an in-group or a reference group |
| 64–75 | **Close and assign.** Flag hazing and initiation as a bridge to Module 6 (deviance). Assign homework 5 |

## Discussion questions

1. Is the step squad a primary or a secondary group — or does it depend on who you ask?
2. What does membership in a Greek organization *give* a person, and what does it *cost*?
3. Name an in-group you belong to and the out-group you define it against. What does the boundary do for each side?
4. What is a reference group you measure yourself against without belonging to it?
5. Why do organizations write rules, and what do the unwritten ones enforce that the written ones cannot?

## Instructor notes

**If the room drifts into "fraternities are just popularity."** Pull it back to structure: a formal
organization has membership criteria, a hierarchy, and an initiation — none of which a friendship has.
The sociological question is what the *organization* does, not whether the members are likeable.

**If a student raises hazing.** Do not shut it down and do not moralise yet: hazing is a form of
initiation and a form of deviance, which is exactly Module 6. Say that and park it — the course returns
to it next week with the labelling lens.

**If someone asks why the show keeps returning to the Greeks.** Because they are the campus's most legible
organizations — the place where belonging, hierarchy, and ritual are all visible at once. That is why they
are useful to sociology, whatever one thinks of them.

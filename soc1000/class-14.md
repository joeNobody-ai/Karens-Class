# Session 14 — Reading a Text Sociologically (Synthesis)

**Big question.** Can you hold the whole toolkit against one episode?

**Where we start.** The finale. Every thread of the season — the money crisis, the relationships, the
family, the institution's future — reaches a turning point at once. This week is not a new concept; it is
all of them, applied to a single text, the way a sociologist actually works.

## Sociological frame

1. **The whole toolkit.** This week is the test of Modules 1–13: imagination, culture, socialization,
   groups, deviance, stratification, race, gender, family, education, media, movements — all live in one
   episode.
2. **The gift and reciprocity** (Mauss): a gift is never free; it creates obligation and relationship.
   The title announces the concept.
3. **Institutional reproduction**: how an institution persists across generations — what is handed down,
   and what it costs to hand it down.
4. **A text as a whole**: a season is not fifteen scenes; it is an argument with a shape. The finale is
   where the shape shows itself.

## The 75 minutes

| Min | What |
|---|---|
| 0–12 | **Screen the finale's turning point.** Ask: which *concept from this term* is doing the most work in this scene? |
| 12–26 | **Map the episode.** Build a shared board: each major thread, and which concept lights it up. No thread without a concept, no concept without a scene |
| 26–40 | **The gift.** Mauss's reciprocity; the title's ancestor; what is given, to whom, and what obligation it creates |
| 40–54 | **Institutional reproduction.** What the finale argues about the college's future — and how that is a claim about institutions generally |
| 54–66 | **Pair drill.** Each pair writes a one-paragraph sociological reading of one scene using three concepts and one piece of documented evidence |
| 66–75 | **Close and assign.** The capstone. Assign homework 14 |

## Discussion questions

1. Which concept in the course best explains the finale — and which one fails to?
2. What is being *given* in the finale, and what does it obligate?
3. What does the finale argue about the future of the institution — and how do you know?
4. How is a season different from the sum of its episodes?
5. What can a single episode never show, no matter how well you read it?

## Instructor notes

**If the room wants to "rank" the concepts.** Refuse the ranking and ask for *joint work*: a good reading
uses several concepts at once, and the skill is in the combination, not the favourite.

**If a student struggles to start.** Offer the lowest bar: name one thing in the scene, then ask "which
concept explains that?" The toolkit is on the board; the job is matching, not invention.

**If someone says the finale "just ties things up."** The way it ties them — what it resolves, what it
leaves open, what it argues survives — is a sociological argument. Reading the *shape* of the ending is
exactly the skill.

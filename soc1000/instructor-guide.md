# Instructor guide

Everything here is for you, not for students. It assumes you are teaching a large, first-year section
with no prerequisites and no assumed knowledge of the show. It focuses on the three things specific to
*this* course: the show, the evidence rules, and the conversations that go sideways in an intro room.

---

## 1. Why this course works (and what it is not)

It works because one shared, watchable case lets a first-year room talk about concepts instead of
arguing about which example to use — and because students already know how to watch television. It is
**not** a media-appreciation class and **not** a representative portrait of Black life. It is an
introduction to sociology that uses two deliberate, commercial, partial texts as its running example,
with the limits of those texts as an explicit topic (Module 12).

**The course's one-sentence claim**, which you should say in Module 1 and again in Module 15: *the
sociological imagination is learned by seeing one case clearly — and a television campus, watched with
the toolkit in hand, is as good a case as any.* This is the intro version of the upper-division course's
stronger claim (SOC 4150, which argues Black experience is where general theory gets tested). SOC 1000
teaches the toolkit; SOC 4150 tests the theory. Keep that difference clear so this course does not drift
into pretending to be the other.

## 2. Before the term starts

1. **Check availability.** The 2026 season (10 episodes) streams on Netflix. The 1987–1993 original
   varies by region and service — **verify before you set the schedule**, and tell students where to
   find it. Every module names its essential scene so you can screen it in class; students who cannot
   access the full series lose nothing essential.
2. **Watch Ep 5 yourself before Module 6.** Its published summary does not name the crisis it depicts.
   You cannot run that module blind, and the materials deliberately do not tell you what it is.
3. **Verify the numbers.** Any figure in these materials is a "check before you teach" item. Homework 7
   makes students pull their own, so keep a current one on hand for the discussion.
4. **Open the reference site** (https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/) and read two or
   three callback cards. If you understand its three evidence tiers, you understand this course's standard.
5. **Decide about the exam keys.** They live in `soc1000/exams/*-key.md` and, if built, in the
   `instructor/` pages of the site. If the repository is public, note that trade-off explicitly.

## 3. The evidence rule (teach it in Module 2, enforce it all term)

| Tier | Meaning | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Documented** | A title, air date, credit, or published summary | Can be looked up; the reference site cites its source |
| **Interpretation** | A reading of what a scene argues | Labelled as a reading; you can disagree and still be right |
| **Hypothesis** | A claim offered for the room to test | Stated as a guess, and it can be falsified |

When a student says "the show is saying X", the standard reply is: *is that documented, or is that you?*
Both are welcome; only confusion between them is penalised. This is also the fastest way to handle a
confident wrong claim: "Let's sort that — documented or interpretation?"

## 4. The hard conversations

**Norms to set in Module 1 and enforce every week.**

0. **The vocabulary protocol.** Two rules, stated on day one and never relaxed: the instructor does not
   perform the vernacular, and no student is asked to define, translate, pronounce or perform a term.
   Definitions come from the cited sources (see `lingo.md`), the "handle with care" terms are numbered on
   slides and discussed by number, and nobody in the room is ever the consultant on their own language.
   If you break rule one — easy to do by accident — name it out loud and correct it; the room learns more
   from the correction than from never seeing it.

1. **No spokesperson rule.** Nobody speaks for a group. You will be asked, at least once, to justify your
   position without being asked to represent anyone.
2. **Text first.** Claims must come from the episodes, the readings, or the data — not from general
   impressions of "how people are".
3. **The strongest version.** When disagreeing, state the strongest version of the view you are
   disagreeing with before answering it.
4. **Silence is fine.** An intro room is full of people learning to think in public; a pause is not a
   failure to fill.

**The conversations that will happen, and how to hold them:**

- **"It's just a sitcom."** Concede it is entertainment and hold the point: entertainment is exactly
  where most people meet most of their ideas about groups they are not part of. That is data, not taste.
- **The Cosby problem.** The original began as a spinoff whose creator was later convicted of sexual
  offences. Teach it, do not resolve it: the gap between an image and the institution that produced it is
  a sociological finding, and it is Module 12's subject.
- **A student with personal experience of the material** (a label, a health crisis, a family situation).
  Thank them, move the room back to the text, and do not make them the example. Follow your campus's care
  procedures. The modules flag the heavy ones in advance.
- **"Race isn't real."** "Socially constructed" and "real in its consequences" are the same sentence, not
  opposites. The category organizes housing, policing and life chances even though its biology is
  invented. The question is how to *see* it, not whether to *stop using* it.

## 5. Running a big room on screened scenes

- Screen the named scene (not whole episodes) — each module's "Before class" table says exactly what to
  show. Keep it short; the discussion is the course.
- Caption everything. Announce content notes before the heavy modules (6, 8, 9, 10).
- For the evidence drill, have the reference site up on the projector. The cards are the shared object.
- The pair drills are the engine of a large section: they make everyone speak, not just the confident few.

## 6. Adaptation notes

- **MWF schedule:** each module splits into two 50-minute meetings (frame + screen on day one; drill +
  discussion on day two).
- **No screening rights:** the modules are written so the *documented summary* of a scene can stand in for
  the clip. Read the "Why this one" column aloud instead.
- **The capstone** (homework 15) doubles as an easy final-week grade buffer and a genuinely useful
  assessment of whether the transfer happened.

# Session 10 — Family and Intimate Relationships

**Big question.** Is romance personal, or structural — or both?

**Where we start.** "Cuffing season" arrives and every relationship on campus gets messier at once. The
title is a one-word edit of a 1987 episode — the *rules* underneath changed completely. Who we love, and
how, is not just a matter of the heart; it is shaped by structure.

## Sociological frame

1. **Family forms**: family is not one thing — nuclear, extended, chosen, and everything between. What
   counts as family is a social question, not a biological one.
2. **Kinship**: the web of relations by blood, marriage, and choice — and the obligations that come with
   them. The aunties are kin whether or not they are relatives.
3. **Mate selection and the marriage market**: who pairs with whom is patterned — by class, race,
   education, and geography. The "market" is a structure wearing the mask of a feeling.
4. **Othermothering** (from Module 4, returning): family as something a community does, not just something
   it has.

## Discussion questions

1. What is chosen in a relationship, and what is chosen *for* you before you even meet?
2. Is the aunties' bond a family? Argue from the definition, not the feeling.
3. What does the title edit — "Those Who Can...Cuff" — tell you about what changed between 1988 and 2026?
4. How does a parent's marriage shape a child's romance, without anyone saying a word about it?
5. Why is "the marriage market" a structure rather than just a metaphor?

## Instructor notes

**If the room thinks love is purely personal.** Concede the feeling and hold the pattern: individual
people fall in love individually, but *who is available to whom* — by class, race, geography, education —
is patterned before any two of them meet. The personal and the structural are both true, which is the
course's whole point.

**If a student raises the family "culture" debate (the Moynihan question).** Flag it: that is the loaded
and live argument about whether Black family outcomes are explained by culture or by structure, and it is
Module 11's material. Do not resolve it here.

**If someone asks whether "cuffing season" is real.** It is documented as the episode's own premise (the
summary says "cuffing season arrives"), and the word itself is in the language reference. The sociological
question is not whether it is real but what it *does* — it names a seasonal structure for romance.

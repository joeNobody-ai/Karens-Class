# SOC 1000 — Introduction to Sociology: Reading the Social World through *A Different World*

**First-year course · 15 modules · 75 minutes · no prerequisites**
*(Renumber to fit your catalogue. Written to be taught by a sociologist who has never used a TV show as a text before.)*

## 1. Catalogue description

Sociology is the study of how the social world gets made — and how it makes us. This course teaches
that discipline the way it is best learned: with one concrete case held up to the light all term. The
case is *A Different World* — the 1987–1993 NBC sitcom set at a fictional historically Black college,
and its 2026 Netflix revival, which picks the story up 39 years later with the original's central
couple now the parents of the new lead. Each week introduces one core idea — the sociological
imagination, culture, socialization, groups, deviance, stratification, race, gender, family, education,
media, social movements — and tests it against a scene from the show. The point is not to study
television. The point is to learn to see structure, agency, and institutions at work in ordinary life,
using a shared example everyone in the room can watch together. No background in sociology or knowledge
of the show is assumed. You will finish able to apply the discipline's core toolkit to the world around
you — and to a screen in front of you.

By the end of the course, students can:

## 3. How the course works

**One case, fifteen concepts.** *A Different World* supplies two eras of stories about one institution —
a campus, a set of relationships, and an argument about what a Black college is. We use it the way a
chemistry course uses a lab: as a stable object everyone can look at together, so that when we disagree
about a concept we are disagreeing about the *concept*, not about which example we happen to remember.

**You screen scenes, not seasons.** The 2026 season (10 episodes) streams on Netflix. The 1987–1993
original is longer (144 episodes) and its availability varies by region and service — **verify before
you set the schedule**, and tell students where to find it. Every module names the scene that matters,
so you can screen it in class and students who cannot access the full series lose nothing essential.

**The evidence rule — from the first week.** The course keeps three things apart everywhere: what is
**documented** (a title, a date, a credit, a published summary), what is an **interpretation** (a
reading of what a scene argues), and what is a **hypothesis** (a claim on the table for testing).
Students are graded on keeping them apart. This is a transferable skill, and Module 2 teaches it
explicitly.

**The reference site.** All modules point at a documented reference site that pairs the revival's
callbacks with the original episodes and labels its evidence:
https://joenobody-ai.github.io/different-world-notes/v2/ — students use it *and* check it, because
every entry there carries a tier and a source.

**Meeting cadence.** Each module is one 75-minute block, or two 50-minute meetings on a Monday-Wednesday-
Friday schedule. **Folded topics** (religion, government and the economy, health and medicine) are not
standalone modules but are named where they do work: religion inside Culture and Institutions;
government and the economy inside Stratification, Education, and Social Movements; health inside
Deviance (stigma and medicalization). This keeps the term to fifteen weeks while covering a standard
introductory table of contents.

## 6. Policies

**Content note.** This course discusses racism, racial violence, HIV and sexual health, and relationship
conflict. You will be told in advance what each module involves, and no one is required to disclose
personal experience to participate. If a module's material lands hard, the instructor will say so and
make room; campus counselling is listed in the instructor guide.

**Discussion norms.** Everyone speaks from the text and the evidence; nobody is asked to speak for a
group; disagreement is expected and graded on argument, not on comfort.

**Academic integrity and AI.** Generative AI may be used for transcription, accessibility, and language
polishing **with disclosure**. It may not be used to generate the analysis, the reading of a scene, or
the argument — those are what you are being taught to do. Any submission may be discussed orally; you
should be able to defend every sentence you hand in. Undisclosed AI-generated analysis is a violation.
Most assignments ask for something AI cannot supply: your own claim, tied to a specific scene, checked
against a specific source.

**Accessibility.** All screened material is captioned. Slides, handouts and the course site are
screen-reader compatible; accommodations are honoured without documentation theatre — talk to me.

**Late work.** Homework is worth little individually and matters cumulatively: one week late, half
credit; after two weeks, no credit but a conversation. Exams are due as scheduled except by prior
arrangement.
